## Opening

Hi everyone, I'm He Xingze, R&D at Domob's Platform Division ADX. Born in 2003.

In the fall of 2022 I was a sophomore, just starting to learn programming, and ChatGPT was released. I hadn't even figured out how to write Python, and AI could already write it for me. In 2023, GPT-4 came out, and I started using it for projects. In 2024, GPT-4o launched while I was job hunting, and I suddenly felt this thing was more than just a tool. In 2025 I graduated, Agents were taking off, and I joined BlueFocus—from day one it was humans and AI working side by side.

So I'm probably the person in this room whose career has involved AI from literally the first day. But today I don't want to talk about what AI can do. I want to start with something that has nothing to do with AI.

Let me do a quick calculation: line up the longest-lived people end to end, each one having known the next—the entire span of human civilization, from the invention of writing to today, is just sixty people. Sixty people is a cocktail party. Twenty people take you back to Jesus, six to the Renaissance.

The first time I saw this I was genuinely shaken. Because it made me realize we might still be very early in the overall story. The things everyone's anxious about today—the economy, the industry, whether AI will replace me—on the scale of sixty people, they might just be content from the first few pages.

## Signals Are Not Truth

In 1970, the Soviets drilled a hole into the ground on the Kola Peninsula. Before drilling, scientists used seismic wave data to infer what the subsurface structure should look like—which layer was granite, which was basalt, what the temperature was, how density was distributed. The model was elegant.

Then they actually drilled. At 12 kilometers, the borehole wall temperature hit 380°C, the drill bit couldn't take it, and they stopped. And they discovered that the actual conditions underground were far from what the model predicted—the story inferred from surface signals basically fell apart in the real environment.

There's another interesting angle to this. Going to space is actually relatively easy for humans—you're heading outward, with an open view. But figuring out what's really going on a few kilometers beneath your feet turns out to be much harder. Temperature, pressure, the environment—everything is extreme, and you need completely different equipment and a completely different mental readiness.

I think this is a lot like where we are right now with AI. AI can process all the data you give it, and what it produces looks pretty convincing. But it's never been in the real environment—a person born blind can learn every word about color—"red is passionate," "blue is calm"—but they can't truly understand what color is. AI output is the same: it's a signal, not truth. There's a gap in between, and someone has to walk over and look for themselves.

Earlier this year, I took that walk.

## Three and a Half Weeks, Negative Profit, and a Choice

We run an ad exchange—we make money on the spread between buying and selling. At the start of the year, the team estimated that if we built a pricing conversion system, a single channel could earn an extra 100,000 RMB per day. The logic was sound; top platforms in the industry were all doing something similar.

I spent three and a half weeks, working alone, taking this thing from idea to production. In the past, this would have taken at least three months. Everyone thought this was a textbook case of AI empowerment. I was pretty proud of myself too.

Then on the first day after launch, it lost money. My boss and several business leads sat around a table, staring at a screen showing my negative profit. The only thought in my head was: I'm done.

Later we traced it to a strategy configuration issue, fixed it, and the system started running. But once it was running, we discovered this direction was only adding a few thousand RMB per day. It wasn't that the system was bad—it was that for the current business structure, the upside of this path was just that small.

The system wasn't shut down; it's still running today. But what if it had taken three months instead of three and a half weeks? With three months of investment sunk in, who's going to say "forget it, let's change direction"? Most likely you'd keep optimizing, push it from a few thousand to ten thousand, and burn half a year on it.

Because it only took three and a half weeks, by week four I had already shifted my focus to a different direction—no longer trying to calculate a single "right price," but letting the system adapt on its own in every real-time auction. That solution is live now, serving 11 clients, with a Q4 target of 1 million RMB in incremental gross profit.

Magee once used a metaphor: he said the human condition is like holding an incomplete set of puzzle pieces in your hand, always wanting to assemble a complete picture. But what you should actually do is accept that the puzzle has gaps, and just put the pieces you have in the right places.

The first system was one puzzle piece—it wasn't the whole picture. I'm not sure the pivot was the "right" choice. But I know one thing: if I had insisted that the first piece was the entire picture, I never would have gone looking for the second one.

## An Uncomfortable Question

We're very good at telling stories about distant horizons—AI empowerment, globalization, efficiency gains. But what about the ground beneath our feet? What are clients actually thinking? The proposals we put out—are they really being used inside the client's organization? Faster, more—is that real prosperity, or false prosperity?

My own experience was a small-scale version of false prosperity—built a system in three and a half weeks, looked great on paper, but the moment you validated it against actual business, it was just okay. Now I wonder: could the same thing be happening at the company level? We've deployed a lot of AI tools, run a lot of Agents—but coming back to the most basic question: are our clients more satisfied this year than last year?

I don't have the standing to give this question an answer. But I think it's worth asking. Just like the Kola Borehole—no matter how elegant the conclusions derived from surface data are, someone still has to drill down and take a look.

## Choosing What to Believe

Back to where I started. We're probably still very early in the story. The AI thing has only just begun, and nobody knows what comes next.

Magee calls his own attitude "active agnosticism"—accepting that you don't know, but not stopping because of it. He has a line I remember clearly: "Ignorance is a reason not to believe, not a reason to believe."

Let me be honest: I've spent the last fifteen or so minutes talking about judgment and choices, but I don't know if I chose right either. The game-theoretic bidding direction might turn out to have a low ceiling too, a few months from now.

But I don't think that matters. Admit you don't know, and keep walking. Not because you're sure there's a road ahead—because standing still guarantees there isn't one.

Moving fast lets me get feedback sooner. As for when to persist and when to pivot, that's a judgment I have to make myself.

If there's one thing worth taking away from today: next time before you use AI to kick off a project, write down one sentence—what result would make me change my mind.

We are all at the beginning of the story.

Thank you.
