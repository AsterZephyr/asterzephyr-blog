*The Time Game* tries to answer a question bigger than how to invest: why do humans form societies, why do they accept certain rules, and why, within the same society, do people have such different feelings about the future?

Zhou Luohua's answer begins with time. Talk of time usually means duration, earliness, sequence. What he cares about, instead, is what a stretch of time contains: whether one might lose a livelihood at any moment, whether in a few years there will be opportunities worth pursuing, whether one can change one's situation through action or can only wait for someone else to decide. In his usage, time encompasses risk and opportunity, as well as a person's judgment of risk and opportunity.

An individual can rarely determine these things alone. Who bears the cost of illness, whether property can be kept, whether a stranger's promise is credible, whether one can start over after failure; all of this reshapes what the future looks like to a person. And so the question of time becomes a question of society: through what cooperative arrangements do people spread survival risks and create opportunities for development? Zhou Luohua calls such arrangements collective games and uses "Above Others" and "Beyond the Horizon" to capture two distinct orientations within them.

The two names are easily misread. "Above Others" might seem like nothing more than a critique of status competition; "Beyond the Horizon" might seem like nothing more than praise for breadth of mind and vision. But the distinction in the book involves much more concrete institutional questions: whom protection is attached to, what success derives from, whether a leading position can be permanently occupied, and how much room an individual has to exit and choose again.

Where Fei Xiaotong, Bourdieu, Hirschman, and Ostrom appear, they are comparisons brought in from outside the book and do not imply that Zhou Luohua wrote in accordance with their theories. All page numbers refer to the printed pages of the 2024 edition published by Shanghai University of Finance and Economics Press; the illustrations are explanatory diagrams, not original illustrations or empirical charts from the book.

## What Does Zhou Luohua Actually Mean by "Time"?

### The Clock Is the Same; the Future People Face Is Not

A day has twenty-four hours, a shared unit of measurement. But take the same upcoming six months: one person can use them to learn a new skill, while another must use them to repay a debt already due. The first has several arrangements to try; the second's income and its uses have been pre-committed. The calendar is the same, but the disposable life is not.

This is exactly the kind of difference Zhou Luohua cares about. On pages 10 through 15, he explains time as a combination of risk and opportunity, and repeatedly uses "near-term" and "far-term" to describe two kinds of situations. Near-term pressure is already closing in; a person has no time to change conditions and can only bear the outcome. The far-term is not yet fully determined; a person can learn, prepare, find partners, or adjust direction. The difference between the two lies in how much action can accomplish.

Near and far here cannot be measured in months alone. An exam tomorrow, if one is well prepared, may not pose a serious threat. A ten-year commitment that absorbs all income from today onward may not make a person feel spacious about the future. Zhou Luohua often writes calendar-distance and psychological hope in the same breath; when reading, it is necessary to separate them. **Deadline, conditions for action, and feeling about the future are three things that influence each other but cannot substitute for one another.**

This decomposition also explains why the same person changes. With stable income and good health, one is willing to try a new life; after taking on caregiving responsibilities and losing income, one may value reliable arrangements more. One should not conclude, just because choices differ before and after, that the person has become timid or lost ideals. The risks they face, the obligations they must honor, and the number of mistakes they are allowed to make have all changed.

### Risk in Time Means Things That Cannot Be Responded to in Time

Risk in the book carries a strong situational flavor. It does not merely refer to the probability of some bad outcome; it also involves whether an individual can absorb the bad outcome once it occurs and whether any remedy is available. Losing a source of income has obviously different consequences for someone who has other income streams and for someone whose entire life depends on that one source.

Discussing risk therefore requires looking at the event, the loss, and the capacity to absorb it simultaneously. The probability of illness may not change, but if one person can get treatment and another cannot afford it, the threat to their lives differs. Unemployment rates may be the same, but one person has savings, transferable skills, and accessible safety nets while another does not, and the room to refuse unreasonable terms differs accordingly. These are further distinctions drawn along the author's line of thinking and cannot be simplified into "a good attitude eliminates risk."

Zhou Luohua sometimes tightly links a person's strength to a decline in the sense of risk. This captures the role of experience and ability: someone who knows how to handle problems may indeed no longer fear certain changes. But subjectively feeling capable of coping does not prove that one is objectively safe. Confidence may come from preparation; it may also come from misjudgment. A serious treatment of his view of time should preserve this gap.

### Opportunity Requires a Path to Walk

Opportunity, too, cannot merely mean that something good happened somewhere in the world. A new profession may emerge, but if a person can neither access training nor qualify to enter the relevant industry, this development is not yet a feasible option for them. When the author says opportunity lies in the distance, he implicitly assumes at least two conditions: a new possibility exists, and the individual can approach it through some pathway.

This makes "opportunity" inherently social. Whether a school allows students to change majors, whether an industry allows new entrants, whether a worker who switches jobs can retain basic protections; all of this determines how open the future actually is. If there are only abstract success stories but no accessible channels, people may hear encouragement while feeling increasingly powerless.

Conversely, opportunity need not appear grand at the outset. Being able to retake an exam, switch to a different work method, or gain the right to reapply; any of these can make the future something other than a mere extension of the old life. For an individual, what matters is that subsequent action can still make a difference. If outcomes are predetermined by fixed identities no matter how hard one tries, a long timeline may only mean a long wait.

### "Creating Time" Is a Conditional Claim

In this context, creating time can work in two directions: reducing threats that must be dealt with immediately, or increasing the actions one can take later. The former keeps a person from being entirely trapped by present difficulties; the latter gives waiting something worth waiting for.

The two often depend on each other. Without basic subsistence secured, distant opportunities are hard to convert into action; with only protection but no new room for activity, life may fall into repetition. It is because the author links these two directions together that he moves from finance to social organization, to how people improve, and to how they form expectations about the future.

But "creating time" is, after all, an interpretive expression. It cannot be directly redeemed for a specific number of added years of life, nor can it be written as a formula where a given reduction in risk equals a given increase in time. Some of the book's financial passages push the analogy quite far without providing a model sufficient to support such conversion. The question can be accepted it raises while refusing to treat a metaphor as a measurement tool.

There is also a distinction that is easy to overlook: receiving an extension and improving one's situation are not the same thing. A postponed deadline may allow someone to complete training or recover health, but it may also just let old obligations pile up further. To judge whether time has added real value, one must look at what happened during the extension and who gained greater freedom of choice. Simply keeping a relationship going does not automatically mean its participants are better off.

## Why Call Society a Game?

### Games Place Action Inside Shared Rules

At the start of the "collective games" chapter, Zhou Luohua recounts a shift in his own thinking: when young, he tended to view price fluctuations as natural phenomena amenable to modeling; later he emphasized investor cultivation; still later he turned his attention to the social activities in which everyone participates together. (Page 69.) This self-account reveals how his vantage point moved: from the object itself, to the actor, to the relationships among actors.

Studying one person's ability and will alone cannot explain many outcomes. The same qualifications may receive different recognition under different systems; the same promise carries different weight when someone enforces it versus when no one does; the same effort may be rewarded completely differently when evaluation rules change. "Game" brings these normally undiscussed conditions into view.

A recognizable social game includes at least participants, available actions, recognized outcomes, and methods for maintaining those recognitions. An exam requires knowing who can take it, how to answer, what counts as passing, and how scores are used. Mutual aid requires knowing who should contribute, when one may ask for help, and what happens when obligations are refused. People need not discuss every clause before joining; they will still learn the rules through repeated participation.

Rules can therefore be written in documents or expressed as tacit understanding. What is formally permitted may be sanctioned by peers; habits never codified may bind more powerfully than formal requirements. Zhou Luohua's interest in customs, face, and moral rhetoric comes precisely from this hidden yet effective layer of rules.

### A Game Is Neither Entertainment Nor a Scam

In everyday language, "game" suggests voluntariness, lightness, and the ability to quit at any time. Social relationships often lack these qualities. Birth assigns a family position; livelihood compels entry into a division of labor; many commitments cannot simply be retracted. Using games to explain society requires preserving the asymmetry of participation: some people can choose to join, while others have almost no alternative.

If all of life is called a game, one still needs to explain what this word says beyond "institution" or "cooperation." Zhou Luohua's distinctive move is to link rules, risk, and human investment. When people join an activity, they do not merely execute a procedure; they come to believe a certain kind of victory is worth pursuing and invest honor, hope, and fear in it. A game can thereby mobilize people and also keep them in place.

Having shared recognition, however, does not mean everyone is deceived. Money, credentials, and promises work because others acknowledge them; once acknowledgment forms, it produces real consequences. Conversely, the fact that an arrangement is social does not mean it can be arbitrarily modified. Skills already developed, infrastructure built, resource dependencies established, and mutual commitments made all impose costs on changing the rules.

The other end the game perspective most easily slides toward is explaining all goodwill as calculation. Helping others may simultaneously involve emotion, duty, habit, and the prospect of future reciprocity. Pointing out a risk-pooling function does not license the declaration that the feelings are fake. A sociological explanation can add a layer of understanding; it need not cancel the participants' original sentiments.

### Why Do People Join a Game, and Why Is It Hard to Leave?

On page 109, Zhou Luohua proposes three mechanisms that sustain collective games: incentives that attract participation, penalties that prevent exit, and monitoring that encourages members to follow the rules. This is a set of questions applicable throughout the book, but it needs to be understood in concrete relationships.

Incentives are not necessarily monetary. Identity, recognition, a sense of belonging, eligibility for aid, and the expectation that things may improve in the future can all serve as reasons to participate. Monitoring is not limited to formal inspection; it may come from peer evaluation, reputation transmission, and everyday interaction. Exit costs may include losing existing credentials, losing relational support, or being unable to take past investments along.

Not all exit costs should be understood as penalties deliberately set by organizers. Learning a profession takes time; leaving means the original skills are temporarily useless. Building a shared facility requires commitment; withdrawing at will may harm others. These costs can have legitimate origins. To judge whether a constraint is problematic, one must ask whether it is proportionate to the shared task, whether it can be negotiated, and whether one party uses it to extract boundless obedience.

### Games Can Also Change What Players Want

People enter a set of rules already carrying desires, but the process of participation also reshapes those desires. If an environment rewards only ranking over a long period, people tend to equate being ahead with success. If refusing extra demands always leads to loss of aid, people learn to treat compliance as reliability. If raising new questions can obtain resources, exploration may become a sustainable habit.

This is not to say that individuals are entirely shaped by their environment. Under the same conditions, people still make different choices, and some are willing to bear costs to oppose existing rules. But explaining recurrent group behavior solely through personality risks overlooking the shared conditions that produce such behavior. An environment that constantly generates the same anxiety, then demands that everyone eliminate it through self-cultivation, often fails to address the root of the problem.

From here, the connection between games and time also becomes clear. Rules tell people which investments are worth sustaining over time and which aspirations are best abandoned early; they change an individual's estimate of the future, which then, through individual action, maintains or changes the rules themselves. Zhou Luohua's discussion of time does not stop at inner feeling; it follows this reciprocal relationship toward social order.

## What Does Sociology Reveal That Games Tend to Hide?

### Relationships Define How Far Obligations Extend

In *From the Soil*, Fei Xiaotong used the concept of the "differential mode of association" (差序格局) to describe a relational network that extends outward from the individual according to closeness, contrasting it with group-based structures that have clearer boundaries. Obligations vary with relational distance and cannot be fully explained by a single set of abstract membership criteria. What is borrowed here is his analytical approach; it is not equated directly with Zhou Luohua's two models. [*From the Soil*: "The Differential Mode of Association" and "Morality Maintained Through Personal Ties"](https://rprc.scau.edu.cn/_upload/article/files/73/cb/8b7a469241529a1630c3878ba891/d2a08f16-7ca3-4e4c-8c48-eb9c12be86da.pdf)

Applied to *The Time Game*, this perspective makes "relationships are useful" a more precise question. Whom can a person turn to in difficulty? Must help be justified by demonstrated closeness? What obligations follow from having received help? These are not the same question. The scope and layers of a relational network determine whom aid reaches and also who is constantly performing the labor of maintaining relationships.

The same request for help may be taken for granted inside the circle and have no entry point outside it. Seen this way, whether protection exists and whether protection is universally accessible require separate judgments. A society can possess very strong mutual aid and still leave people who lack connections in isolation. What Zhou Luohua touches upon when criticizing dependence includes precisely this situation, where eligibility shifts with relationships.

But one should not then write off all rural or communal relationships as oppression. Many such relationships carry caregiving and emergency functions that formal institutions have not yet covered, and members may genuinely cherish one another. What needs discussion is what conditions accompany help and whether those conditions can be renegotiated, not a prior verdict that closeness is necessarily backward and distance necessarily progressive.

### Players Enter the Game Carrying Their Past

In "The Forms of Capital," Bourdieu emphasizes that social opportunity is shaped by historical accumulation. Economic resources, cultural capital, and social connections can perpetuate advantage in different ways; participants do not re-draw lots from the same starting line every round. ["The Forms of Capital"](https://jan.ucc.nau.edu/~sj6/Bourdieu%2C%20The%20Forms%20of%20Capital.pdf)

Using this reminder to examine *The Time Game* means ability cannot be treated as a personal quality that appears out of nowhere. Who has the conditions to receive training, who still has a livelihood after failure, who can access trustworthy information; all of this affects the initiative a person later displays. A willingness to explore may reflect both one's own effort and costs that others have borne on one's behalf.

This does not deny ability, nor does it cancel personal responsibility. It only means that before praising strength, the question of how strength was formed has to be added. Otherwise, first renaming resource differences as personality differences, then using personality differences to prove the rules are just, sends the argument in a circle. Zhou Luohua's aspiration toward "Beyond the Horizon" avoids becoming a reproach to the disadvantaged only when placed alongside the conditions for cultivating capability.

One must also look at which kind of ability the rules recognize. Skilled expression, familiarity with a certain etiquette, possession of a particular credential; these may genuinely aid cooperation, or they may mainly screen for a specific background. The more an evaluation standard is taken as self-evident, the more it is worth asking what task it serves. Games carry sociological weight in part because their scoring systems are not naturally embedded in the world.

### Habit Lets Rules Persist Without Constant Commands

Many forms of order endure because people have already learned to adjust themselves in advance. They know which words are better left unsaid, what achievements are worth displaying, what requests should not be made publicly. Over time, external demands become everyday judgments and may even become a sense of decency or shame that requires no explanation.

Following Zhou Luohua's discussion of customs and monitoring, this process can be understood as the routinization of rules. An organization need not issue commands to each person continually; as long as members know what consequences follow refusal, they may regulate themselves. At the same time, members observe and correct one another, so that rules propagate through lateral relationships.

This is more complex than "someone at the top controls, so everyone obeys." Ordinary members may simultaneously be constrained by a rule and actively uphold it, because their own safety also depends on others continuing to comply. When one person insists a peer not take a risk, there may be both a desire for control and a fear that the risk will ultimately fall on oneself. Understanding these mixed motives explains why merely replacing a leader often does not prevent old relationships from reconstituting themselves.

### Acceptance of Rules Is Itself a Form of Power

Rules require not only enforcement but also the belief that they are worth enforcing. Zhou Luohua frequently asks what role a moral claim plays: why a certain obligation is called loyalty, why a certain distribution is called fairness, why certain risks must be endured. This line of questioning brings legitimacy into the analysis.

Legitimacy here means people's acceptance of an arrangement as proper; it is not the same as legal validity. A rule may be legally established yet lack trust; a practice may never have been written into law yet enjoy deep support from members. Both affect whether a game can continue at low cost, and organizers therefore invest considerable effort in explaining why they should hold decision-making power.

However, explaining that a certain idea helps maintain order is not sufficient to prove it was originally designed for that purpose. A custom may have undergone long evolution and may serve multiple functions simultaneously. The original book sometimes infers ancient origins directly from present-day functions; such inferences should be kept as hypotheses. The strength of a sociological perspective comes from examining relationships closely, and a neat story cannot substitute for historical evidence.

## "Above Others": Why Survival Problems Become Positional Problems

### Starting from Collectively Facing Danger

"Above Others" sounds like a form of ambition, yet the author places its origin in vulnerability. Individual strength is limited, resources are tight, and new opportunities are hard to come by; anyone who encounters a shock alone might not survive it. So people reduce their exposure to danger through mutual dependence. (Pages 133-146)

Under such conditions, joining a collective has practical reasons. Pooling reserves, working together, and providing temporary relief can give a person who otherwise couldn't protect themselves a source of support. Even if mutual aid is imperfect, you can't hold up some ideal guarantee that doesn't yet exist and demand that someone immediately abandon the real assistance available to them. Understanding this starting point helps avoid characterizing the search for protection as some kind of personality defect.

The author then advances a stronger claim: if opportunities are viewed as fixed and the collective cannot or will not expand available resources, members will become increasingly concerned with their relative positions. Someone else seizing an advantageous position first may mean you'll be left in a more dangerous spot. Competition thus shifts from improving shared conditions to avoiding falling behind others.

Real scarcity needs to be distinguished from perceived scarcity. Some slots are genuinely limited; other resources can be increased through improvement. But if people believe opportunities can only be fought over, they may act as though the total is fixed. Conversely, verbally proclaiming that opportunities are unlimited won't magically create more positions. Analyzing a game requires examining both material conditions and people's understanding of those conditions.

### Equally Bearing Risk Is Not the Same as Receiving a Minimum Guarantee

On page 138, the original book specifically distinguishes two things: everyone bearing the same misfortune versus everyone receiving a minimum level of protection. The former focuses on whether people are roughly the same; the latter focuses on whether each person can still get by. This is the step most easily missed when understanding "Above Others."

Suppose a group of people can only obtain very little food. Equal distribution might reduce mutual suspicion, but it won't necessarily bring anyone up to basic survival needs. Conversely, an arrangement that guarantees a minimum standard of living can allow members to earn different incomes above that floor. Relative differences and absolute floors are not the same metric, and reducing differences doesn't necessarily mean improving conditions at the bottom.

Zhou Luohua's critique of "egalitarianism" primarily targets a specific dynamic: members treating the prevention of peers from surpassing them as more important than whether shared conditions have improved. However, this critique cannot be expanded into a rejection of all demands for equality. Demanding equal treatment for equal qualifications, demanding procedures that don't favor particular identities; these have independent justifications and don't imply wanting everyone to become poor together.

The author himself shifts his usage of "fairness" across different passages. Sometimes he uses the word to criticize the satisfaction of suffering misfortune together; later he lists fairness as a legitimate fundamental demand of participants. Therefore, when reading the book you can't extract a single line like "fairness impedes development" and stop there. You need to keep distinguishing: are the people in question demanding procedural consistency, open opportunities, and basic rights, or are they demanding that no one be allowed to surpass them?

### Why People Care Most About Those Closest to Them

In the author's account, people don't necessarily envy all successful individuals equally. They may be indifferent to the wealth of distant figures yet extremely sensitive when someone in a similar position gains even a slight advantage. The reason isn't just narrow perspective; it may also be that they're within the same allocation pool: whoever gets an opportunity first directly changes everyone else's expectations.

This mechanism can be illustrated with very small everyday scenarios. If a class has only a few recommendation slots, students will pay attention to their classmates' grades; an outstanding student appearing in another city usually won't immediately change one's own situation. If evaluation also depends on familiar relationships and opaque judgments, even tiny differences become easier to interpret as favoritism.

This is one explanation for anxiety about relative position, but it doesn't justify concluding that every instance of dissatisfaction stems from envy. Someone who objects to a peer receiving an opportunity may be resisting an unfair process; someone who accepts a larger gap may simply lack any channel for expression. Motives must be judged in conjunction with actual rules; you can't reverse-engineer them from outcomes alone.

The original book includes some anecdotes that state the inner feelings of the people involved with great certainty. As a reader, you can retain the possible mechanisms the author proposes while acknowledging that he hasn't exhausted all explanations. Understanding "Above Others" this way allows it to serve as an analytical tool rather than a label used to dismiss other people's legitimate concerns.

### Why Horizontal Conformity Coexists with Vertical Hierarchy

If everyone demands equality, why does "Above Others" ultimately emerge? The author's answer is that mutual aid among members and the organizer's decision-making power operate on different levels. Ordinary members monitor and constrain each other, while who allocates resources, interprets rules, and certifies contributions may still be concentrated in a few hands. (Pages 147-156)

This produces a structure that is not contradictory: horizontally, pulling ahead of peers is discouraged; vertically, certain people are acknowledged as possessing extra authority. Members simultaneously prevent those around them from standing out while hoping to get closer to those who can provide protection and opportunities. A certain emphasis on equality can actually coexist with submission to hierarchy.

The key question is whether members have the equal right to participate in making the rules. If they can only compete for better treatment within an existing allocation but have no power to discuss who gets to allocate or by what criteria, then competition easily revolves around the organizer. Everyone pursues a safer position, yet collectively they continuously strengthen the center that determines positions.

"Above Others" therefore doesn't just mean wanting to be better than others. It also means that safety and opportunity are distributed according to identity and closeness, and individuals must improve their circumstances by climbing within relationships. Even someone who verbally opposes hierarchy will find it hard not to keep competing for a favorable position if losing it means losing basic protection.

## How "Above Others" Transforms from Mutual Aid into Dependence

### Mutual Aid Has Real Value and Maintenance Costs

Zhou Luohua lists ten mechanisms that sustain "Above Others," spread across pages 147-156. Rather than treating each one as a social law, it's more useful to recombine them by the problems they address. The initial question is how to ensure members receive help in times of difficulty; only later does it become about how such arrangements produce surveillance, restrict exit, and concentrate power.

A mutual-aid relationship that depends on members' continuous contributions must deal with situations where someone only accepts help and refuses to bear obligations. It needs to know who has the capacity to contribute and who is genuinely in difficulty, and it needs to make people believe that after helping others, they won't be forgotten in the future. Being familiar with one another, emphasizing loyalty, maintaining reputations; all of these can reduce such uncertainty.

However, the more help depends on personal commitments, the harder it becomes for members to define the boundaries of their obligations. How a single act of aid should be repaid, whether it implies accepting future arrangements, whether refusing a particular request will be seen as ingratitude; these are often left unspecified. Relationships can be more flexible than contracts, but this same flexibility can also make them endless.

The question, therefore, is not whether personal favors are useful, but how much autonomy a person retains after receiving help. There is no naturally clear line between caring for each other and controlling each other; it requires members to continuously clarify, negotiate, and limit. The fact that aid once genuinely occurred does not automatically legitimize every subsequent demand the aid-giver makes.

Among these, expressions like "presumption of guilt" and "manufacturing poverty" carry strong critical overtones. Zhou Luohua vividly depicts the extremes to which a certain kind of relationship can develop, but he does not use them to complete a systematic cross-societal examination. A more prudent reading treats these items as mechanisms to watch for, not as a verdict that can be applied the moment you see people helping acquaintances.

In particular, surveillance itself cannot all be categorized as oppression. Shared use of resources often requires knowing whether agreements are being upheld. The distinction lies in whether surveillance targets specific behaviors or personal loyalty, whether it applies to those in power, whether results can be appealed, and whether the scope of surveillance exceeds the needs of the shared task. An accountable audit and a demand that members constantly prove their loyalty are not the same arrangement.

### Why Leaving Triggers Moral Pressure

When a capable member leaves, a mutual-aid network may lose a contributor, and part of the burden he bore falls on everyone else. Keeping him therefore serves the network's practical interests. This is an important mechanism in the author's discussion of restricting individual development. (Pages 152-154)

Conflict over departure doesn't necessarily arise from malice. A family may genuinely need a particular member to care for a sick person; a small group may genuinely be unable to do without a certain skill. But the fact that the need genuinely exists doesn't automatically determine who should bear it, nor does it prove that a particular person must indefinitely give up their own life. The question of fairness arises precisely in how burdens are shared.

If aid appears only as personal favors, the organization can easily transform a debatable question of burden-sharing into an irrefutable moral demand: you once received help, so you should stay. Past support gradually becomes a claim on the future, and members find it hard to distinguish whether they are fulfilling a limited responsibility or being asked to continuously repay a debt that can never be settled.

In the author's language of time, a relationship may once have given someone the time to get through a difficult period, but later it may also pre-empt time in which they have not yet made choices. Both functions can occur within the same relationship. Acknowledging the former need not deny the latter; opposing boundless demands need not deny past genuine feeling.

### Why Organizers May Need Members to Remain Insufficiently Secure

A protective arrangement is supposed to reduce the threats members face. But if the organizer's position depends on members' continued need for help, the more independent the members become, the more likely the organizer is to lose influence. This conflict of interest is one of the sharpest judgments in the original book.

When an organizer controls both aid and the interpretation of the outside world, they can simultaneously influence what people can obtain and what people believe they would face if they left. Actual risk and advertised risk blend together, making it difficult for members to independently judge whether the protection is necessary or whether its price is reasonable.

There's no need to presuppose that every organizer is a skilled manipulator. Some may sincerely believe their protection is irreplaceable; others, being responsible only to existing members, may underestimate the value of new options. Personal good intentions and institutional consequences need to be discussed separately. As long as the evaluation system rewards maintaining dependence rather than member growth, the same outcome can recur under different leaders.

The author's language about "manufacturing fear" and "maintaining poverty" should be understood within this incentive mechanism. It cannot be extended into "all difficulties are deliberately created by someone." Disasters, resource shortages, and capacity constraints can all genuinely exist. The analytical focus is: has someone exploited genuine difficulties to expand unnecessary power, and once difficulties ease, can the corresponding restrictions be withdrawn?

### Why Stability in This Pattern Does Not Necessarily Mean Development

"Above Others" can produce highly stable expectations. Members know whom to rely on, how to gain recognition, and which behaviors will be punished. This can reduce some everyday coordination costs and may help a group survive danger. But if stability depends primarily on restricting differences and preventing exit, it may consume energy that could otherwise be used to improve conditions.

Individuals devote their energy to proving loyalty; the organization devotes its energy to judging who might leave; new capabilities and approaches must constantly demonstrate they won't threaten existing relationships. The problem isn't that all coordination is waste, but that activities to maintain position may crowd out activities to solve shared problems. Order continues to function, yet opportunities grow ever scarcer.

Once this happens, scarcity is used in turn to justify the necessity of dependence: precisely because there are no opportunities outside, everyone should stay under existing protection. The author's critique here forms a circular explanation. But to demonstrate that it actually occurs, one needs to observe the reasons opportunities have diminished, the alternative options available to members, and the specific behavior of organizers. A pervasive sense of stagnation alone is not enough to draw conclusions.

## "Beyond the Horizon": Moving Competition from Fixed Positions to New Possibilities

### Why Call It Beyond the Horizon

In the typical "Above Others" scenario, one person's rise is easily interpreted as another's decline. "Beyond the Horizon" attempts to open up a different imagination: new activities can emerge beyond existing positions, improved capabilities can create previously nonexistent opportunities, and human development need not forever revolve around the same seating chart. (Pages 169-183)

"Beyond the Horizon" therefore contains a certain attitude toward the unknown. A person who is already doing well can still discover things they don't understand, fields they haven't entered, and people they can collaborate with. Comparison doesn't disappear, but the scope and content of comparison can change. Being ahead now doesn't mean the future has been exhausted.

This doesn't imply that all resources are unlimited, nor that every exploration will create shared benefits. A particular position, a piece of land, a slice of attention can still be rivalrous. What the author emphasizes is the direction of expanding possibilities through capability, technology, and division of labor; this cannot be used to deny real-world distributional conflicts.

If a new domain is quickly sealed off by a few people, and latecomers can only enter by purchasing expensive credentials or relying on personal connections, then a nominally novel activity may rebuild the old positional competition. Judging "Beyond the Horizon" requires looking at whether new opportunities remain open to others, not just at whether the industry is high-tech or whether participants call themselves innovators.

### Why Capability Changes the Experience of Time

Zhou Luohua places the strong individual at the starting point of this pattern. Strength here is primarily expressed as the ability to tackle problems hands-on, willingness to bear the consequences of action, and maintaining curiosity about the unknown, rather than endlessly waiting for someone else to provide complete arrangements. (Pages 184-189)

Capability can change what a given risk means. Faced with the same change in working methods, some people can only worry about being replaced, while others also know how to learn and adapt. This doesn't guarantee the latter will succeed, but the latter has actions available to take, so the future is no longer merely waiting for a verdict. The relationship between time and capability in the book can be understood at this level.

However, initiative requires real authority. If a person bears consequences but cannot choose methods, if they are asked to take responsibility but cannot access necessary information, then hesitation doesn't necessarily indicate a lack of accountability. An organization can verbally encourage independence while punishing unauthorized action through actual rules. This contradiction cannot be resolved by criticizing individuals for "waiting, relying, and asking."

Capability also needs to be exercised and accumulated. Someone who has long had no opportunity to make decisions can hardly learn independent judgment overnight; someone only allowed to execute can't easily develop an understanding of systemic problems. Strength, therefore, can be both a precondition for participating in a certain game and a result the game ought to cultivate. If the precondition is overemphasized, it excludes precisely those who need opportunities the most.

### Why Independent Individuals Still Need Cooperation

The author uses the murmuration of starlings to express an imagined form of cooperation without a fixed center, where individuals continuously adjust their positions. (Pages 173-184) This metaphor aims to show that participants can coordinate actions through mutual responsiveness without surrendering all decisions to a permanent center.

A flock of birds obviously cannot prove that society can necessarily operate this way. Human action involves commitments, property, division of labor, disputes, and rights, far more complex than flocking. The metaphor is suitable for raising a question: can people maintain independence while jointly addressing difficulties that exceed individual capacity? It doesn't solve specific institutional design.

Independence also doesn't mean not needing others. A more operational meaning is that a person need not stake their entire life on a single aid-provider, need not accept comprehensive control over all other areas of life in order to preserve one collaboration. They can simultaneously hold different roles, cooperate according to corresponding rules, and not submit to the same hierarchy of closeness in every matter.

Cooperation among strangers is important here. If every collaboration first requires proof of a deep relationship, new attempts are constrained by existing networks. Rules can provide sufficiently credible expectations so that people can undertake limited, concrete cooperation with those they didn't previously know. But trust among strangers also needs to be built; it's not enough for everyone to simply declare that they follow the rules.

### Exploration Requires Allowing Temporarily Poor Results

The author lists curiosity, optimism, and individual heroism as conditions for "Beyond the Horizon." These words easily turn into motivational rhetoric, but the practical question is quite specific: if an activity must prove from the outset that it will ultimately succeed, how much room for exploration remains?

Some learning involves detours; some methods can only be evaluated after being tried. If every short-term failure results in permanent loss of standing, participants will prioritize actions that are easy to explain and easy to deflect blame for. Even if innovation is publicly demanded, actual behavior may remain deeply conservative. How failure is handled influences choices far more than praise of courage does.

Allowing trial and error also needs boundaries. Explorers cannot pass all losses on to uninformed parties; they cannot invoke possible future success to exempt themselves from present responsibility. Acceptable trial and error should ensure that those bearing the risk are informed, that losses are proportionate to the task, and that learning and correction after failure are preserved as much as possible. Otherwise, the risk-taking of a few may simply come at the cost of other people's lives.

## The Institutional Conditions for "Beyond the Horizon" Matter More Than Courage

### The Author Lists Ten Points: You Can't Read Only the First Five

If you extract only "strength, optimism, heroism, curiosity, and independence," *The Time Game* easily becomes an article urging people to be proactive and enterprising. But the points that immediately follow (property rights, basic living guarantees, high taxation and antitrust, protection of the vulnerable, and freedom) change the nature of this set of propositions. (Pages 184-199)

They show that the author understands individual initiative alone cannot sustain an open society. People need to believe that the fruits of their efforts won't be arbitrarily taken away, that failure won't destroy their entire livelihood, and that those in the lead can't permanently seal off the pathways. Without these conditions, encouraging optimism may amount to nothing more than asking participants to ignore real risks.

The institutional questions in the table are an organization of the author's propositions, not ten individually verified causal laws. Especially when it comes to taxation, social security, and regulation, policy effects also depend on specific design. The book provides a direction but has not yet produced an institutional blueprint ready for direct implementation.

### Why Basic Guarantees May Support Freedom

Guarantees in "Beyond the Horizon" serve the function of freeing people from having to devote all their attention to immediate survival. On pages 193-194, the author explains that encouraging people to explore the future requires reducing their real-life anxieties; on pages 348-349, he further connects healthcare guarantees to individual independence.

The logic here is not the same as "provide a comfortable enough life that people stop acting." Guarantees first prevent certain catastrophic consequences from falling entirely on one person, and then let that person make choices based on interests and abilities. They cannot guarantee that someone will explore, but they can lower the constraints that would otherwise make exploration impossible.

How guarantees are provided also affects their results. If protection is available only to those who remain within a specific relationship, and individuals lose eligibility the moment they change their way of life, then guarantees may deepen dependence. If eligibility is relatively stable and not dispensed at the whim of someone in power, people may have more room when negotiating and choosing. The book does not offer a complete institutional design; the portability of guarantees extends the author's line of thinking.

Moreover, guarantees are not costless resources. Who contributes, which risks are covered, how to prevent the exclusion of those who need help most; all require discussion. Treating guarantees as a condition for freedom does not mean that any measure labeled "welfare" will produce the same effect, nor that long-term fiscal capacity can be ignored.

### Protecting Results: Why Position Ossification Must Also Be Prevented

When the author discusses property rights, he emphasizes protecting the pathway through which people obtain property through effort, and opposes interpreting this protection as meaning that wealthy status can be permanently fixed. (Pages 192-195) This expresses a normative claim: success should be rewarded, and latecomers should still have the possibility of entry.

It should be noted that his distinction between property rights and property uses his own special terminology, which cannot be directly taken as a legal definition. Standard property-rights arrangements involve rights of possession, use, income, and transfer over existing property, far broader than "the opportunity to acquire property." Understanding his meaning makes it possible to discuss the relationship between protecting results and maintaining open opportunity, without adopting his conceptual boundaries wholesale.

This relationship contains real tension. If the results of effort can be stripped away at any time, individuals may be unwilling to make long-term investments; if one result can expand indefinitely into the power to exclude latecomers, open competition may lose its conditions. Institutions need to distinguish results that deserve protection from unnecessary closure created in the name of results.

"Preventing ossification" also cannot become a justification for arbitrarily punishing those in the lead. Being ahead may come from ability and sustained contribution, or from controlling the entrance; the two should be specifically distinguished. If the response to seeing someone succeed is simply to demand they be pulled back, that falls precisely into the relative-position anxiety the author himself criticizes.

### What Problems Do Taxation and Antitrust Solve in the Author's Framework?

Zhou Luohua places high taxation and antitrust together, primarily to prevent excessive concentration of opportunity and to keep those in the lead from permanently occupying the most advantageous positions. His concern is that once latecomers feel all paths have been sealed, they will lose positive expectations about the future, and the participatory motivation on which "Beyond the Horizon" depends will weaken along with it. (Pages 194-195)

This is a functional explanation, insufficient to conclude that all high tax rates promote mobility or that a particular tax will definitely not harm new investment. Identifying and governing monopoly similarly requires distinguishing advantages formed through legitimate competition from behavior that obstructs others' entry. The book's generalizations about different countries' tax systems and wealth inheritance are rather broad and cannot be directly written up as universal facts.

What is more interesting is the internal dilemma the author faces: on one hand, he hopes individuals can cooperate spontaneously through rules, reducing dependence on powerful organizers; on the other hand, guarantees, taxation, and antitrust all require public institutions with enforcement capacity. If institutions are too weak, promises cannot be honored; if institutions are unconstrained, enforcement power may become dominion.

Therefore, "Beyond the Horizon" does not merely require participants to improve their qualities; it also requires that the responsibilities, procedures, and accountability of public power be properly arranged. Trust cannot fully substitute for enforcement, and enforcement cannot expand without limit. The book raises this dilemma but does not fully develop it; readers need not pretend on the author's behalf that the contradiction has been resolved.

### Protecting the Vulnerable, So That Exploration Doesn't Become the Exclusive Privilege of the Strong

If only those who already possess resources can afford failure, the notion that everyone is free to explore becomes hollow. By placing protection of the vulnerable within "Beyond the Horizon," the author signals that this model must account for those who are not yet strong, who have temporarily lost capability, or who cannot compete at the same pace.

The goal of protection also cannot be measured solely by whether someone might eventually become strong. Children, the ill, caregivers, and the disabled should not lose the right to be taken seriously simply because they temporarily or permanently cannot create more opportunities. The book often uses development to justify the value of guarantees; a point from outside the book should be added: people's basic livelihood and dignity need not wait until an economic return has been demonstrated before they are established.

Seen this way, the openness of the Beyond the Horizon model can be tested in two directions. Looking upward: does it permit new capabilities and activities to emerge? Looking downward: does it still allow those in disadvantaged circumstances to retain their livelihood and their right to participate? Focusing only on the first direction makes it easy to mistake the vast space enjoyed by a few successful individuals for everyone's freedom.

## The Boundary Between Two Modes Cannot Be Drawn Between Groups of People

### Comparing Arrangements, Not Ranking People

When Zhou Luohua discusses the two modes, he frequently uses words like "the strong," "the weak," "freedom," and "security." These words are expressive but can easily turn institutional analysis into character judgment. If "Above Others" is understood as a game for lesser people and "Beyond the Horizon" as a life for superior people, what you've done is produced yet another hierarchy.

A person might be willing to explore in learning but need stability in caregiving arrangements; might possess independent judgment in one collaboration but have to rely on others in another matter. A single organization may likewise have both open discussion and strict division of labor. Real-world units are not pure types, and individuals even less so have lifelong fixed game attributes.

Page 158 of the book already cautions that hierarchy and freedom cannot simply be assigned to two entirely separate worlds. Pages 220-223 further compare the two modes to different days of the week, with real-life circumstances falling mostly in between. The author's subsequent qualifications should retroactively constrain his earlier forceful language.

Therefore, the table below compares two orientations that the author has highlighted. In actual observation, one needs to examine item by item which end a given arrangement tends toward, and whether it changes over time, not stamp an entire society with a single label.

### Two Modes of Risk Pooling Cannot Be Collapsed into One Notion of "Averaging"

Zhou Luohua attempts to distinguish the two modes by how risk is distributed between people and across time. In his description of "Above Others," he emphasizes collective burden-sharing among different members at the same point in time; in his description of "Beyond the Horizon," he emphasizes how a single individual, through sustained participation and adaptation, avoids bearing excessive risk at any single moment. (Pages 135-138, 183-184, 217-218)

This distinction is illuminating, but it also exposes places where the book's terminology is insufficiently rigorous. Sharing among people involves who bears whose losses; distributing actions over a longer period involves when risk is borne. These two dimensions can be combined and are not inherently mutually exclusive. A single collective guarantee can both distribute costs among members and help one person get through a difficult period.

Lengthening the time horizon does not itself guarantee risk reduction. If risks continuously accumulate, or if participants keep increasing their commitments, a longer horizon may actually expose more. What the author wants to show is that people can adjust their actions, improve their capabilities, and seek opportunities over a longer period of participation; these conditions need to be spelled out, not left as a bare conclusion that "the long term is safer."

Similarly, mutual aid among people can cushion the shocks an individual encounters, but it cannot necessarily handle situations where all members are simultaneously harmed. If everyone depends on the same income source and faces the same disaster, risk does not disappear just because the number of people increases. Whether mutual aid is effective depends on the correlation among members' risks and whether there are sources of support outside the collective. The book does not develop this layer.

### Relationships and Capability Need Not Be Mutually Exclusive

The author uses options as a metaphor: a relationship that offers protection resembles a fallback for unfavorable circumstances; capability helps a person act when favorable opportunities arise. (Pages 159-162, 200-204) This can be used to distinguish two functions, but one should not literally treat kinship, friendship, or skills as financial contracts with explicit prices and expiration dates.

Relationships can help people learn, and capability can give people more room to care for others. A person's independence often comes precisely from certain relationships that once supported them. The question is not whether to have relationships, but whether a relationship demands total obedience, and whether capability can be put to use across different collaborations.

If a particular skill is recognized only within a single organization, the person possessing it may still be highly dependent on that organization. If a person can obtain qualifications through open standards and collaborate with different partners, their capability is more likely to serve as a condition for their own choices. Therefore, the narrative of shifting from relationships to capability also needs to connect with institutional questions about credential recognition, information, and access to collaboration.

Summarizing the entire book with a moral formula of "relationships bad, capability good" simultaneously erases both the value of mutual aid and the social conditions under which capability is formed. The most explanatory parts of Zhou Luohua's work are precisely where he lets readers see the interdependent arrangements behind individual circumstances.

## The Book's Turning Point: What Do Players Want, and What Do Organizers Want?

### Why Safety and Freedom Are Not Enough

In earlier chapters, Zhou Luohua often uses safety and freedom to describe the two games. But when the reader reaches the chapter on "Individual Players and Game Organizers," he explicitly states that participants' core demands should be understood as fairness and development. (Pages 220-223) This is the passage in the entire book most worth pausing over theoretically.

Fairness means that individuals want the right not to be arbitrarily treated differently, and want collective burden-sharing not to always fall on themselves. Development means they want their current lives to be improvable, and their capabilities and effort to open new possibilities. People typically care about both at the same time; it is only that in different circumstances, the urgency of each shifts.

The reason the author switches to this pair of terms is that safety and freedom can be reinterpreted by organizers. Protection can be rewritten as obedience; exploration can be rewritten as enduring present hardship. The organizer still claims to be providing what participants want, but the actual arrangements may drift further and further from the original purpose.

This turning point means the two modes are no longer just a comparison table; they also contain an analysis of power. One cannot simply listen to an organization declare which game it belongs to; one must also compare promises with actual consequences: what did members receive, what did they bear, and do they have the right to raise objections about the outcomes?

### How the Promise of Protection Becomes a Demand for Obedience

When safety depends on a particular person's approval, members may prioritize maintaining the relationship over pursuing rules. Every instance of receiving help further proves that person's indispensability; every refusal of a demand is interpreted as undermining collective security. What was originally specific protection gradually expands into a comprehensive relationship of dominance.

Such arrangements do not necessarily fail to provide safety at all. They may genuinely resolve problems and may be more reliable than external alternatives. The difficulty is that there is no natural ceiling between contribution and power. Does a person who has resolved past crises thereby earn the right to make final decisions on everything? The fact of having helped cannot, on its own, answer this question.

Participants therefore need to be able to distinguish gratitude from authorization. One can be grateful for a particular act of assistance while simultaneously demanding that allocation criteria be made public; one can acknowledge an organization's importance while opposing unnecessary control. If all criticism is treated as ingratitude, members lose any means of correcting the protection arrangement and can only choose between obedience and leaving entirely.

The author describes this degradation as organizers manufacturing fear and harvesting obedience. In making real-world judgments, one can also look for more specific signs: whether risks are subject to independent verification, whether restrictions have expiration and revocation conditions, whether managers also bear responsibility for failure, and whether members can offer experiences that differ from the official explanation. These questions are more reliable than speculating about whether someone's inner motives are evil.

### How the Promise of Development Becomes an Indefinite Wait

"Beyond the Horizon" has its own mode of distortion. The organizer continuously paints pictures of new frontiers and distant possibilities but does not seriously address immediate livelihood, caregiving, and guarantees. Members are asked to believe in the future, yet when and to whom the costs and benefits of the future will arrive are not given commensurate arrangements.

Long-term investment may inherently require sacrificing current interests; one cannot declare it a scam simply because waiting is involved. The question is whether the waiting has discussable conditions: have the original assumptions changed, can progress be verified, are those bearing the costs informed, and can adjustments be made when there is no improvement over a long period?

If every delay is met only with an even grander vision, participants may be asked to keep paying costs yet never be able to judge whether the promises are approaching fulfillment. The organization's narrative remains full of freedom, but the individual's future is consumed by one obligation after another. This is where "Beyond the Horizon" can regress back into a relationship of dependence.

When Zhou Luohua criticizes this situation, he does not abandon exploration; rather, he asks who bears the burden of exploration. Some people's future projects cannot automatically substitute for other people's lives today. Fairness and development need to appear together precisely because emphasizing only one end allows the other to be indefinitely deferred.

### Beyond Exit, There Must Also Be a Path to Changing the Rules

In *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*, Hirschman distinguishes different responses to organizational deterioration: leaving, or staying and expressing dissatisfaction to push for change. Loyalty affects the relationship between the two, and exit does not always substitute for voice. [*Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*](https://www.rwi.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:ffffffff-a9a1-300b-ffff-ffff9023d189/HirschmanExitVoiceLoyalty.pdf)

Bringing this point back to *The Time Game*, independence cannot be understood merely as having the ability to leave. A person may cherish a shared life and wish to correct the rules rather than abandon the relationship. If leaving is the only option, those with the fewest alternatives find it hardest to defend their interests, and the organization may lose precisely the members most willing to raise serious questions.

Voice must have consequences to be more than an emotional outlet. After a member points out unfairness, whether someone is obligated to respond, whether burden-sharing can be renegotiated, and whether there exists a dispute-resolution process not solely controlled by the party being complained about; all of these affect whether rules can improve. Simply allowing people to speak, while leaving all decisions unchanged, does not necessarily increase participants' actual rights.

From this perspective, evaluating a game benefits from examining entry, staying, and exit simultaneously. Is entry open? Can those who stay participate in revising the rules? Does exit carry responsibilities commensurate with the original commitments? These three dimensions jointly determine the relationship between the individual and the collective; no single one can substitute for the other two.

## How the Two Modes Mix and Change

### New Opportunities Can Loosen Old Dependencies, but Don't Automatically Replace Protections

The author argues that an increase in opportunities and an improvement in capabilities can erode the scarcity conditions on which "Above Others" depends for its maintenance. (pp. 156, 223, 348-349) If a person has multiple paths to income and cooperation, their dependence on any single relationship may decline, and they gain more leverage in negotiation.

But when leaving old arrangements, existing protections may disappear first, before new protections have been established. The caregiving, emergency assistance, and job referrals once provided by family, work units, or acquaintances won't be instantly replaced just because new markets appear. The costs of transition may fall disproportionately on those with the fewest reserves.

Therefore, opening opportunities and ensuring continuity of protections need to be discussed together. You cannot merely demand that people leave dependency and then use the difficulties they face after leaving as proof that they weren't strong enough. If a reform expands choices for a few while imposing most of the transition costs on others, whether it achieves the fairness and development the author pursues still needs to be examined.

Equally, you cannot conclude from the existence of transition costs that old arrangements must be permanently preserved. The key is to separately list which functions are still needed and which restrictions are no longer reasonable. Eliminating unnecessary controls does not require dismantling valuable mutual aid along with them.

### Success Can Also Re-close an Open Game

The degeneration of "Beyond the Horizon" doesn't come only from failure; it can also come from success. Once explorers achieve a lead, they may prefer to protect existing gains and no longer welcome new competitors. The barriers they once opposed may later become tools for maintaining their own position.

This need not be understood as someone suddenly turning bad. A change in position changes interests: latecomers care about entry, while leaders care about whether their existing investments will be protected. Both concerns have understandable elements, yet they can also come into conflict. If the leader simultaneously acquires the power to set admission standards, the conflict cannot be resolved by their self-discipline alone.

The author's emphasis on antitrust and positional mobility can be understood along this line of change. Openness cannot be announced just once at the start of the game; it must also prevent winners from converting temporary advantages into permanent authority over who may participate. Otherwise, the new space created by exploration will quickly be converted back into a fixed hierarchy.

Conversely, organizations with stable divisions of labor need not remain forever in a state of dependency. As long as rights and responsibilities are open to discussion, qualifications can be acquired through learning, and members can take their capabilities into other collaborations, certain hierarchical relationships may serve specific tasks without controlling a person's entire life. Whether hierarchy exists and whether it comprehensively dominates members are things that need to be observed separately.

### Collective Cooperation Has Forms Beyond These Two Labels

In her research on polycentric governance, Ostrom discussed institutional arrangements that cannot be captured by the state-versus-market dichotomy. Resource users can participate in formulating and enforcing rules, and public agencies at different levels, communities, and private actors can share governance responsibilities; outcomes depend on specific conditions and cannot be addressed with a one-size-fits-all solution. [2009 Nobel Prize lecture revised manuscript](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/0a7a52e6-8cf0-42c3-ae20-776095e6fd1d/download)

This comparison suggests that there is no need to categorize all shared responsibility as "Above Others," nor to understand individual independence as mutual isolation. Members can jointly own an arrangement, constrain the power of administrators, and retain channels for discussing the rules. The existence of obligations within cooperation and members being dominated by a single center are still two different things.

When reading *The Time Game*, this adds one more layer of questions: must protections always be bestowed by someone, or do members themselves have the right to participate in decisions? If shared responsibility is formed through negotiation among participants, with contributions visible and accountability possible, it cannot be labeled an obstacle to freedom merely on the basis of the word "collective."

This can also supplement the book's imagination of spontaneous order. Spontaneous formation does not mean zero maintenance work, and public deliberation does not necessarily mean the suppression of individuals. Many forms of cooperation precisely require people to invest time: formulating rules, handling disputes, attending to different needs. If all this work is omitted, individual independence easily becomes an ideal built on other people's invisible labor.

### Different Scenarios Require Different Intensities of Coordination

Emergency rescue and long-term research obviously differ in their demands for unified command versus autonomous exploration. The former may require rapid collective action; the latter often needs to allow different directions to proceed in parallel. You cannot deny all centralized coordination just because exploration is valuable; nor can you extend the same intensity of control from emergencies to all everyday affairs.

Rules need to match tasks, and they need to be adjusted as tasks change. Whether temporarily centralized power can be returned after an emergency ends is one important test; whether an organization allows different approaches to be proposed when existing procedures cannot solve new problems is another.

The two modes Zhou Luohua provides are useful as reminders to observe these changes, but they are not sufficient to replace concrete judgment. They are most useful when they help reveal the connections between protection, opportunity, and power; they are most dangerous when they are used to prove once and for all that one particular form of organization is always correct.

## How to Take This Theory Seriously Without Overclaiming on Its Behalf

### Distinguishing Concepts, Mechanisms, and Evidence

*The Time Game* is not a sociological monograph that systematically tests hypotheses using a unified methodology. On page 205, the author explains that his work is more about finding new uses for familiar words; the afterword also acknowledges that no rigorous system has been established and explicitly states that economics and finance have not been overturned. (pp. 366-367)

Accordingly, the book's arguments can be divided into three layers. The conceptual layer proposes how to look at problems, for example, understanding time as risk and opportunity. The mechanism layer explains how things might happen, for example, when protection is tied to a specific relationship, exit costs may increase. The evidence layer needs to demonstrate the range within which such mechanisms actually occur and why other explanations are insufficient.

The first two layers being illuminating does not mean the third has been completed. A personal experience can help in understanding a certain relationship but cannot prove it is universal; a few historical fragments can spark comparisons but cannot single-handedly explain the rise and fall of civilizations. Serious reading does not require turning every sentence the author writes into a confirmed conclusion, nor does it require erasing all valuable questions just because evidence is insufficient.

Page 345 of the original book is especially worth preserving. Zhou Luohua explicitly acknowledges that his conclusions about certain mechanisms of relational mutual aid lack quantitative statistics and sociological survey support, and he calls his reasoning a hypothesis. If a review article is more confident than the author himself, declaring all relational protection to be empty deception, that would actually be an unfaithful rendering.

### A Probability Example That Needs Supplementation

On page 184, the author compares a 30% predation risk in a single day with a 1% daily risk over thirty consecutive days, calling the latter a "safe journey." This passage is meant to illustrate that risk distribution can change one's situation, but you cannot simply interpret thirty days of 1% daily risk as an overall risk of only 1%.

If one assumes that on each day, conditional on having survived up to that point, there remains a 1% probability of death, then the probability of encountering at least one fatal event over thirty days is:

`1 - 0.99^30 ≈ 26.0%`

Under this explicit assumption, it does differ from a single-day 30% risk, but is far from the gap implied by a direct drop from 30% to 1%. If conditions change due to learning, migration, or environmental shifts, the nature of that change needs to be separately specified; you cannot prove safety simply by spreading risk across more days.

The original book does not calculate the cumulative risk over the full journey, so the "safe journey" judgment lacks adequate justification. This gap marks the boundary between analogy and model. When discussing social risk, "shared burden," "reduced exposure," "delayed occurrence," and "improved coping capacity" may produce different effects and cannot all be collapsed into a single averaged-out diagram. In particular, one should not leap from a bird-flock analogy directly to personal savings or investment strategies.

### Functional Explanation Cannot Substitute for Evidence of Origins

The original book frequently links customs with risk management: a certain practice makes it easier for a group to cooperate, a certain moral requirement makes it harder for members to exit, a certain narrative helps authority gain recognition. These explanations can help readers notice previously overlooked consequences.

But the fact that a practice has a certain function today does not prove it originally emerged for that purpose; that it benefits certain people does not prove that all participants knew about and pursued that outcome. Customs may be repeatedly reshaped by different groups, and the same form may serve different functions at different stages.

Likewise, you cannot assume that an institution is necessarily beneficial to the entire collective merely because it has persisted. It may benefit some people while imposing heavy costs on others; or it may simply be that changing it requires overcoming powerful vested interests. Explaining stability and demonstrating legitimacy are two different tasks.

This is especially important for reading about "Above Others." The author's explanations sometimes move all the way from risk-sharing to hierarchy, religion, and historical institutions, a very large span. These causal chains can be broken into specific questions for step-by-step discussion, without needing to accept that every historical origin has been proven.

### Optimism and Opportunity Cannot Circularly Prove Each Other

The "Beyond the Horizon" narrative frequently links optimism, initiative, capability, and new opportunity together. These factors can indeed reinforce each other, but without independent evidence, they easily form a closed explanation: success proves they are strong, strength explains their optimism, and optimism is then used to explain their success.

To avoid this circularity, conditions and outcomes need to be observed separately. Do participants truly have the resources for training and experimentation? After failure, can they still continue to live? Are new opportunities open to people of different backgrounds? Even when someone displays a positive attitude, these questions cannot be skipped.

By the same logic, you cannot first define conservative behavior as a characteristic of the weak and then use that behavior as evidence of being in a backward game. Bearing non-transferable caregiving responsibilities, or facing consequences where a single failure means losing one's basic livelihood; these can make caution a reasonable choice. If a theory cannot accommodate this interpretation, it ends up blaming individuals for constraints created by their environment.

The author wants to encourage people to develop capabilities, and this direction can be preserved; but a theory that simultaneously discusses society should also ask who has been given the conditions for developing capabilities. Expectations of individuals and demands on institutions need to constrain each other.

### How to Turn the Questions This Book Raises into Testable Questions

If one wants to further investigate "whether protection increases dependency," one can observe the outcomes of the same type of protection under different modes of provision: whether eligibility follows the individual, whether it is interrupted after changing jobs or residences, whether expressing dissent affects receipt, and whether those who leave bear additional losses. Only then can one distinguish protection itself from control over protection.

If one wants to test "whether opening opportunities increases exploration," one cannot merely count the stories of the successful; one must also look at who enters, who exits, what the failures cost, and whether people who previously lacked resources have genuinely gained more choices. An increase in the number of new activities may coincide with an increase in the cost of entry.

If one wants to judge whether an organization is blocking change due to vested interests, one needs to compare its publicly stated mission, its actual rewards and punishments, and its resource allocation, rather than judging solely by whether it uses the word "safety" or "freedom." Even with identical slogans, different modes of execution can produce opposite results.

These questions are not fully answered in the original book, but they can carry its concepts further. The more attractive an explanatory framework is, the more it needs to allow others to point out cases that don't fit. Otherwise, "everything is a game" will quickly become a statement that is always correct yet incapable of helping judge any specific matter.

## Reconnecting the Entire Book

Zhou Luohua started from finance, but what he ultimately cares about is how people live in society. People both encounter dangers beyond their own capacity and aspire to possibilities beyond their current lives. Collective cooperation means the former need not be borne alone and provides conditions for the latter; but once cooperation takes shape, it produces positions, obligations, and decision-making power that do not necessarily continue to serve the original participants.

"Above Others" highlights one possible path: under conditions of scarce opportunity where protection depends on relationships, individuals compete for positions in order to gain safety, and mutual aid may gradually turn into dependency. "Beyond the Horizon" highlights another path: supporting individual development through basic protections and relatively stable rules, enabling new opportunities to keep emerging, while preventing leaders from closing the channels for latecomers. Both modes must confront the power problems they themselves generate.

This is also why the book cannot stop at urging people to choose freedom, or urging people not to engage in status competition. As long as survival conditions still depend on someone else's arbitrary decisions, you cannot demand that people free themselves from dependency through willpower alone; as long as new opportunities belong only to a few, you cannot use the success of those few to prove that everyone has freedom.

By the end, the questions worth continuing to pursue are these: Does an arrangement make people more capable of coping with difficulties and more entitled to choose their future? After receiving support, can people gradually cease to depend on a specific benefactor? After a new activity develops, can latecomers still enter? Can those already within it collectively revise the rules?

None of these questions belongs exclusively to the successful. The future a society offers people should also include those who have temporarily failed, who act slowly, who bear caregiving responsibilities, or who have not yet found their direction. In this sense, the value of time is not only reflected in how far a particular person has traveled, but in whether different people all have the chance to begin changing their own lives.

In the afterword, Zhou Luohua understands his writing as a form of self-reconciliation. He does not leave behind an all-encompassing social formula, but he offers a perspective worth using seriously and worth repeatedly calibrating: when seeing people's aspirations, continue looking at the conditions that sustain those aspirations; when seeing shared rules, continue looking at who gets to decide the rules, who bears the costs, and who still retains a tomorrow they can choose.
