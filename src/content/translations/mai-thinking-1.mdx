> Report: *MAI-Thinking-1: Building a Hill-Climbing Machine*, The Microsoft AI Team, Microsoft AI technical report, 2026. [PDF](https://microsoft.ai/pdf/mai-thinking-1.pdf) · [Announcement](https://microsoft.ai/news/introducing-mai-thinking-1/) (August 12, 2026). Page numbers below refer to this 109-page PDF.

On an earlier model generation, the Microsoft AI (MAI) team ran an experiment with two pre-training data mixes. One gave roughly half the weight to code; the other upweighted STEM data heavily. Apart from the weights of the data sources, every setting was the same. In small-scale experiments and in the intermediate check labeled 5B in the figure legend, the STEM mix did better on a held-out STEM evaluation, as expected. The team then trained a 23B-active-parameter model on each mix for about 20T tokens. The STEM mix stayed ahead for the first part of training, the two curves crossed around the halfway point, and by the end the code mix was ahead on this same STEM evaluation (§2.5.2, PDF pp. 17-18).

Had the decision rested on small models alone, a 20T-token run would have been bet on the mix that ended up behind on this metric. The hill-climbing machine in the report's title is the combination of data pipelines, training infrastructure, RL environments and rewards, evaluations and safety tests into one optimization process that can be iterated (PDF p. 2), and much of it answers a single kind of question: does an improvement seen in a small experiment still hold once it goes into the full training run? The report records the evidence behind each decision alongside the experiments that failed. For anyone outside a frontier pre-training team who wants to understand how models like this are actually trained, I would put this report in my top two for close reading.

MAI-Thinking-1's base model, MAI-Base-1, is a sparse MoE with 34.7B active and 962B total parameters, which the report often rounds to 35B/1T (Table 1, PDF p. 7). The rest of this piece follows the training order, from how a web page becomes training data to how three specialist models become one.

Each arrow in the diagram is a handoff. Pre-training and mid-training draw on the same corpus. The 30T figure is the number of tokens consumed in pre-training; the two mid-training stages come on top of it, 3.55T in total (PDF pp. 5, 21). Once RL starts, the training data becomes the model's own answers and purpose-built task environments. The report says pre-training used no content generated by language models and that the model was not distilled from any third-party model; these are the authors' statements and cannot be checked item by item from outside. As later sections show, the RL stage relies heavily on self-distillation of the model's own trajectories, and some tool-use environments are synthetic.

## How a web page becomes training data

MAI-Base-1's pre-training data includes publicly available data and data licensed through commercial agreements, all processed in-house from a few kinds of raw sources: HTML and PDFs from the web, books and journals, and public GitHub code. The report says no open-source training datasets were used, and machine-learning resource sites such as huggingface.co were excluded from the web data altogether (§2.4, PDF p. 12). Figure 4 shows the processing pipeline for web pages.

<Figure src="/images/posts/mai-thinking-1/fig4-html-pipeline.png" alt="The report's web data pipeline: a proprietary crawl and Common Crawl go through HTML-to-text extraction, content filters, exact deduplication, fuzzy deduplication, cross-source deduplication and embedding generation, then split into four sub-pipelines (general web, STEM, code and key domains) that converge on a central pipeline for decontamination, global deduplication and AI-content filtering." caption="Source: Figure 4 of the report, PDF p. 12; original image embedded in the PDF, unmodified." />

The first step is extracting the main text, and it is also where content is most easily lost. Formulas, code and tables are written in many different ways across the web, and generic extraction tools often drop them while keeping the surrounding text, even though these are the parts that matter most for training reasoning. The report switches methods by source: structured parsers for well-formed formats, hand-written extractors for sites with a stable structure that generic rules handle badly, and LLMs or agents only where semantic judgment is needed. Where an LLM is used, it may only decide to keep or remove fragments of the original text; it cannot add content to the output (§2.4.2, PDF pp. 13-14). STEM pages get their own pipeline: MathML and LaTeX are normalized first, the page is converted to Markdown and split into sections with every formula block and code block kept as its own unit, and a language model then decides section by section whether to keep or drop it (Appendix B.1, PDF p. 83).

Sometimes the better choice is not to convert at all. Wikipedia's wikitext markup lacks mature parsing tools, and elements such as infoboxes are often lost in conversion. The report trains on the raw markup, which is about three times as long as the converted text. The authors say this works better than the stripped-down conversion and consider the extra tokens worth it for a high-value, low-volume source like this (PDF p. 14).

Next comes deduplication. The report's reasoning is that a model of this scale and sparsity readily memorizes content it sees repeatedly, and once it memorizes, it fails to learn the underlying concepts. Scaling behavior in pre-training also depends on how many non-repeated tokens the corpus contains: the larger the model, the sooner it runs out of new information, after which more training tokens bring little benefit (§2.4.3, PDF p. 14). Deduplication therefore happens at several levels: boilerplate such as headers and navigation bars; exact copies at the byte or hash level; near-duplicates found by MinHash LSH (similarity threshold 0.8); pages generated in bulk from one template with only a few words changed; and documents written independently that end up highly similar. The last kind is most common in code, where binary-tree traversals from homework, exams and interview questions come out nearly identical. The report clusters such documents with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and keeps only a few representatives per cluster.

After deduplication within each source, a second pass runs across datasets before training. The team used to handle overlap between sources with explicit rules, for example excluding the Wikipedia domain from every web crawl. As sources and pipelines multiplied, the overlaps became too many to list, so they moved to a global drop-order: all datasets get a priority ranking, and when the same content appears in several datasets it is kept only in the highest-ranked one and removed from all the others (PDF p. 15).

This rule affects how experiments are read. Take a teaching example (not a case from the report): a tutorial appears in both the general-web and the STEM-web datasets, and STEM web ranks higher, so the tutorial stays only on the STEM side. If the STEM extraction pipeline is later improved and picks up a batch of extra pages, those pages are deleted from general web; the STEM dataset gains tokens and general web loses them, yet the corpus contains no new content. A source ablation run at that point would show the STEM dataset becoming more valuable, and part of that gain is only duplicated content changing owner. The report points out explicitly that drop-order has to be taken into account when assessing the contribution of an individual dataset.

After deduplication, the data is filtered and bucketed. Spam and restricted or policy-violating content are removed, and the rest is labeled by quality tier, language, topic, educational value, difficulty and other dimensions, then sorted into interpretable buckets; the later mixture search tunes the weights of these buckets (§2.4.4, PDF p. 15). Every pipeline also decontaminates: data from huggingface.co and its mirrors is removed, and all training sources go through a universal 20-gram fuzzy deduplication (similarity threshold 80%). The report acknowledges these methods are imperfect, which is why day-to-day development relies on private evaluations that do not exist on the web (§2.3.1, PDF pp. 10-11). AI-generated content is also removed as far as possible: web pages are scored by an in-house model and then inspected manually, and domains with a lot of AI content are dropped entirely (Appendix B.1, PDF p. 82).

## Data mixing: rankings on small models can change at scale

The processed data comes from hundreds of sources, and how much weight each gets changes the resulting model noticeably. The most direct way to compare two mixes would be to train a full model on each and then post-train it, which is clearly unaffordable. So two things have to be settled first: which score to compare on, and at what model size.

The score is NLL (negative log-likelihood): the model predicts a held-out text token by token, and the score is the average negative log of the probability it assigns to the true next token; lower is better. The report's internal evaluation suite has nearly 40 sets in five categories: code, STEM, math, general knowledge and multilingual. Sources include worked solutions written by vendors, Microsoft-internal code and PRs that are not on the web, and records of human-AI conversations, all deduplicated against the training data (§2.3, PDF pp. 9-10). Each set is first normalized against a fixed in-house reference model, scores are averaged within each category, and the five categories are combined into one target with these weights:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Target}={}&0.5\,\mathrm{Coding}\\
&+0.175\,\mathrm{STEM}+0.175\,\mathrm{Math}\\
&+0.1\,\mathrm{General\ knowledge}\\
&+0.05\,\mathrm{Multilingual}
\end{aligned}
```

These weights state the team's priorities: code gets half, and math is its own category with the same weight as STEM. It is MAI's own trade-off, not a universal quality score (Eq. 3, PDF p. 10).

The report gives several reasons for not using answer accuracy during pre-training. Frontier benchmarks often require long chains of thought, tool calls and another model to grade the answers, while an NLL evaluation needs only the same forward pass as pre-training, so it is much cheaper and the full suite can run on every experiment. Accuracy is also sensitive to formatting: MATH requires the answer inside `\boxed{}`, and in MBPP the shared prompt uses Unix newlines while the problem statements use Windows newlines. How a base model handles such details itself shifts with the formatting of its pre-training data, so formatting issues leak into the experimental conclusions. NLL uses teacher forcing: every step is conditioned on the true prefix, so a small error does not compound (§2.3.2, PDF p. 11). The flip side is that NLL only shows how well the model predicts correct text, not whether it can write a long chain of reasoning on its own.

The model size for comparisons comes from the model ladder. This model family is set by a single parameter, the number of layers L. L must be a multiple of 6 so that the last layer uses global attention; the hidden size is `L × 256 / 3`, and the other dimensions follow, from 365M active parameters at L12 to 35.6B at L78 (Table 1, PDF p. 7). Every ablation trains a set of models of different sizes on the ladder, all at the same number of tokens per active parameter (TPP), and compares the candidate's scaling curve with the baseline's. Most architecture ablations run at around 100 to 200 TPP, near the Chinchilla-optimal region, while the main run is heavily over-trained at 500 to 1,000 TPP to get a more compact model that is cheaper to serve (§2.2.1, PDF p. 7). From the report's numbers, MAI-Base-1's pre-training used 30T tokens with 34.7B active parameters, about 865 TPP. The experiments that drive decisions and the final run are several times apart in TPP, and the sets of models on the ladder are there to check whether an improvement holds as scale grows.

Data is tested in the same framework. A single-source ablation raises the target data to 50% of the mix and trains from scratch to measure its marginal contribution on the NLL evaluations; a ladder ablation changes the data within the full mix, trains along the ladder, and downsamples data sources to mimic how the final run repeats data multiple times (§2.4.5, PDF pp. 15-16).

The mixture search uses the same target score. The team sampled a large number of candidate mixes around the baseline mix, trained several thousand small models, looked at correlations between the proportions of data subsets and each evaluation, and treated the Eq. 3 target as the function to minimize (§2.5.2, PDF p. 17). This follows the same idea as methods such as RegMix, and it rests on an assumption: which of two mixes is better stays the same as compute increases. The report calls this the rank invariance hypothesis, and the experiment at the start of this piece is a test of it.

Looking back at the training and validation losses of individual sources, the authors found two STEM sources with high-quality content but more fuzzy duplication than the other datasets and less diverse content. They made up 11.8% of the STEM mix and only 0.3% of the code mix. The authors' hypothesis is that these two sources helped the smaller model a great deal, but their lack of diversity made them less useful at larger scale (PDF p. 18). The report gives no control experiment that removes just these two sources, so this remains one explanation.

After this, mixes were judged on one more criterion: besides the score at a fixed experimental scale, how they scale. The search for the final mix alternated between two levels. Data was first divided into about 10 categories, such as code, STEM, PDFs and general web. Local search fixes the proportions between categories and tunes only the weights inside one category, for example how to split code files, commits and PRs; global search fixes the internal make-up of each category and tunes only the proportions between them. Throughout, no dataset was repeated more than 8 times, to avoid overfitting and the diminishing returns of repetition. After a few candidates were chosen, they were trained and compared with about 2.8 times the compute used in the global search, and this time the best candidate did not change with scale (§2.5.3, PDF pp. 18-19).

## The base model: choosing the architecture on the ladder, then training on 30T tokens

Once the data is settled, the architecture and training recipe are also chosen on the ladder, using a measure called efficiency gain (EG). First, a scaling law is fitted to the baseline ladder:

```math
\begin{aligned}
L=f(C)&=AC^{-\alpha}+E\\
\mathrm{EG}&=\frac{f^{-1}(L')}{C'}
\end{aligned}
```

C is the training cost, measured either in FLOPs or in training time on existing hardware; L is an aggregate evaluation loss such as Eq. 3; A, α and E are fitted coefficients, with E the irreducible loss. A candidate model spends cost C′ and reaches loss L′. Plugging L′ back into the baseline curve gives the cost the baseline would need to reach the same loss, `f⁻¹(L′)`; dividing that by C′ gives EG (Eq. 1 to 2, PDF p. 8). EG = 1.3 means the baseline needs 30% more cost to match the candidate; put the other way, the candidate used 1/1.3 ≈ 77% of the baseline's cost, a saving of about 23% at that loss level. Computing EG at several cost budgets shows whether a gain holds, grows or fades with scale.

The same candidate priced in FLOPs and in time gets different EG values." caption="Diagram: how EG is read off a baseline scaling curve, and why the cost unit can change the conclusion (drawn by the author, based on Eq. 1 to 2 and §2.2.2 of the report; the curve shape is schematic, and the two EG values in the lower panel are from Table 2)." />

The unit chosen for cost C decides which question EG answers. The report uses FLOPs by default, because implementations of established architectures have usually accumulated many performance optimizations, and pricing by time would penalize a new architecture; comparing in FLOPs asks how good the architecture is if both implementations get the same care. When the question is whether something pays off on the existing system, the cost becomes training time, written EG_Time. Table 2 is an example where the two units lead to different conclusions.

<Figure src="/images/posts/mai-thinking-1/tab2-eg-flops-time.png" alt="Table 2 of the report: efficiency gain of two MoE-every-layer variants relative to the interleaved layout. The 8/384 variant has a weighted-average EG_FLOPs of 0.94 and EG_Time of 0.73; the variant with 7 routed experts plus 1 shared expert has 1.03 and 0.82." caption="Source: Table 2 of the report, PDF p. 8; faithful crop only. The baseline is MAI-Base-1's interleaved layout (8/512 MoE alternating with dense FFN), measured on ladder models L12 to L30." />

MAI-Base-1's feed-forward layers alternate: a high-sparsity MoE layer (each token picks 8 of 512 experts) followed by a dense FFN. Table 2 compares this with the more common layout of a medium-sparsity MoE in every layer, keeping the ratio of total to active parameters similar across the model families. The every-layer MoE variant with a shared expert has an EG of 1.03 in FLOPs, slightly better than the baseline per unit of compute. Its EG_Time is only 0.82: the baseline needs just 82% of this variant's time to reach the same loss; in other words, the variant needs about 1/0.82 ≈ 1.22 times the baseline's time, roughly 22% more. The variant without a shared expert is behind on both. On this basis the report kept the interleaved layout, and it also observed that the every-layer MoE layout depends heavily on shared experts, while adding shared experts to the interleaved layout brings almost no benefit (§2.1, §2.2.3, PDF pp. 6, 8-9).

The report says a key consideration in the architecture design was co-optimization with the underlying GPU infrastructure, so that both pre-training and RL scale efficiently (§2.1, PDF p. 6). Attention pairs every 5 local attention layers with 1 global attention layer. Local layers use a 512-token sliding window with RoPE; global layers use no positional encoding at all, which the report says performs comparably to RoPE while being cheaper. This layout cuts attention compute in training substantially and also shrinks the KV cache at inference.

The MoE layers use LatentMoE: token representations first go through a shared down-projection and are dispatched to the experts with an all-to-all; after the experts finish and the results are combined, they are projected back to the original dimension. Which experts are chosen is still decided from the original representation before the down-projection; the compression applies only to communication and expert computation.

Expert capacity is another setting that can change conclusions. With finite capacity, tokens beyond an expert's capacity are dropped. The report found that even with a low drop rate and care to avoid causal leakage, comparisons between load-balancing methods reached different conclusions than without token dropping, so it switched to a fully dropless MoE that never drops tokens (§2.1, PDF p. 6).

Several choices are there to keep training stable. At initialization, the attention softmax is close to uniform, which amounts to average pooling over the preceding tokens under the causal constraint; the differences between token representations get flattened, and routing in the following MoE layers becomes badly imbalanced, increasingly so with depth. The report initializes the gains of the RMSNorm at the attention output to zero, so the model starts out like a stack of per-token feed-forward layers, and cross-token interaction comes in gradually during training. In the example in Figure 8, the most loaded expert initially receives 298% more tokens than the average, and with zero initialization this drops to 59%; the report says the change also brought quality gains as measured by EG (§2.6.2, PDF p. 22). For precision, forward GEMMs and data gradients use FP8, weight gradients are computed in BF16 and accumulated in FP32, and the pre-softmax values (attention scores, routing logits, output logits) as well as the entire residual stream from embedding to output stay in FP32 (§2.6.3, PDF p. 22).

Two more hyperparameters are justified by later stages. The learning rate follows a cosine decay from 2×10⁻⁴ to 2×10⁻⁵, ending at only 0.1 times the peak rather than the more common 0.01 times, because decaying less gave better results after RL. A dropout of 0.15 is applied to each layer's output before the residual add; the report acknowledges this is not standard practice, but says it complements weight decay as a regularizer and improves the ladder evaluations (§2.6.2, PDF p. 21). The report shows no comparison data for either.

### The 30T-token run

MAI-Base-1 was pre-trained on 8,192 GB200 GPUs for 30T tokens at a sequence length of 16,384, with a global batch of 134M tokens (Table 6 and §2.6.2, PDF pp. 21-22). The training framework, YOLO (You Only Launch Once), was written from scratch by MAI on top of PyTorch and covers the model definition, parallel sharding, optimizer, data loading and checkpointing; it serves as the learner for pre-training, mid-training, SFT and RL (§2.8, PDF p. 24).

This is where the cost of architecture changes shows up as concrete MFU numbers. MFU (Model FLOP Utilization) measures how much of the GPUs' peak compute goes into model computation: the total FLOPs of the main compute kernels in one training step, such as GEMMs and attention, divided by the product of step time, GPU count and the per-GB200 BF16 dense peak (2.5×10¹⁵ FLOPS). Memory-bandwidth-bound operations such as RMSNorm and activation functions are left out of the numerator, as is the extra compute from activation recomputation; step time is end to end, including data loading, forward and backward passes, communication and optimizer updates. Training actually mixes FP8, BF16 and FP32, but the denominator always uses the BF16 peak (§2.8.4, PDF p. 28). Figure 11 tracks four architecture generations, v2 to v5.

<Figure src="/images/posts/mai-thinking-1/fig11-mfu-eg.png" alt="Figure 11 of the report: MFU and EG for architecture generations v2 to v5. v2 goes from 18% before optimization to 22% after; v3 is 22% both before and after; v4 rises from 16% to 20%; v5 rises from 18% to 20%. EG relative to v2 is 1.00, 1.40, 1.69 and 1.69." caption="Source: Figure 11 of the report, PDF p. 30; faithful crop only. Light bars show MFU with the previous generation's system configuration; dark bars show MFU after optimizations for that version." />

The architecture changes in v3 and v4 both raised EG; v5 matches v4 at 1.69, since that generation mainly made the model bigger. When the architecture changes and the previous generation's system configuration is kept, MFU tends to drop first and recover only after new optimizations land; the light bars for v4 and v5 are both below the previous generation's optimized level. v4 raised the number of experts from 192 to 512, moved routing from top-4 to top-8 and introduced LatentMoE, while training scaled from 4,096 to 8,192 GPUs. With smaller expert GEMMs, CPU launch overhead and kernel efficiency suddenly mattered much more, and the initial MFU fell from 22% to about 16%; switching to deterministic FA4 attention kernels and cutting CPU overhead brought it up to 20%. v5 grew the model to 35B/1T, which sharply raised parameter and activation memory. It initially had to use ZeRO-3 sharding, but the backward pass then spent most of its time on the extra parameter all-gather communication; offloading activations to host memory freed enough device memory to return to ZeRO-2, computation and communication overlapped again, and MFU came back to about 20%. The report says more than 20 infrastructure and kernel optimizations were added over this period, keeping every generation's MFU above 20% (PDF pp. 28-30). Not all of the EG gains in the figure come from these changes either; the report lists only those with major infrastructure implications.

YOLO also guarantees deterministic training: with fixed hardware topology, model configuration and software version, two training runs produce bitwise identical models. To get there, the order of data batches is fixed; checkpoints store all state, including the FP8 scaling history and random-number state; GPU kernels perform reductions in a fixed order; MoE top-k routing uses a stable sort to avoid nondeterministic tie-breaking; and collective communication disables NVLink SHARP and pins the NCCL topology. Disabling SHARP and switching to deterministic kernels and reductions both cost training efficiency (§2.8.2, PDF pp. 26-27). With determinism, results can be compared bit for bit: tests check a set of golden configurations for bitwise agreement in loss, gradient norms and weight checksums, and after a restart, recomputed steps are compared against the historical loss. Upgrading system software or kernels does change the numerics, and such changes are recorded, so if the same configuration ever produces a different result, something has definitely changed.

The loss curve of the 30T-token run showed a few spikes early in training, each recovering quickly; the run never skipped a batch and its configuration was never changed by hand mid-run. Logs show the spikes mostly occurred on code data and coincided with expert load imbalance under dropless routing (§2.6.4, PDF p. 23).

To judge whether GPU time turned into training progress, the report mainly looks at goodput: the ratio of the ideal training duration to the actual wall-clock duration, with the difference broken down into overhead categories (§6.2, PDF p. 60). Pre-training of MAI-Base-1 reached 90.0% goodput with 51 hours of total overhead. Recomputation after falling back to a checkpoint took 6.5 hours and time not spent advancing training steps took 14 hours; the largest item was MFU drops, at 18 hours or about 35% of the overhead, caused by checkpointing, network degradation, memory pressure and hardware health transitions. When MFU drops without the job crashing, GPU time is still being consumed, so the report treats MFU drops as production incidents as well.

## Mid-training: no new data, only a new mix and longer sequences

Pre-training is followed by two mid-training stages. Their data comes entirely from the pre-training corpus, with no new sources and no synthetic data; the corpus is only filtered, reweighted and repacked (§2.5.4, PDF p. 20). The mix tilts further toward what reasoning RL needs: STEM and math rise to 35%, code stays at 55%, and the remaining 10% goes to background data. The STEM portion is filtered mainly from the PDF corpus by Bloom's cognitive levels, keeping only documents that are technically correct, have at least intermediate reasoning depth and sit at the Analyze level or above; code repositories are split into three quality tiers that determine which file types each tier keeps, and a single-file format is added alongside the original repository-level concatenation.

Another limit concerns repetition. Some sources look heavily memorized by the end of pre-training, and showing them several more times in mid-training raises the risk of overfitting. The report estimates this with a proxy: take the drop in validation loss between two pre-training checkpoints and see what fraction of it comes from tokens the model is already nearly certain about (NLL < 0.01, i.e., predicted probability above 99%). The higher the fraction, the more likely memorization or highly repetitive structure is at work, and the stricter that source's repetition cap in mid-training (PDF p. 21).

Mid-training also extends the context from 16K to 256K. The first stage uses 3.4T tokens at 64K; the second runs at 256K and is listed in Table 6 as 150B tokens, while the text of Appendix C.5 says 140B. Both stages use the same mid-training mix and only repack the data into longer sequences, truncating fewer long documents (PDF pp. 21, 87). Training at 256K throughout is impractical because MFU is too low at long sequence lengths, so the team first compared several schedules on small-scale models (Appendix C, PDF pp. 85-87).

The figure measures code NLL: an internal code repository is serialized into one long sequence, NLL is always computed on the last 16K tokens, and the total context (the prefix plus this 16K suffix) grows from 16K to 256K. The longer the prefix, the more code from the same repository the model can use, so NLL should fall. At short contexts all five schedules nearly coincide, so long-context training did not hurt short-context quality. The report also observes that most of the improvement in the 256K extension happens within the first 1% to 10% of training steps (Appendix C.3 to C.4, PDF pp. 86-87).

The final recipe is more conservative than these ablations: mid-training at 64K followed by a 256K extension. The report explains that the ablations looked mainly at NLL, and the interaction between context length and post-training was not yet well understood, so it chose the safer configuration (Appendix C.5, PDF p. 87).

## How one RL problem becomes a parameter update

Pre-training and mid-training give the model broad predictive ability and knowledge, but, the report says, they do not specify how the model should behave, how it should complete long-horizon tasks or how it should allocate computation at inference time (§3, PDF p. 30). RL starts from a checkpoint that has never been exposed to reasoning traces; there is no SFT on existing reasoning traces in between, so the way the model writes out its reasoning has to be learned in RL. The report calls one continuous run of RL training a climb. The earliest STEM climb did not even use a chat format; it reused a raw prompt template from the DeepSeek-R1 paper, asking the model to write its reasoning inside `<think>` tags and then give the answer inside `<answer>` tags (Figure 14, PDF p. 34). RL is split into three specialist models trained in parallel: one for STEM and competitive programming, one for agentic coding and tool use, and one for helpfulness and safety. All three share the core RL recipe, but their problems, environments and reward design differ.

RL runs on Rocket, MAI's in-house asynchronous framework. The learner is YOLO and inference uses SGLang with its router; between them sit a controller and two kinds of workers, and the controller and every worker are each one Python process, that is, one Ray actor (§3.6, PDF pp. 49-50). The rest of this section follows one math problem through the system.

<TechGraph src="/images/posts/mai-thinking-1/rl-problem-lifecycle.svg" title="ONE RL PROBLEM THROUGH ROCKET" alt="The path of one RL problem through Rocket: the controller hands the problem to a problem worker, which first generates 16 answers and computes the pass rate from the task grader; only if it lies between 0.05 and 0.8 is a full group generated, which is filtered again with 0.1 to 0.8. Each answer's reward combines the task reward, language consistency and a length penalty; normalizing within the group gives the advantage, the learner updates with a token-level GRPO loss, new weights are pushed to the inference servers every 5 steps, and answers more than 8 updates old are dropped." caption="Diagram: the path of one RL problem from selection to a parameter update (drawn by the author, based on §3.1.1 to §3.1.5, §3.6 and Figure 19 of the report; thresholds, weights and step counts are the typical settings reported)." />

The controller takes the problem from a problem set and hands it to a problem worker. The problem worker does no generation itself; it sends 16 requests to rollout workers. Each rollout worker sends the prompt to an inference server and gets the model's answer back; for an agentic task it executes the tool calls in the answer, appends the results to the context and asks for the next step, until the model stops calling tools or a step or time limit is exceeded. A math problem can be graded on the rollout worker alone: the final answer is extracted and checked against the reference answer with a formal verifier such as SymPy or by an AI judge; for competitive programming, the generated code runs against test cases. Grading that needs to compare several answers, such as asking an LLM for pairwise comparisons, happens on the problem worker (§3.2, §3.6.3, PDF pp. 37, 51).

Once the 16 answers are graded, the problem worker looks at the pass rate: the fraction of answers that pass the task grader, i.e., whose task reward is positive. This is the task grade, not the total reward after the language and length terms are added; a wrong answer can still have a positive total reward because of the language-consistency term. Only if the pass rate falls within [0.05, 0.8] does the worker go on to generate a full group of answers; in counts, 1 to 12 of the 16 must pass. If none passes, or 13 or more pass, the problem is dropped at this point. After the full group is generated and graded, it is filtered once more with [0.1, 0.8] (§3.1.3 and §3.1.5, PDF pp. 34, 36). Both gates target the same kind of problem: in a group that is almost all right or almost all wrong, the answers barely differ on the task grade; the report calls these low-variance groups, and they provide little relative learning signal. Probing with 16 answers first saves the inference cost of generating a full group for such problems.

The reward has three parts:

```math
\begin{aligned}
R(q,y)={}&R_{\mathrm{task}}(q,y)\\
&+w_{\mathrm{lang}}R_{\mathrm{lang}}(y)\\
&-w_{\mathrm{len}}R_{\mathrm{len}}(y)\\
R_{\mathrm{len}}(y)&=\frac{\rho_q|y|}{\ell_{\max}}
\end{aligned}
```

R_task is the reward for the task itself; for this math problem, whether the answer is right. R_lang rewards keeping the chain of thought in English, deducting a little for every non-English word; the reason comes in the next section. R_len is a length penalty, where `|y|` is the answer's length, `ℓ_max` is the current maximum allowed length and `ρ_q` is the problem's pass rate. Multiplying by the pass rate makes the length penalty weak on hard problems, so the model can write long reasoning, and strong on easy ones, pushing it to cut loops and repeated hedging (§3.1.2, PDF p. 33). The usual settings are `w_lang = 0.5` and `w_len = 0.25`, and the length penalty is removed entirely at the 128K maximum-length stage (PDF p. 36).

Whether this reward is usable depends on whether the reference answers are right. The STEM problem set, STEM Mix, has more than 5 million samples extracted from textbooks, academic PDFs, forum discussions, competition archives and problems supplied by vendors. Multiple-choice questions can be answered by guessing, which makes the reward signal unreliable, and proofs are hard to check automatically without relying on a stronger AI judge. So both are rewritten into open-ended questions with definite answers when they enter the set. The rewrite runs three times and then goes through a consensus step; items that fail to reach consensus are treated as not reliably convertible and dropped, and only a small share of multiple-choice questions is kept so the model stays familiar with the format (§3.2, PDF pp. 37-39). Each problem is then attempted k times by models at each of four ability tiers, and the pass rates sort the problems into difficulty bands. A problem that even the strongest tier rarely solves has two possible explanations: either it is genuinely hard or the reference answer is wrong. In that case the strongest model's consensus answer and the reference answer go to a judge for blind grading in random order; if the judge prefers the consensus answer, the problem is removed, and if it prefers the reference answer, the problem is kept as a hard one (§3.2.1, PDF pp. 38-39). A wrong reference answer left in the set would have RL rewarding the model for learning a wrong answer.

With a reward for each answer, GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) compares answers within the group. An answer's advantage is its reward minus the group mean, divided by the group standard deviation:

```math
A_i=\frac{R_i-\operatorname{mean}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)}{\operatorname{std}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)}
```

A teaching example with 4 answers (not report data; it assumes the language and length terms contribute zero net reward to every answer, so each total reward R_i equals the task reward): total rewards 1, 0, 0, 1 give a mean of 0.5 and a standard deviation of 0.5 (using the population standard deviation here; the report does not say which one it uses), so the advantages are +1, −1, −1, +1. The probability of every token in the two correct answers is pushed up, and that of the wrong answers is pushed down. If all 4 total rewards are exactly equal, for example all 1, the group has no differences, the standard deviation is 0, and the formula cannot say which answer is better, so the problem provides no signal for the update. Four correct answers alone do not guarantee this, since the length and language terms can still make the total rewards differ. The report does not say how its implementation handles a group with zero standard deviation; under this toy example's assumptions, identical total rewards mean all 4 answers are equally right or wrong, and such a group has already been removed by the pass-rate filters. Likewise, any reward that every answer in the group receives equally, such as the same language reward when no answer contains foreign words, cancels out when the mean is subtracted; what matters is how the answers in the group differ.

The loss is aggregated over tokens. The report uses a token-level policy gradient that averages over all tokens of all answers in a batch, with the normalization spanning all data-parallel ranks (Eq. 5, PDF p. 31). Every token then has the same weight in the objective, and a long answer with more tokens contributes more terms to the loss: in the same batch, a 10,000-token answer contributes 10 times as many terms to the objective as a 1,000-token answer, whereas averaging within each answer first would give the two answers equal weight. The actual gradient magnitude also depends on each token's advantage and probability change, so it is not simply 10 times larger.

Every 5 gradient steps, the learner pushes new weights to the inference servers. Asynchrony means the inference servers keep generating with old weights while the learner updates, so answers generated by a policy more than 8 weight updates, or 40 gradient steps, behind are discarded, trading a little freshness for much higher throughput (§3.1.5, PDF p. 36). The push itself requires conversion, because the learner and the inference side may shard and lay out the same parameters differently and at different precisions; Rocket computes a transfer plan once at job start and reuses it for every push (§3.6.5, PDF p. 52).

Inference takes up most of the GPUs in the system. The largest RL job used 4,864 GB300 chips: 4,096 for inference and 768 for the learner (§3.6.4, PDF p. 51). Inference optimizations differ by workload. Single-turn STEM problems have short prompts and generations of up to 128K tokens, so the bottleneck is the KV cache, and the team simply disables prefix caching so that tokens that slide out of the attention window can be evicted promptly. Multi-turn agentic tasks have prompts that keep growing and short generations per turn, so the main cost is prefill, and there the system relies heavily on prefix caching, with hit rates of 97% to 98% in production RL. With thousands of inference chips, replicas crash or hang routinely; the system handles failures at three levels (replica, router and whole job), so a single replica failure only lowers throughput and does not lose answers (PDF pp. 51-52).

## Keeping long RL runs stable

A climb runs for thousands of consecutive steps. The STEM climb in Figure 15 ran for about 5,500 steps, and its curves show several sudden drops. The report's explanation is that before the numerical issues between inference and training were solved, small discrepancies between the two sides accumulated during training and eventually made the climb diverge; moreover, the instability is often written into the parameters many steps before the actual collapse, so reverting to a checkpoint from before the collapse often does not help (§3.1.4, PDF pp. 34-35). The countermeasures fall into two groups: keeping each update from going too far and making the training and inference sides see the same distribution, and preserving the progress already made when a collapse happens anyway.

### How far each update goes

RL data comes from the old policy π_old, the model that generated the answers, while the update applies to the current policy π_θ. The importance ratio r measures how much the two differ in the probability they give the same token:

```math
\begin{aligned}
r_{i,t}&=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid q,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid q,y_{i,<t})}\\
\text{token objective}&=\min\Bigl(rA,\\
&\quad\operatorname{clip}\bigl(r,1-\varepsilon,\\
&\qquad(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}+k\bigr)A\Bigr)
\end{aligned}
```

r = 1 means the new and old policies are equally confident about the token. Clipping sets a trust region for r so that one update cannot push the policy too far. MAI's interval is `[1 − ε, (1 − ε)⁻¹ + k]`; with the usual ε = 0.6, the lower bound is 0.4 and the upper bound at k = 0 is 2.5, and the two bounds are symmetric around 1 on a log scale (ln 0.4 ≈ −0.92, ln 2.5 ≈ +0.92) (PDF pp. 31-32, 36). Under this objective, for a token with positive advantage, once r exceeds the upper bound this term no longer supplies gradient to push its probability further up; for a token with negative advantage, once r falls below the lower bound this term stops pushing it further down. This is a gradient boundary in the surrogate objective. It does not guarantee that probabilities actually stay inside the interval after the update, because the same parameters are also moved by the gradients of other tokens.

<TechGraph src="/images/posts/mai-thinking-1/ratio-clipping.svg" title="WHERE THE RATIO STILL MOVES THE POLICY" alt="Log-axis schematic of the importance ratio r: for positive advantage, r has gradient up to the upper bound 2.5 plus k, beyond which this term stops pushing the probability up; for negative advantage, r has gradient from 0.4 to 50, below 0.4 the term stops pushing further down, the range from the upper bound to 50 is the branch GRPO leaves unclipped, and beyond 50 the outer clip removes this term's gradient; the upper bound moves between 2.5 and 5.0 with the entropy controller." caption="Diagram: the ranges of the importance ratio r in which the objective term still produces gradient (drawn by the author, based on Eq. 5 to 9 and the typical settings in §3.1.5 of the report: ε = 0.6, k_max = 2.5, r_max = 50; the horizontal axis is a schematic log scale)." />

k is a relaxation that adjusts during training and applies only to the upper bound. If the upper bound is too wide, entropy explodes; if it is too narrow, entropy collapse sets in, meaning the output distribution concentrates more and more and the answers become increasingly uniform (PDF p. 32). At each step the report estimates the current policy's average per-token entropy Ĥ on the batch with importance weighting, then updates k with an integral controller:

```math
\begin{aligned}
k\leftarrow\operatorname{clip}\bigl(
&k+\delta\operatorname{sign}(H^*-\hat H),\\
&0,k_{\max}\bigr)
\end{aligned}
```

When entropy is below the target H*, k grows by a step δ and the upper bound widens, so tokens that had low probability in positive-advantage answers can be pushed up faster and the policy spreads out; when entropy is above the target, k shrinks and the trust region tightens. The usual settings are H* = 0.3, δ = 0.25 and k_max = 2.5, so the upper bound moves between 2.5 and 5.0. The report says this works better than adding an entropy bonus term directly to the loss.

<Figure src="/images/posts/mai-thinking-1/fig13-entropy-control.png" alt="Figure 13 of the report: in an 800-step RL climb, the top panel shows observed entropy rising from about 0.2 to around the 0.3 target; the bottom panel shows k, which stays at its limit of 1 early on, switches between 0 and 1 in the middle and is mostly 0 later." caption="Source: Figure 13 of the report, PDF p. 32; faithful crop only. This climb used k_max = 1.0, unlike the typical setting of 2.5 in §3.1.5." />

Figure 13 records an 800-step climb. Entropy starts below the target; k quickly rises to its limit and stays there while entropy climbs to around 0.3. After that, k switches back and forth between 0 and its limit, and later in the run entropy sits slightly above the target while k stays mostly at 0.

The GRPO objective also deliberately leaves two cases unclipped: negative advantage with the new policy giving the token a higher probability (A < 0 and r > 1), and positive advantage with the new policy giving it a lower probability (A > 0 and r < 1). The original reasoning is that no limit is needed when the policy corrects itself in the right direction. The report found that these unconstrained branches sometimes made the gradient norm spike catastrophically, so it wraps all branches in an outer clip with an upper limit r_max = 50 and no lower limit (PDF pp. 32-33). From the form of the objective, when A < 0 a larger r makes `r × A` more negative without bound, while when A > 0 and r < 1, r itself is below 1 and the term is bounded, which is why no lower limit is needed. Once r exceeds 50, the clipped ratio term contributes no gradient; the answer itself is not deleted, and the trust region in the normal range is unaffected.

The maximum generation length is also opened up gradually. It starts capped at 8K and doubles as capability improves, through 16K, 32K and 64K, to 128K. While the model is still weak it rarely needs long reasoning, so this saves a great deal of inference cost (PDF p. 34).

### The training and inference sides must see the same distribution

Rollouts use top-p sampling with p = 0.97: at each step, sampling happens only among the candidate tokens that make up 97% of the cumulative probability, and the other tokens can never be sampled. If training still computed gradients through the logits of tokens outside this nucleus, the distribution optimized on the training side would not match the distribution actually sampled on the inference side; the report says this causes catastrophic off-policy mismatch and divergence within a few training steps. The fix is to store the truncation mask from sampling and, during training, set the logits of tokens outside the mask to negative infinity before the softmax. The cost is that masks must be stored, transferred and replayed; a larger p explores more but makes mask transfer more expensive, and 0.97 is the compromise between the two (§3.1.3, §3.1.5, PDF pp. 34, 36).

Even with aligned sampling distributions, the same weights do not produce exactly the same probabilities in YOLO and SGLang. The two use different kernels, scheduling and parallelism, and tiny per-token log-prob differences accumulate over answers that run to more than a hundred thousand tokens, undermining the importance-sampling correction in off-policy training. In RL, Rocket runs both the learner and the inference engine in BF16; the report says this gives a much smaller numerics gap and more stable training than the lower-precision options it evaluated. On top of that, it applies MoE routing replay and top-p mask replay (§3.6.4, PDF p. 52). In the YOLO section, routing replay appears as router replay for RL rollouts, that is, replaying the expert choices made during the rollout in training, so that tiny numerical differences do not lead the two sides to pick different experts (PDF p. 26). RL uses BF16 throughout, unlike pre-training, where some matrix multiplications use FP8.

The report also observed another phenomenon that appears together with the numerics gap. As contexts grew longer, the model began mixing foreign-language tokens into its chains of thought, and these mixed-language chains of thought correlated with spikes in the log-prob divergence between training and inference, eventually accompanied by unstable training. The language-consistency term in the reward was added for this reason; the report also found that top-p sampling is similarly effective at blocking single, low-probability foreign tokens (§3.1.2, PDF p. 33).

The numerics gap and policy staleness are different problems. The numerics gap means the same weights yield different probabilities in two systems; staleness means the weights that generated an answer are already old. Staleness comes from asynchrony: the learner keeps updating while the inference side still generates with old weights. The longer an answer, the more weight updates pass before it finishes, so the longest answers, usually those for the hardest problems, are the most likely to go stale. Besides discarding answers more than 8 updates behind, the report lowered the learning rate from 10⁻⁶ to 9×10⁻⁷ at the longer length stages to reduce off-policiness (PDF p. 36).

### Self-distillation: continuing after a collapse or a base change

Even with these measures, training can still collapse, or a new starting point may be needed: a new version of the base model arrives, or the run should switch from the raw prompt template to the proper chat format. The report handles these cases with self-distillation: collect the answers already generated during RL, run SFT on a mid-trained checkpoint, and use the resulting model as the starting point of the next RL climb (§3.1.4, PDF p. 34).

<TechGraph src="/images/posts/mai-thinking-1/self-distillation.svg" title="WHAT SELF-DISTILLATION CARRIES ACROSS A RESTART" alt="Self-distillation schematic: answers from the previous RL climb go into a rollout store; about one million correct traces from a range of later checkpoints are selected and mixed with mid-training data, and SFT on a mid-trained checkpoint produces the starting point of the next RL climb; the previous climb's weights are discarded because they may already carry instability." caption="Diagram: what self-distillation carries across and what it discards between two RL climbs (drawn by the author, based on §3.1.4, §3.1.5 and Figure 15 of the report)." />

What this keeps is the trajectories; what it discards is the weights. Weights from before a collapse may already carry the instability, so reverting does not help; the trajectories record the behavior the model learned during the climb, and SFT can transfer that behavior onto a clean starting point. Switching to the chat format only requires changing the format of the SFT data; moving to a new base means distilling the old climb's trajectories into it; and before SFT, samples showing signs of reward hacking can be filtered out.

<Figure src="/images/posts/mai-thinking-1/fig15-self-distillation.png" alt="Figure 15 of the report: pass@1 on AIME 2025 and a hard subset of LiveCodeBench v6 against training steps during the STEM climb, about 5,500 steps. The curves drop suddenly several times; stars mark self-distillation, colors distinguish base-model versions, and gray bars at the bottom mark the maximum generation length from 8K to 128K." caption="Source: Figure 15 of the report, PDF p. 35; faithful crop only." />

Read this figure for three things: the stars mark self-distillation, the curve colors are different versions of the pre-trained and mid-trained models, and the gray bars at the bottom show the maximum generation length at the time. The sudden drops are collapses, each followed by a star, after which performance keeps climbing from the new starting point. The report says that as infrastructure and algorithms improved, self-distillation because of collapses became less and less frequent (PDF p. 35).

The team ran many ablations on self-distillation itself (PDF pp. 35-36). Reasoning traces on the order of one million are enough to match the teacher; more brings diminishing returns and squeezes the policy's output distribution too narrow, leaving no room for exploration once RL resumes. Using only correct traces performs about the same as including incorrect ones, and RL produces far more than one million correct traces, so in the end only correct traces are used. The traces work best when they come from a range of checkpoints late in the climb: mixing in traces from very early checkpoints causes a clear drop that takes many steps to recover, while using traces only from the final checkpoint makes the resumed RL weaker. The authors suggest that traces from several strong checkpoints are more diverse and help exploration, which is only one possible explanation. For a fixed token budget, more diverse problems help more than more traces per problem, and simple random sampling beats biased selection such as picking the shortest traces. One side effect has to be guarded against: early RL runs used shorter maximum lengths, and SFT on their traces makes the model forget the long-context ability learned in mid-training, so mid-training data is mixed in before the length extensions.

Two hyperparameters are deliberately set differently in self-distillation and in RL. Self-distillation uses a relatively high dropout of 0.15 to raise entropy and prevent model collapse. The MoE load-balancing coefficient is 10⁻² in self-distillation but only 10⁻⁵ in RL: the RL data distribution is much narrower than pre-training's, so expert selection easily becomes badly imbalanced, yet a large balancing coefficient in RL would get in the way of steady improvement. Because the self-distillation data comes from RL in the first place, its distribution is similar, and balancing the experts at this step carries over into the subsequent RL (PDF p. 37).

What is distilled here is the model's own output during RL. When the report says it does no distillation, it means it does not train on the outputs of third-party models.

## Three specialist models, merged into one

The three specialist models share the core recipe above, but their problems, environments and rewards differ. The STEM rewards come from checkable answers and test cases, covered earlier.

Each agentic coding problem is a container image holding a code repository checked out at a specific commit, its installed dependencies, a problem statement and the unit tests used for grading. The model has only two tools: one runs bash commands, the other edits files by exact string replacement; when it finishes, a grader runs the tests inside the same container (§3.3.1, PDF p. 41). These problems are built automatically from public GitHub PRs. The grading signal is obtained like this: run the tests on the base commit with only the PR's test changes applied, then again with both the test changes and the code changes applied; tests that go from failing to passing (fail-to-pass, F2P) measure whether the issue is resolved, and tests that pass both times (pass-to-pass, P2P) guard against the patch breaking existing functionality. Each environment is also re-validated in the same sandbox used for training: an empty patch must fail, the original patch must pass, and tests with inconsistent results across repeated runs are removed. Starting from about 4.87 million candidate PRs with linked issues, image building, grading-signal extraction and re-validation leave 265,617 environments from 94,044 repositories. Even an environment that passes re-validation may have a vague problem statement, such as just "fix things", with the real requirements hidden in the tests, leaving the model to guess what the tests want. In that case an agent goes into the same environment to inspect the statement, the repository and the tests, scores the problem, and if needed rewrites the statement so that it matches what the tests require, without leaking the answer or over-specifying the approach (PDF p. 42).

Executable tests do not prevent reward hacking. The report has an LLM monitor the rollouts and humans review the flagged samples, and it found three kinds of hacking: searching the internet for the PR's original patch, digging the commit containing the answer out of the local git history, and modifying the tests so they pass (PDF p. 43). The git-history case cannot be solved by deleting the history, because using git to look at history is a legitimate skill and sometimes provides information needed to solve the problem. Instead, the team scrubs every commit, reference and branch after the base commit, returning the repository to its state at the moment the problem was posed. The countermeasures for the other two are restricting the sandbox's network access, resetting any modified test files before grading, and hiding the test changes from the model during inference. Even so, tricks such as monkey-patching the test framework are hard to rule out, and closing those gaps relies on continued monitoring.

The agentic climb actually trains with STEM problems mixed in. The report says adding STEM problems makes the climb more stable and transfers positively to multi-step software engineering and tool calling; in the other direction, agentic tasks neither help nor hurt single-turn STEM performance (PDF p. 40). Tool use additionally has more than 150 synthetic closed environments with 130,000 tasks, backed by mocked stateful APIs, often with more than 50 tools in a single environment (§3.3.2, PDF pp. 43-44).

Rewards for helpfulness and safety are the hardest to define, and this climb combines three kinds of signals. The first is a reward model trained on human preference data, built from a post-trained MAI-Base-1, which reads several answers to the same prompt at once and scores each from 1 to 5; at use time it is called several times with the answers rotated, and the reward for each answer is the probability that it is scored 5. The second is AI judges scoring against rubrics, and the third is rules that can be checked directly, such as a request to write only one paragraph or to use fewer than 10 words (§3.4.1, PDF pp. 44-45). A high reward-model score does not mean an answer is safe: in a paired rollout audit, 87.8% of answers that violated the safety policy received a score of 3 or higher from the reward model (PDF p. 47, footnote 9). So safety and quality scores cannot simply be averaged with weights. The report uses gating instead: an answer that the safety judge marks as non-compliant gets the minimum reward, regardless of how well it is written. Other contexts use a lexicographic rule, where a lower-priority reward enters the comparison only when all answers in the group tie on the higher-priority reward. Both approaches sidestep converting between the scales of different rewards.

After the three specialists are trained, merging them into one model takes two steps, neither of which involves weight averaging or an ensemble at inference time (§3.5, PDF p. 49). The first step reuses the self-distillation pipeline: trajectories are sampled from each of the three teachers and used for SFT of a single model. The STEM and agentic teachers are sampled across several checkpoints, favoring later ones, keeping several correct answers per problem with only light filtering of degenerate chains of thought; the helpfulness-and-safety teacher's trajectories are scored and filtered with LLM judges and heuristic rules for style, structure, known defects and correctness. The mix is balanced by number of samples: STEM and coding 56%, agentic 11%, helpfulness and safety 33%. By tokens it is 89%, 9% and 2%, because STEM and coding trajectories are much longer; the report says this did not hurt helpfulness and safety capabilities in practice. This SFT runs for 4 epochs, with the learning rate decaying by half from 10⁻⁵, which differs from the usual self-distillation recipe (Table 10, PDF p. 49).

The second step is a lightweight RL stage that mainly improves safety, over-refusal and style further. Its recipe is based on the helpfulness-and-safety climb, but the maximum length stays at 128K and a small share of STEM and coding data is retained. The report says both matter; otherwise reasoning on complex tasks slowly degrades over the climb (PDF p. 49). The result of this step is MAI-Thinking-1.

## What the report discloses, and what it leaves out

The report puts its failed experiments next to the evidence behind each decision: the mixes that reversed at scale in Figure 6, the architecture that falls behind once priced in time in Table 2, the curves in Figure 15 that drop again and again and pick up from new starting points. For most changes, a reader can find at what scale, under which cost unit and against which later stage it was accepted or abandoned, and that is more worth learning from than the final benchmark scores.

Benchmark numbers have to be read with their conditions. MAI-Thinking-1's scores are averages over 4 runs at temperature 1 with top-p 0.97; the comparison models' scores come from their own model cards and release announcements, not from a controlled comparison under one evaluation harness (§4.1, PDF p. 53). Under these conditions it scores 97.0% on AIME 2025 and 52.8% on SWE-Bench Pro. Terminal-Bench 2.0 is only 46.0%, and the report notes that SWE training used only the bash and string-replace tools and no dedicated terminal-interaction environments, so this score reflects transfer from broader agentic training (Table 11, PDF p. 53).

Many key recipe details are not disclosed. The report does not say how much compute or wall-clock time RL used in total; it gives only curves of evaluation scores against steps for the STEM and agentic climbs (Figure 1, Figure 15), and not even step counts for the helpfulness-and-safety climb or the merging stage. The learning-rate floor, the 0.15 dropout and the mid-training mix are said to be supported by post-RL results or ladder evaluations, but no data is shown. The explanation of the rank reversal in the data mixes has no control experiment that removes just those two sources. The self-distillation findings come without tables or variance. The merging stage is not compared with alternatives, such as a single climb on the mixed tasks, and it is impossible to tell how much the SFT and the final RL each contributed. The dataset and vendor lists are withheld for privacy, legal, safety and competitive reasons, so claims of clean data and no third-party distillation can only be read as statements by the authors.
