> Paper: Multi-channel Autobidding with Budget and ROI Constraints<br />
> Authors: Yuan Deng, Negin Golrezaei, Patrick Jaillet, Jason Cheuk Nam Liang, Vahab Mirrokni (Google Research, MIT)<br />
> Published: ICML 2023 (PMLR 202). Theorem numbers follow arXiv v3 (June 2023) by default; where the proceedings version differs, both are given.<br />
> Links: [arXiv:2302.01523](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01523) · [Proceedings version](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/deng23c.html)

A business advertises on Google Ads and Meta at the same time. It has one total budget and an overall return requirement, for example at least 1.3 units of conversion value for every unit of money spent. The two platforms ask for much the same settings: a budget for each campaign, plus a target such as a target cost per conversion. Once these are filled in, each platform's autobidding system buys traffic in auction after auction on the business's behalf and, after a while, reports the total spend and total conversions for the period.

<Figure src="/images/posts/multi-channel-autobidding/fig1-interfaces.png" alt="Campaign creation screens in Google Ads and Meta Ads Manager with budget, target CPA and start and end dates" caption="Source: Figure 1 of the paper, PDF page 3, extracted unchanged. Left: Google Ads; right: Meta Ads Manager, both as they looked when the paper was published in 2023. The red box is Meta's own description of its autobidding goal." />

How money is split across placements inside a platform is the platform's own decision, and the platform sees every one of its auctions. Across platforms, nobody sees all the auctions. Google does not know how Meta's auctions went, and vice versa; the business, an agency or a third-party buying tool can only enter a budget and a target for each platform and read back the totals each platform reports. What the business wants, though, is an optimum in the global sense: the most conversions across all channels, with total ROI on target and total spend within budget.

The paper asks whether per-channel budgets and targets alone can achieve what deciding auction by auction could. Its answer comes in two halves. Targets alone are not enough: in the worst case conversions can fall arbitrarily far below the global optimum (Theorem 3.2). Budgets alone are enough: they reach the global optimum exactly, and channel targets are redundant (Theorem 3.3 and Corollary 3.4). The second half is the starting point of the paper's algorithm, since on that basis the authors learn only budgets, with a method called SGD-UCB. The example behind the first half is clean. The proof of the second half has a step that needs an extra condition, and when the condition fails, budgets alone may get no conversions at all.

## The advertiser can set only budgets and targets

The paper models procurement in three layers (Section 2). At the bottom are auctions: in each period, channel j runs mj parallel auctions, each with a value v (expected conversions) and a cost d (for example the highest competing bid one must pay to win it in a second-price auction). The (v, d) pairs of all auctions in the period are drawn together, as one vector zj, from an unknown discrete distribution pj. A period might be an hour or a day.

<TechGraph src="/images/posts/multi-channel-autobidding/g1-setup-en.svg" title="MULTI-CHANNEL SETUP" alt="The advertiser sends budgets and target ROIs to M channels, which bid in their own auctions and report back only total conversion and spend" caption="Diagram: modeling multi-channel procurement (drawn by the blogger, based on Section 2 of the paper)" />

In the middle are the channels. Given the advertiser's target ROI γj and budget ρj, a channel solves a linear program over the auctions drawn in this period (Eq. 4 of the paper):

```text
max  vᵀx
s.t. vᵀx ≥ γj · dᵀx      channel-level ROI
     dᵀx ≤ ρj            channel-level budget
     x ∈ [0,1]^mj
```

Each component of x is the probability of winning the corresponding auction, so buying only part of an auction is allowed. Both channel constraints must hold for every single draw of zj, so each period is accounted for separately, and this is exactly where the later counterexample bites. The paper assumes that channels really do maximize conversions this way on the advertiser's behalf, which it calls optimal autobidding, and cites a line from the Meta screen in Figure 1 as supporting evidence: “Meta will aim to get the most landing page views and try to keep the average cost around $1.00”. Footnote 1 of the paper treats the target ROI as the inverse of the target CPA, which requires every conversion to carry the same value. When conversion values differ, an inverted CPA only counts conversions per unit of money, and a target ROAS that bids on conversion value is closer to the ROI used here.

At the top is the advertiser. The ideal case is called GL-OPT: the advertiser decides auction by auction whether to buy, the decision may change with every drawn z, and total ROI and total budget only need to hold in expectation. The real case is called CH-OPT: the advertiser can only choose each channel's (γj, ρj) and leaves the channel to solve Eq. 4, with the global constraints again taken in expectation. CH-OPT has three options: budgets only (IB, with γj fixed at 0), targets only (IR, with unlimited budgets), and both (IG). Eq. 2 of the paper requires every channel budget in IB and IG to lie in [0, ρ], where ρ is the total budget. No setting can get more conversions than GL-OPT (Lemma 3.1), because whatever the channels end up buying is itself a feasible solution of GL-OPT; the question is how large the gap is.

## With targets alone, a channel cannot buy half

Theorem 3.2 builds an example from three auctions. The global ROI target is γ = 1, the budget is unlimited, and (v, d) has a single possible value, so there is no randomness.

<TechGraph src="/images/posts/multi-channel-autobidding/g3-roi-fail-en.svg" title="ROI-ONLY FAILURE" alt="Channel 1 has a zero-cost auction, channel 2 has two auctions with ROI below 1; the global optimum gets 1+X conversions, ROI targets alone get 1" caption="Diagram: the counterexample of Theorem 3.2 (drawn by the blogger, based on Section 3 and Appendix A.2 of the paper)" />

Channel 1 has a single free auction, with value 1 and cost 0. Channel 2 has two auctions with values X and 2X and costs 1+X and 2(1+X), so both have ROI X/(1+X), slightly below 1, and on its own neither meets the global target. The global optimum buys channel 1's free conversion and uses the ROI surplus it builds up to subsidize one below-target auction in channel 2, for total conversions of 1+X, total spend of 1+X and an ROI of exactly 1.

With targets only, channel 2 gets a single γ2. Its two auctions have identical ROI, so as long as γ2 is at most X/(1+X), the channel buys both, giving total conversions of 1+3X, total spend of 3+3X and a global ROI below 1, which is infeasible; raise γ2 slightly and the channel buys neither. The advertiser ends up with only the single conversion from channel 1, a ratio of 1/(1+X) to the global optimum, which approaches 0 as X grows.

The trouble is that a target is a cutoff. Auctions with the same ROI in channel 2 either all clear it or all fail it, and the advertiser has no way to tell the channel to buy only half. A budget can do that: with no target and ρ2 set to 1+X, the channel stops once it has spent 1+X and gets X conversions, which together with channel 1's one conversion is exactly the global optimum. This is a deliberately constructed linear program. It shows how the two settings differ in mechanism, not how any platform's product actually behaves.

## With budgets alone, bad days still get bought

The paper then proves that budgets alone are enough (Theorem 3.3): for any global target γ > 0 and total budget ρ > 0, the best conversion achievable with budgets only equals GL-OPT. Corollary 3.4 adds that setting both also equals GL-OPT and that there is an optimal solution with every γj = 0, so channel targets are redundant. The ICML proceedings version states both results the same way as arXiv v3.

The idea of the proof is short (Appendix A.3 of arXiv v3, Appendix B.3 of the proceedings). Take an optimal solution x̃(z) of GL-OPT, set each channel's budget to x̃'s expected spend in that channel, ρ̃j = E[dᵀx̃j(zj)], and show that a channel solving its own problem with ρ̃j gets no fewer conversions and no worse ROI than x̃. The key step is Eq. (16) of the proceedings, Eq. (17) of arXiv v3: for every draw zj, the channel's solution is at least as good as x̃j(zj), on the grounds that the channel's solution is optimal. Optimality only compares feasible solutions, so the step needs x̃j(zj) to be a feasible solution of the channel problem for that draw, meaning dᵀx̃j(zj) ≤ ρ̃j for every zj. But ρ̃j is only x̃'s expected spend. A GL-OPT solution may vary with z, spending more in one period and less in another, as long as the average stays within budget, whereas a channel budget is a hard cap in every period. As soon as the global optimum spends unevenly across periods, this step has nothing to stand on.

The example below is the blogger's construction, not an erratum from the paper's authors. It shows what the conclusion turns into once that step lacks its condition.

<TechGraph src="/images/posts/multi-channel-autobidding/g7-good-bad-day-en.svg" title="GOOD DAY, BAD DAY" alt="One channel, good day v=20 d=10 and bad day v=1 d=10 with equal probability; global ROI target 2 and total budget 10; global optimum 10, budgets alone 0, adding a channel target recovers 10" caption="Diagram: a counterexample where budgets alone fail (constructed by the blogger, based on the stochastic setting of Eq. 1, 2 and 4 of the paper; checked with exact rational arithmetic)" />

There is one channel with one auction, a period counts as one day, and good days and bad days each have probability one half. On a good day the value is 20 and the cost 10, so v/d = 2; on a bad day the value is 1 and the cost 10, so v/d = 0.1. The global ROI target is γ = 2 and the total budget ρ = 10, so under the paper's rule the channel budget must lie in [0, 10].

- Global optimum: buy the whole auction on good days and nothing on bad days, for an expected spend of 5 and expected conversions of 10. Buying even a little on a bad day makes the ROI surplus negative, and since the good-day ROI is exactly 2 there is no surplus left to subsidize it, so GL-OPT = 10.
- Budgets only: γ1 = 0 and the same budget b every day. The auction has positive value on both kinds of day, so the channel buys anyway, and for b up to 10 it buys a share of b/10 on both. Expected conversions are 1.05b and expected spend is b, an ROI of only 1.05, below 2. Every positive budget violates the global ROI, only b = 0 is feasible, and the best value with budgets only is 0.
- Adding a channel target: γ1 = 2 with a budget of 10. On bad days v/d is only 0.1 and the channel buys nothing by itself; on good days it buys the whole auction. Expected conversions are 10 and expected spend 5, both global constraints hold, and the global optimum is reached. Any γ1 above 0.1 and at most 2 gives the same result.

The example stays within the paper's own setting: the channel constraints hold state by state (Eq. 4), the global constraints hold in expectation (Eq. 1), and the channel budget stays within [0, ρ] (Eq. 2). In exact rational arithmetic, the ROI surplus of the global problem is −19·x_bad/2 (x_bad being the share bought on a bad day), and with budgets only it is −19b/20, so neither conclusion depends on a numerical grid or floating-point error. The missing step can be seen concretely here. GL-OPT's expected spend in this channel is 5, so the proof sets the channel budget to 5, while buying the whole auction on a good day costs 10 and does not fit under that cap: the purchase that Eq. (16) compares against is not even feasible on a good day.

So budgets alone may get no conversions at all, and the equality in Theorem 3.3 fails; reaching the optimum requires γ1 > 0, so the statement in Corollary 3.4 that some optimal solution has every γj = 0 fails as well. A budget is the same number every day, and when a bad day comes it still gets spent. A channel target in Eq. 4 is a cutoff checked separately on each day, and bad-day traffic does not clear it.

The budget cap itself can also create a gap. If the total budget is only 5 and the global target is 1, the global optimum still buys the whole auction on good days, but that day costs 10, more than the channel budget cap of 5. Budgets alone and both settings together then reach only 5.25, so the statement in Corollary 3.4 that setting both also equals GL-OPT needs a condition in the stochastic setting too.

What these examples refute are the two unconditional statements of Section 3. The learning guarantee of Section 4 adds two assumptions. Assumption 4.1 requires a channel to spend any budget up to ρ in full in every state. Assumption 4.2 requires every channel in every state to offer some way of buying with ROI strictly above the global target γ. In the first example, the good-day v/d is exactly 2 and the bad-day v/d only 0.1, so neither kind of day satisfies Assumption 4.2, and the example cannot be used directly to refute the theorem of Section 4.

Nor does stationary traffic repair the step. The distribution in the example is itself stationary, with every day drawn independently from the same distribution of good and bad days; what is missing is feasibility in each state, not stability of the distribution over time. For the step to hold, (v, d) must take a single value (the setting Theorem 3.2 uses), or it must be shown separately that the global optimum satisfies the channel budget constraint in every state. When each period contains many auctions, part of the difference between good and bad states may average out and the gap may shrink, but that would need its own argument, which this article does not provide.

## Each setting handles one job

Taken together, the two examples make the division of labor clear. In Theorem 3.2 a budget can make a channel buy only half and a target cannot, so subsidizing across channels takes budgets. In the good-day, bad-day example a target can make a channel hold back on bad days and a budget cannot, so screening out bad periods takes targets. Both situations are common in practice. Channels like branded search or retargeting have very high ROI while prospecting misses the target on its own; give every channel the same target and prospecting either does not run or runs fully, missing the target overall. When traffic quality swings over time, during sales events, on weekends or around breaking news, a fixed daily budget still gets spent on the bad days.

Platform products are not the same as the paper's model. According to Google's current help pages, [Maximize conversions](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7381968) without a target tries to get as many conversions as possible within the budget, close to IB; [Target CPA](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6268632) or Target ROAS plus a budget is close to IG; and a very loose budget with bidding driven only by the target is close to IR. As these help pages describe them, the targets and budgets of these Google products are averages. Target CPA sets the average cost per conversion one hopes to achieve, and actual costs vary around it. [About spending limits](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/10486637) says that for most campaigns the daily billed cost can reach twice the average daily budget and a month stays within 30.4 times the average daily budget, with exceptions such as campaigns that pay for conversions and separate rules for budgets changed mid-month. This makes the product's constraints closer to GL-OPT's expectation constraints than to the per-period hard cap of Eq. 4, letting a good day spend more and a bad day less. The multipliers have exceptions, though, and nothing here shows that a longer window brings results closer to the global optimum; that would need a separate proof.

How a channel solves its own problem is also a product decision, and it changes. In its [note on bid strategy changes](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/17061251), Google writes that budget-limited campaigns with target-based bidding often used to overperform their targets, for example a $10 target CPA achieving $5, and that after the change they move toward the target more consistently; the change covers campaign types including Search, Shopping, Performance Max, Demand Gen and Travel. [The accompanying FAQ](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/17125145) says the rollout began on August 17, 2026 and finished on August 27, did not change the auction mechanics, did not automatically change users' budgets or targets, and leaves campaigns not limited by budget unaffected. In the Eq. 4 model, when the budget binds first the channel buys only the most cost-effective share, so beating the ROI target is simply part of the solution; a platform that now moves toward the target shows that a product's way of solving can differ from this simplified model. Using a 2023 model to infer a platform's response curve today means checking this first.

## Conversions as a function of budget form a concave curve

The budget-only policy may not always be optimal, but it is what the paper's algorithm learns, and its structure determines how to learn it (Lemma 4.3, Lemma 4.5 in the proceedings). With γj = 0, Eq. 4 keeps only the budget constraint, and for one fixed draw of auctions it is a fractional knapsack: buy in decreasing order of v/d until the budget runs out. For that draw, conversions grow piecewise linearly with budget, the slope of each piece is the v/d of that auction, and the slopes get smaller as the budget grows. The expected conversion Vj(ρj) is the probability-weighted average over all draws, so each of its slopes is a weighted combination of the corresponding slopes across draws. A weighted average of concave functions is still concave, so Vj is also piecewise linear and concave overall.

<TechGraph src="/images/posts/multi-channel-autobidding/g4-greedy-en.svg" title="FRACTIONAL KNAPSACK" alt="The channel buys in decreasing v/d, so conversion grows with budget along a concave piecewise linear curve" caption="Diagram: the greedy structure of the channel problem for one fixed draw (drawn by the blogger, based on Lemma 4.3 of the paper; the expected curve is a weighted average of the per-draw curves)" />

Concavity buys two things. First, the advertiser's problem of splitting budget across channels becomes a convex optimization. Second, the corresponding Lagrangian

```text
Lj(ρj, λ, μ) = (1 + λ) · Vj(ρj) − (λγ + μ) · ρj
```

first rises and then falls in ρj, so the optimal budget sits at one of the curve's kinks. λ is the dual price of the global ROI constraint and μ the dual price of the total budget. The formula uses the budget ρj directly as the spend, which relies on Assumption 4.1: the channel always spends whatever budget it is given. Let s be the slope of the current piece, the extra conversions bought by one more unit of money, that is, the marginal ROI. Adding budget pays off exactly when (1+λ)·s − (λγ+μ) ≥ 0, which rearranges to

```text
s ≥ (λγ + μ) / (1 + λ)
```

Together the two dual prices set one common marginal ROI cutoff: each channel keeps adding budget until the marginal ROI of the next piece falls below it. All channels share the same cutoff, which is what equal marginal ROI usually means and a plain statement of the KKT conditions of this convex problem. Because the curves are piecewise linear, the optimal budget often stops at a kink, where the slopes on its left and right bracket the cutoff, so the numerical slopes of different channels need not be exactly equal. Figure 2 of the paper shows one channel finding its optimal kink at given dual prices.

<Figure src="/images/posts/multi-channel-autobidding/fig2-lagrangian.png" alt="Lagrangians of one channel under three realizations and their expectation, maximized at the second kink" caption="Source: Figure 2 of the paper, PDF page 16, extracted unchanged. The solid grey line is the expected Lagrangian; the red dot is the optimal budget r(j,2) at dual prices c = (4, 2); the two bold segments are the slopes on either side of the optimum." />

The flatter the slopes on either side of the optimum, the less it costs to get the budget slightly wrong, but the more samples it takes to tell which kink is optimal, which is exactly the difficulty in learning.

## Learning budgets: one value to try per period

The advertiser does not know what Vj(ρj) looks like. The distribution pj is unknown, channels do not report individual auctions, and in each period each channel can be given only one budget and returns one total conversion count; what the budgets not chosen would have produced is never seen. This is bandit feedback.

The standard approach to constrained optimization is dual SGD: keep two prices λ and μ, in each period find the budget that maximizes the Lagrangian at the current prices, and update the prices according to how much the constraints are violated. The middle step is where it gets stuck, because taking the argmax requires knowing Lj, which is exactly what is unknown. Remark 4.1 of the paper contrasts this with the budget pacing of Balseiro et al. 2017. There the bidder faces the auctions directly and the Lagrangian has a special structure, so scaling the value by the prices gives the bid v/(1+μ) outright and bandit feedback does no harm. At the multi-channel layer, a channel's black box sits between setting and outcome, and no such closed form exists.

The authors' answer is to give each channel a multi-armed bandit (Algorithm 1).

<TechGraph src="/images/posts/multi-channel-autobidding/g5-sgd-ucb-en.svg" title="SGD-UCB LOOP" alt="Each period: pick budget arms by UCB, observe conversions, update dual prices, and trigger the safety valve if needed" caption="Diagram: one period of SGD-UCB (drawn by the blogger, based on Algorithm 1 of the paper)" />

1. Discretize the budget range [0, ρ] into K arms with spacing δ, and in the first K periods pull each arm once in turn.
2. In each later period, each channel picks the arm with the largest optimistic Lagrangian value: the arm's mean conversion plus the confidence radius √(2 log T / N), multiplied by (1+λ), minus the spend cost (λγ+μ)·ρj at current prices.
3. Observe the conversions and update the arm's mean and count. Under Assumption 4.1 spend equals the budget, so it need not be observed separately.
4. Compute the ROI slack g1 = Σ(Vj − γρj) and the budget slack g2 = ρ − Σρj, update λ and μ by projected gradient steps, and cap both at CF.
5. Safety valve: if the cumulative ROI slack becomes too negative or cumulative spend too high, stop learning and give every channel a very small budget for the remaining periods.

The final output is the time average of the budgets over the T periods. Averaging has a concavity rationale: a concave function evaluated at the average budget is no lower than the average of its values at the individual budgets. Constraint satisfaction and the error bound hold in expectation and require T to be large enough; they do not guarantee that any particular day during learning meets the targets.

### The difficulty is that the prices keep moving

Combining UCB with SGD looks natural, but the analysis is not that of an ordinary multi-armed bandit. Each arm's reward is a Lagrangian value that depends on the current λ and μ. Those prices are updated step by step by SGD rather than drawn from some fixed distribution, so to UCB they act as adversarial contexts. The authors deal with two consequences. First, reward magnitudes are uncontrolled: the larger λ and μ, the larger the absolute value of Lj and the more UCB's error is amplified, so the dual variables are capped at CF to keep Lj within a constant range (Lemma 4.4). Second, how hard exploration is changes with the prices. Back in Figure 2, the slopes on either side of the optimal kink are set by λ and μ. When the slopes are flat, choosing the wrong arm is cheap but hard to detect; when they are steep, it is easy to detect but costly. Lemma 4.5 (Lemma 4.7 in the proceedings) introduces a threshold σ, treats the two kinds of periods separately, and obtains an error bound of O(δT + σT + 1/(σδ)) for the UCB part.

The guarantee the paper states (Theorem 4.6, Theorem 4.8 in the proceedings) is this: under Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2, with η = Θ(T^-1/2) and δ = σ = Θ(T^-1/3), and for large enough T, the cumulative regret over T periods is O(T^2/3), the expected conversions of the output budgets are within O(T^-1/3) of GL-OPT, and both global constraints hold in expectation. The rate comes from adding the UCB error to SGD's own O(ηT + 1/η) and balancing the two with these choices. In Remark 4.2 (Remark 4.9 in the proceedings) the authors acknowledge that the optimal rate for this combination, with one query per round, unknown constraints and a non-smooth function, is still an open problem.

The statement with GL-OPT as its benchmark borrows Theorem 3.3: the first step of the regret decomposition (Appendix B.1 of arXiv v3, Appendix C.1 of the proceedings) cites GL-OPT = CH-OPT(IB) directly. SGD-UCB searches only within the class of budget-only policies, so the best it can approach is the optimum of that class; whether the guarantee carries over to GL-OPT depends on whether that equality holds under Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2. The paper does not argue this separately and this article does not prove it, so the theorem is not rewritten here as if it had already been repaired. The paper also says that the results and proofs of Theorem 3.3 and Corollary 3.4 still hold for multi-slot auctions with increasing marginal values (VCG, for example), which carries the problematic step along with them.

## Experiments: 91% on synthetic data

All numerical experiments use synthetic data (Section 6). There are 10 channels, each with 100 auctions per period, a total budget of ρ = 10 and a global ROI of γ = 1.3. For the first 5 channels values and costs are sampled uniformly from [0,1]; for the last 5, values are sampled from [0,2], giving better average value for money, and the authors call these the lucrative channels. Each channel's support has 5000 values, the probability distribution is generated at random, and the whole experiment is repeated over 100 independent trials. Under the hindsight-optimal budgets, the lucrative channels take about 80% of the budget.

<Figure src="/images/posts/multi-channel-autobidding/fig3-results.jpeg" alt="Left: box plots of the percentage of global optimal conversion for different numbers of periods T; right: heatmap of average percentage at T=200 for combinations of corruption factors" caption="Source: Figure 3 of the paper, PDF page 24, extracted unchanged. Left: α1 = α2 = 1; right: fixed T = 200, normalized percentage computed with conversion under optimal autobidding." />

The left panel shows how many periods learning takes. Read off the chart, the median is about 86.5% at T = 100, about 91% at T = 200, about 95.4% at T = 500 and about 97% at T = 1000, with the boxes narrowing as T grows. The authors point out that T = 200 exceeds 91%, which is 8 to 9 days if a period is one hour. These are results over 100 synthetic trials, not a real campaign and not a guarantee after launch.

The right panel shows what happens when channels do not bid optimally. The authors model this with corruption factors: non-lucrative channels achieve only α1 times the optimal conversions and lucrative channels α2 times. The algorithm sees the discounted conversions, while evaluation switches back to optimal conversions. In other words, the percentages in the heatmap come from taking the budgets the algorithm chose under discounted feedback and scoring them on the undiscounted, optimal conversion curves; they do not say that the channels' actual discounted conversions reached that share of the ideal. With α1 fixed, results improve as lucrative channels get closer to optimal (larger α2), and the worst cell, α1 = 1 and α2 = 0.2, is 81%. With α2 fixed, results improve as non-lucrative channels get worse: in the α2 = 0.2 column, lowering α1 from 1.0 to 0.2 raises the result from 81% to 92%. The authors' explanation is that non-lucrative channels look even worse, so the algorithm shifts more budget to lucrative channels, which offered better value for money all along. With both factors at 0.2 the result is still 92%, which the authors take as graceful degradation when channels do not bid optimally.

Several points in these experiments need a careful reading. Corruption multiplies a whole channel's conversions by a constant. With different factors for different channels, the relative returns of channels change and their ranking can even reverse, but each channel's internal purchase order and the shape of its response curve stay the same, only scaled down. If a channel's suboptimal bidding instead takes the form of buying the wrong auctions inside the channel, such as spending on low-quality traffic, the shape of the curve changes, which this experiment does not cover, and the data are synthetic throughout. Nor is the method compared with any baseline, such as splitting budget in proportion to historical ROI. As for the proof issue above, the paper states that the denominator is GL-OPT as defined in Eq. 1 but does not say how that value was computed. Each support point is itself 100 auctions sampled independently and uniformly, so after sorting by v/d different states look much alike, and on data like this the gap between budgets only and the global optimum may be small. That is an inference about the experimental setup, proved neither by the paper nor by this article.

## Before plugging SGD-UCB into a real system

The paper's modeling frame applies widely: the advertiser never touches the auctions, only settings, and behind each setting is a black box that optimizes on its behalf. Brands splitting budget across platforms, agencies across DSPs and DSPs across SSPs or ad exchanges all have this shape. [The post on ROS pacing and dynamic take rates](/blog/pacing-ros-dynamic-take-rate) is about how to bid inside the black box, and the dual variables mean the same thing at both layers: λ is the price of the ROI constraint and μ the price of the budget.

Plugging SGD-UCB into such a system means checking several premises of the model first. It treats channels as independent with conversions that simply add up, whereas each platform claims conversions within its own attribution window, so the same purchase may be counted by two platforms, and the algorithm then optimizes the sum of reported conversions rather than true incrementality. Assumption 4.1 requires channels to always spend their full budgets, which does not hold for large budgets or channels with limited traffic; when budgets go unspent, spend becomes another quantity to observe. Learning also has a cost: the first K periods try every budget level in turn, the number of levels grows with T, and since the paper gives no constant for δ, how many of the T = 200 periods go to exploration cannot be told. Channels are assumed to bid optimally and periods to be independent and identically distributed, and both need to be checked separately in real campaigns; Section 7.2 only mentions switching to corruption-robust bandit algorithms, without results.

Put together, SGD-UCB learns how to split budget among channels within the class of budget-only policies. In the Eq. 4 model, screening out bad periods takes channel targets, while real Target CPA or Target ROAS products control costs on average and do not promise to reject every bad day. The two examples therefore leave the advertiser with a concrete question: how much its channels differ between good and bad periods, and how much of that a platform target enforced on average can filter out.
