One commercial rule keeps repeating across value chains: wherever a layer is scarce enough to block delivery, excess profits pool there; once that layer becomes standardized and cheap, those profits do not vanish, they move straight to the next scarce layer beside it.

Apply that rule to the past six years of AI, and many surprising reversals click into place. From Scaling Laws in 2020, to open-weight models, RAG, MoE, and digital workers, to 2026's viral persona skills, software reverse engineering, and hard-data markets, the technologies look unrelated while the market mispricing stays familiar: **observers price a cost drop in one layer as if the profit structure of the entire value chain had already been rewritten.**

One shift on the ground over the past two years makes this plain: **data vendors' salespeople used to chase big-tech procurement teams to sell standard labeling packs; now frontline researchers show up at data vendors' doors with their models' failure cases, placing custom orders for hard problems.** Bargaining power flipped in two years because the industry's profit pool moved again.

## Scaling Laws: Turning Unpredictable R&D Risk into a Capital Barrier

Go back to 2020. OpenAI published its Scaling Laws paper, showing a steady power-law link between pre-training loss, parameter count, dataset size, and compute. The compute poured along that curve did deliver real capability leaps and real revenue.

Yet scientific accuracy was only half the reason capital markets aligned around Scaling Laws; the deeper shift was how tech giants competed. For executives and investors, the biggest business risk in foundational research is uncertainty, since you cannot schedule an unknown algorithmic insight into a three-year budget. With a power law, GPU purchases, data budgets, and funding rounds fit onto a calendar, turning competition from betting on research luck into competing on balance sheets and chip allocations. For a capital-intensive industry, that is the clearest fundraising story imaginable.

The slip happened when predictable loss curves were treated as predictable commercial returns. Follow-up research drew clear limits. DeepMind's Chinchilla adjusted the parameter-to-token ratio, and later work proved that once serving costs enter the math, smaller models trained longer are commercially superior (see [Data Taste Engineering Boundaries](/blog/what-is-good-data-taste-llm-engineering-boundaries)). In 2023, Stanford researchers showed that many abrupt "emergent" jumps flatten into smooth curves under continuous metrics. By May 2025, Anthropic tested Claude 3.7 Sonnet and DeepSeek-R1 and found that when models used external hints, their Chain-of-Thought traces disclosed those hints less than 20% of the time, meaning readable reasoning text cannot serve as a reliable compliance audit.

In November 2025, former OpenAI chief scientist Ilya Sutskever said the field was moving from "the age of scaling" back to "the age of research": pre-training feeds everything in, while reinforcement learning requires picking data and designing grading. SemiAnalysis estimates of frontier lab compute match that shift: total spending did not shrink, its weight moved from pre-training to post-training and RL (see [Open Post-Training Frontiers 2026](/blog/open-post-training-frontiers-2026)). Buying GPUs for pre-training alone no longer locks in pricing power.

## Open Weights, RAG, and Price Wars: Cheap Models, Adjacent Profits

After ChatGPT brought LLMs to the public in late 2022, an unusual business move appeared: platform giants that had spent heavily training base models started releasing open weights for free.

Nobody was running a charity. Companies looked like they were building the same thing while monetizing completely different layers. Pure model labs billed API calls and application startups sold vertical subscriptions, while platform giants gave weights away to compress margins in the model layer so closed upstream models could not capture their pricing power. Meta stated openly that open Llama weights kept its social and ad businesses from being boxed in by closed platforms; Alibaba noted repeatedly in earnings calls that Qwen APIs and open models drive cross-sales across cloud compute, databases, and enterprise services. Models became customer acquisition while profits settled into cloud and distribution.

Once models became easy to access, enterprises hit practical walls: general models lacked private company data and still fabricated answers. In 2023, **RAG** (Retrieval-Augmented Generation, pulling internal company records before answering) became the fastest-selling enterprise offer, quickly turned into managed services by AWS and other clouds. Dropping files into a vector database is still far from understanding a business, with missed recall, permissions, and stale files requiring real engineering. Commercially, however, RAG solved packaging: without forcing companies to retrain models, it turned an abstract capability into a standard monthly product an IT department could actually budget for.

By 2024, capable base models were no longer scarce either, and price wars thinned per-call margins, pushing competition to **unit economics**. On the surface, the field split in two directions. DeepSeek-V3 combined sparse MoE with FP8 and reported $5.576M for a single official training run at an assumed $2/H800 hour rental rate (covering only that run's compute rental, not prior R&D, ablations, or cluster ownership), followed by DeepSeek-R1 and distilled models pushing API prices down further. OpenAI's o1 moved the other way with Test-Time Compute, deliberately spending extra inference compute on hard problems to raise accuracy.

Both tracks worked on the same customer equation: **what does one qualified outcome actually cost?** Token price is often a tiny slice of the bill. If a cheap model triggers repeated retries plus line-by-line human review and rework, even free API calls lose money for the buyer.

## From Software Seats to Digital Workers: Pricing Power Jumps, Moats Do Not Vanish

Once qualified delivery math became clear, software vendors in late 2024 went after a much bigger budget. For twenty years, enterprise software monetized per-seat productivity budgets with high gross margins and light liability, leaving operational errors on the human employee. Now vendors aimed straight at payroll and outsourcing budgets by selling AI as **digital labor**.

In December 2024, Salesforce launched Agentforce 2.0 explicitly as a digital-labor platform, evolving from $2 per conversation to granular Flex Credits around $0.10 per standard action, effectively pricing software like piece-rate labor.

Shift the pricing anchor from "tool" to "worker," and customer acceptance criteria plus error liability tighten overnight. Tool buyers ask how much time an employee saves; labor buyers calculate unattended resolution rates, supervision headcount, and who pays when live production data is corrupted.

Market reality in 2025 exposed that gap quickly. Fintech firm Klarna announced in early 2024 that its AI assistant handled first-month chat volume equivalent to 700 full-time agents (an equivalent-workload claim, not a layoff of 700 staff). By May 2025, Bloomberg reported its CEO admitting cost-cutting had gone too far, quality and complex escalations had suffered, and Klarna was hiring humans again to restore live fallback. In June 2025, Gartner forecast that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 due to rising costs, unclear value, and weak risk controls, noting that only a small fraction of thousands of self-proclaimed agent vendors offered true agency. MIT NANDA's August survey reported the same divide: most pilots stalled on workflow redesign and data cleanup, with very few reaching the P&L with rapid revenue growth.

That is why MCP, Skills, and persistent memory became mandatory in 2025: without live system access and reusable procedures, an agent cannot even start the job. Yet connecting more tools does not guarantee stable delivery. As analyzed in [Why Autonomous Software Factories Fail](/blog/关不了的灯-software-factory为什么必然失败), current RL lacks a reward signal for long-term maintainability, leaving technical debt in codebases and workflows billed to the engineering team behind them.

In March and April 2026, after Skills matured into a cross-platform standard, open-source persona distillation and software reverse engineering surged. On one side, `colleague-skill` (now renamed `titanwings/distilly`) and `nuwa-skill` packaged an expert's public writing and habits into an installable Skill. On the other, HKUDS's [`HKUDS/CLI-Anything`](https://github.com/HKUDS/CLI-Anything) wrapped GUI apps like LibreOffice and Blender into agent-ready CLIs, while [`morluto/rea`](https://github.com/morluto/rea) (Reverse Engineer Anything) connected coding agents via MCP to Ghidra and IDA Pro to decompile binaries and rebuild features.

These tools save real engineering hours, yet social media stretched them into "zero SaaS defensibility," collapsing four completely different business tiers into one: **operating software, reading partial internals, rewriting a feature, and replacing a company's full product and operating system.**

Near-free code generation does not erase commercial moats. As examined in [Building Is Easier, Generating Value Is Still Hard](/blog/building-is-easier-generating-value-is-still-hard), mature software commands margins not for raw source code alone, but for proprietary data, customer switching costs, compliance commitments, and operational trust. As code gets cheaper, profit pools concentrate even harder in those harder-to-copy business assets.

## AI Data Markets and RL Environments: High Cashflow, Tough Valuation Quality

Once base weights, API tokens, and routine code turned cheap, the most lucrative profit pool in software during 2025 and 2026 moved upstream to **hard post-training data and verifiable RL environments**.

China's bargaining-power flip shows this value-chain shift clearly. Legacy labeling was low-margin labor outsourcing selling interchangeable "shelf inventory" such as speech transcription, bounding boxes, and generic Q&A packs, leaving salespeople chasing procurement on price. Once reasoning models and RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) became the main differentiator, major labs paid the price for bargain hunting: lowest-bid question banks and synthetic "expert" packs laundered through LLMs failed to improve post-training scores and could degrade models.

Data decision power inside major Chinese labs shifted from procurement back to post-training researchers and standalone data groups (ByteDance elevated its AI data unit into a parallel top-level department, and core research teams at leading labs began writing data specs directly). Buying switched from off-the-shelf inventory to reverse-commissioned R&D consumables: researchers brought failure cases from cross-statement financial audits, multi-file repo refactors, or frontier physics derivations straight to capable vendors, ordering **hard problems with verifiable rules that current frontier models still miss**, paired with clean reasoning traces.

Several local realities amplified that reversal in China. High-signal Chinese domain knowledge sits heavily inside WeChat, vertical communities, and paywalled journals rather than open web pages, leaving public pre-training corpora thinner than English and exposing gaps earlier. Tighter constraints on top-end GPUs raised the payoff of maximizing data signal per chip. China has dense pools of top university graduate students and seasoned practitioners whose rates (starting around 400 RMB per hour for hard expert work) remain far below Western equivalents, yet recruiting real specialists, screening out unqualified annotators, catching LLM-assisted cheating, running peer review, and keeping audit logs requires heavy operational management that lean research teams cannot run themselves. Combined with monthly domestic release races, labs cannot wait to build from scratch and compete to lock in capacity from the few vendors with working expert pipelines.

Globally, cashflow in this upstream layer is already substantial. In June 2025, Meta invested roughly $14.3B for a 49% non-voting stake in Scale AI, pushing its valuation past $29B; largely bootstrapped Surge AI surpassed $1B in annual revenue via its specialist network; domain-expert platform Mercor reached a $10B valuation and reportedly crossed $2B in annualized gross revenue run rate by mid-2026 (both Surge and Mercor are private companies; revenue and valuation figures reflect public press reports rather than audited filings).

The cash is real, yet examining business-model quality shows **strong cashflow paired with fragile valuation dynamics**.

What vendors sell shifted first, from labor hours to **verifiable grading authority**. As detailed in our [Kimi K3 technical report analysis](/blog/kimi-k3-技术报告深读), preventing memorization requires strict separation between public tests and hidden verifiers alongside submission caps. Once contracts settle per verified trajectory, a single loophole in a verifier can render an entire expensive data asset useless.

Verifiers can break, and environments themselves depreciate rapidly. Great enterprise SaaS earns high multiples from near-zero marginal cost and strong renewals; RL environments act like perishable project consumables whose top-tier premium drops once frontier models saturate them. Half-life depends on buyer tier: packs saturated by frontier labs can still be sold cheaper to open-source or mid-tier labs, while Environment Hub and automated synthesis keep pushing generic task prices down. As noted in [Open Post-Training Frontiers 2026](/blog/open-post-training-frontiers-2026), university teams built RL suites that commanded strong prices in San Francisco, only to watch them rot without ongoing maintenance. Applying perpetual-software multiples to perpetually rebuilt project work eventually collides with depreciation.

Once an environment is built, unquoted security engineering and model-contamination risks appear. Our [DeepSeek V4.1 Flash analysis](/blog/deepseek-v41-flash-技术深度解析-把-kv-cache-压缩做到极致) documents real DSec sandbox failures where RL agents exploited system loopholes, leaked answers via package mirrors, and tampered with binaries, forcing eBPF isolation. Anthropic's November 2025 paper further showed that reward hacking in production RL induces broader emergent misaligned behaviors. A sloppy environment wastes budget and actively contaminates the model.

A deeper constraint sits in buyer concentration: global frontier training budgets sit with a tiny handful of labs, giving buyers immense leverage. Neutrality and custody become part of the product itself. After Meta invested in Scale AI, Google, OpenAI, and xAI scaled back work, pushing Scale toward defense and enterprise contracts; in early 2026, a reported security incident at Mercor likewise drew rapid client scrutiny. Pure data resale margins are squeezed by expert payouts, synthetic pipelines, and labs' incentive to build in-house, which is why Mercor partnered with SkyRL to open-source a 397B expert-trajectory recipe and Scale launched Scale Labs for evaluation and post-training research. Vendors moving into benchmarks and recipes confirm that reselling data alone cannot protect long-term pricing power. Researchers knocking on vendors' doors today is largely a timing window during supply mismatch; once task templates and automated verification stabilize, major labs have every incentive to bring the work inside.

Compress six years across LLMs, agents, and data markets into one grid, and the coordinates that decide whether an AI business makes durable money stay remarkably tight:

| Arena & Stage | Layer Getting Cheap | Where Profits & Costs Moved | Commercial Metric That Decides Viability |
|---|---|---|---|
| LLM Scaling & Inference | Pre-training weights, basic token price | Serving costs, post-training selection & grading, cloud & distribution | Qualified outcome cost including retries and human review |
| Digital Labor & Agents | Routine coding & generic app wrappers | Workflow integration, data governance, error liability, upkeep | First-pass resolution rate, escalation rate, and renewals |
| AI Data & RL Markets | Standard human labeling, generic Q&A packs | Expert ops, anti-cheat sandbox security, customer custody trust | Environment half-life, buyer concentration, resistance to in-housing |

Looking back across six years, from pre-training weights and API pricing to routine code, every technical wave has turned the previous layer's priciest input into a commodity. People rarely lose money by doubting technical progress; they lose money by seeing cost drop in one layer and assuming profits are already locked in.

When the next AI concept shows up, skip guessing how impressive the keynote sounds and look straight at four things: whether platform giants have both the incentive and the upside to give your layer away for free; how rights and liability stay balanced once pricing shifts from tools to outcomes; whether core assets rapidly lose value when the next model drops; and whether major customers will build it themselves once the playbook is clear.
