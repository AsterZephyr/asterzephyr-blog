Most LLM teams agree that data sets the ceiling of a model, yet concrete filtering and mixing decisions still split right down the middle. On the same web crawl, some teams discard more than 95% of raw tokens before touching a cluster, while others point to 2026 scaling experiments arguing that aggressive filtering hurts once compute gets large enough. At sign-off time, some teams skim a few dozen rows by eye and ship; others insist on training proxy models to rank candidate mixes first. Those conflicting habits usually end up grouped under a single vague label: **data taste**.

Recently, Datumwell founder Allen published an X essay titled [*What Is Good Data Taste?* (什么是好的数据 Taste?)](https://x.com/allen_datumwell/status/2123813175644782633?s=19), tying together more than a dozen influential papers from 2019 to 2026. Checking each paper and technical report against its primary source points to why these arguments rarely converge: **teams treat "good data" as a static property of text, whereas the actual value of the same corpus flips depending on compute budget, training stage, and verification setup.**

<Callout type="info" title="Reading scope and verification notes">
This article assumes familiarity with the basic roles of pre-training, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning (RL), without requiring prior reading of the cited papers. Allen's X essay is the starting point, while all experimental numbers, mechanisms, and author attributions below have been checked against the original papers and technical reports. Where common secondary summaries diverge from primary sources (such as Nemotron 3 Nano's stage boundaries, DataRater's meta-gradient objective, or FineWeb's deduplication regression), the text corrects them against the original publications.
</Callout>

Turning "data taste" into usable engineering judgment comes down to locating your project along four boundaries before modifying a pipeline: your compute-to-unique-token regime, your current training stage, whether cheap signals can replace manual spot-checking, and, once natural corpora run dry, whether an executable environment and verifier gate your synthetic data.

## Why Practitioners Still Feel Like Data Quality Is Guesswork

Before turning to algorithms and scaling laws, two empirical studies of real LLM teams explain why "taste" remains the default vocabulary in data engineering.

In the ASE 2024 study [*What Makes a High-Quality Training Dataset for Large Language Models: A Practitioners’ Perspective*](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3691620.3695061), researchers interviewed 13 experienced LLM professionals and then surveyed 219 practitioners across 23 countries. Out of 18 candidate attributes, respondents ranked **Reliability, Relevance, Accuracy, and Compliance** highest, placing completeness, balance, absence of duplicates, consistency, and timeliness at the bottom. Their top pain points fell into three buckets: no standardized processing pipeline, no clear stopping rule for when cleaning is good enough, and no reliable quantitative metric afterward.

That same year, Google Research published a CHI 2024 extended abstract, [*Understanding the Dataset Practitioners Behind Large Language Model Development*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16611), interviewing 10 internal practitioners across tooling, modeling, and evaluation. Even inside a top lab, dataset inspection split into two extremes: **eyeballing in spreadsheets** ("read the first 10 examples, and then maybe some in the middle") on one end, and custom Python notebooks on the other, with almost no shared tooling in between.

The paper flags the risk in that habit: **unstructured spot-checking amplifies confirmation bias**. When model outputs look wrong, engineers dig into the corpus with an explanation already in mind. They can almost always find a few messy rows that match their hunch, with no way to tell whether those samples are a real noise source, irrelevant edge cases, or rare long-tail signal.

Getting out of that intuition loop means dropping the blanket question "Is this corpus clean?" and examining the four boundaries that govern data value.

## Boundary 1: Compute and Data Regimes Decide Whether Filtering Helps or Hurts

Every year or two, the field re-argues how aggressively to filter web data. Putting the 2019 to 2026 results back inside their own experimental conditions shows that seemingly contradictory papers sit in two different regimes: **compute-constrained** versus **data-constrained**.

The baseline is DeepMind's Chinchilla study, [*Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556) (2022). Across 400+ runs spanning 70M to 16B parameters, the authors showed that under a fixed compute budget $$C$$, parameter count $$N$$ and training tokens $$D$$ should scale roughly together as $$C^{0.5}$$; a 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens beat the 280B Gopher trained on 300B tokens at comparable compute. That set the rule for modern pre-training: under fixed FLOPs, spending compute on higher-density tokens yields direct gains.

Early web-filtering pipelines followed directly from that rule. Meta's [CCNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00359) (2019) chained three steps over Common Crawl: paragraph deduplication, fastText language identification, and perplexity bucketing (head, middle, tail) using a Wikipedia-trained 5-gram KenLM. When raw crawls are full of broken encodings, machine-translated spam, and template boilerplate, and training budgets are nowhere near exhausting the web, rules and language-model filters rarely backfire.

At multi-trillion-token scale, the first surprise showed up. When Hugging Face built [FineWeb](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17557) (2024, 15T tokens from 96 Common Crawl snapshots) and ran systematic deduplication ablations, **global MinHash fuzzy deduplication across all 96 snapshots degraded downstream benchmark performance.**

Tracing the `2013-48` snapshot exposed why. Iterative global deduplication swept from the newest dump back to the oldest, discarding roughly 90% of early dumps and shrinking the whole corpus to 4T tokens. Training ablation models on the **retained 10%** (~31B tokens) versus an independently deduplicated slice of the **discarded 90%** (171B tokens) showed that **the kept 10% performed worse than the 90% thrown away**. Manual inspection gave the reason: evergreen, high-value pages recur across many crawl snapshots and got stripped out of older dumps, causing global deduplication to upsample one-off ads, keyword stuffing, and malformed pages. Switching to independent **per-dump MinHash deduplication** (retaining 20T tokens before heuristic filtering to 15T) brought scores back up to match RefinedWeb.

FineWeb did not reject filtering or deduplication in general (its FineWeb-Edu classifier later produced large gains). The problem was that **blunt cross-dump deletion silently shifted the surviving distribution**.

In May 2026, a Stanford team (Mohri, Duchi, and Hashimoto) looked at the opposite corner in [*A Bitter Lesson for Data Filtering*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19407) (NeurIPS 2026): the **high-compute, data-scarce regime**. Aggressive web filters discard almost everything: DCLM-Baseline keeps ~2.1% of raw Common Crawl, and RefinedWeb keeps ~13%. To study abundant compute over a fixed token pool at manageable cost, the authors randomly downscaled the 240T-token DCLM-Pool into fixed pools of 670M to 10B tokens and trained dense 15M to 7B Transformers comparing the unfiltered pool against five filters (stop-word, repetition, English-language, RefinedWeb, and DCLM-Baseline), measuring negative log-likelihood (NLL) on C4, FineWeb-Edu, and Cosmopedia.

As scale grew, the curves crossed. At 15M parameters or low compute, filtered datasets won easily. On the 670M fixed pool, once models reached ~330M+ parameters and trained longer, **completely unfiltered Common Crawl crossed over and beat all five filtered subsets**, becoming Pareto-optimal. The authors also ran a junk-injection test: mixing shuffled-word low-quality documents into the clean pool beat the clean pool alone on a 330M model after ~11B training tokens. That aligns with capacity arguments: with enough parameters and steps, models isolate noise in internal subspaces while still pulling residual word co-occurrence signal out of shuffled text.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not read 'A Bitter Lesson for Data Filtering' as 'delete your cleaning pipeline tomorrow'">
[arXiv:2605.19407](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19407) spells out three boundary conditions that block naive extrapolation:<br />
1. **The full-web crossover is far out**: scaling-law projections put the point where unfiltered 240T Common Crawl beats RefinedWeb at roughly $$10^{30}$$ FLOPs, well above current training budgets for almost all teams. When compute is tight, filtering and curation remain the primary lever.<br />
2. **Fixed static pool assumption**: the experiments freeze the raw pool without synthetic expansion. In industrial practice, teams increasingly rewrite, distill, or restructure noisy web text rather than training on it raw.<br />
3. **Dense pre-training NLL only**: the study excludes data curricula, annealing, post-training alignment, and adversarial misinformation that mimics clean style while injecting false facts.
</Callout>

Even inside pre-training, keeping more data does not mean mixing blindly. The [ATLAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22037) study by researchers at MIT, UW, Stanford, and Google (submitted October 2025, ICLR 2026; [Google Research blog post](https://www.research.google/blog/atlas-practical-scaling-laws-for-multilingual-models/) in January 2026) ran 774 experiments across 10M to 8B parameters and 400+ languages, establishing three quantitative laws. Cross-lingual transfer is strongly directional: measuring a $$38 \times 38 = 1,444$$ language-pair matrix showed that Spanish and Portuguese strongly aid Catalan, yet $$A \to B$$ gains do not imply symmetric $$B \to A$$ transfer. Positive transfer also offsets part of the curse of multilinguality: doubling supported languages from $$K$$ to $$2K$$ at constant per-language performance requires only **1.18× parameters**, **1.66× total data**, and **1.96× compute**, dropping per-language data share to **83%** of the original budget. For scarce languages such as Hindi or Swahili, however, repeating data past **1 epoch** triggers rapid diminishing returns and upward-bending loss tails.

When compute is scarce and raw pools are large, filtering is the most efficient way to cut redundancy; when the pool is fixed and compute is abundant, aggressive pruning hits a data wall early. In either regime, cross-lingual transfer direction and epoch limits need measurement rather than guesswork.

## Boundary 2: Pre-Training, Annealing, SFT, and RL Require Different Signals

Another common mix-up is applying pre-training habits to SFT, or treating mid-training (annealing and continued pre-training) as interchangeable with SFT. As covered in our earlier post on [Mid-Training](/blog/llm-mid-training-被tech-report遮掩的关键训练阶段/), major capability jumps often happen during late pre-training annealing and context extension rather than post-training. Looking at concrete recipes makes it clear that each stage optimizes a different target distribution.

NVIDIA's report for [Nemotron 3 Nano (30B-A3B)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.20848) (December 2025) and the [Nemotron open recipes](https://github.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/Nemotron) separate the data stages clearly. One frequent secondary-summary slip is worth correcting upfront: **the 23.5T and 1.5T figures are Phase 1 and Phase 2 of 25T total pre-training, not 1.5T of SFT**. The four stages split as follows:

1. **Pre-training Phase 1 (23.5T tokens)**: the first 94% of pre-training, covering 15 categories (web, code, math, multilingual, and synthetic data) with a focus on breadth and diversity.
2. **Pre-training Phase 2 / Annealing (1.5T tokens)**: switches to curated high-quality sources, raising the weight on reasoning and factual accuracy.
3. **Long-Context Continued Pre-training (121B tokens)**: extends context to 1M tokens using NoPE. Because training solely on `512k` sequences caused slight regressions on short-context benchmarks, NVIDIA mixed **`512k` and `4k` sequences** (20% long-document QA, 1% synthetic retrieval up to 256k tokens, and 79% downsampled Phase 2 data). The synthetic long-context tasks spread key evidence across distant spans and multiple documents with distractors, while keeping answers automatically verifiable.
4. **SFT (~18M samples) and Reasoning Control**: once in SFT, the model moves beyond general next-token modeling $$p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$$ to multi-turn tool use, state-action switching, and controllable reasoning protocols. Section 3.1.5 of the Nemotron 3 Nano report gives a concrete construction: reasoning traces are stripped from a random **10%** of SFT samples so the model answers directly when thinking is disabled, and reasoning traces are randomly truncated to varied token budgets in another **3%** before continuing into the original final answer. At serving time, once a request hits the user's thinking budget, the engine injects `</think>` and the model converges from the partial trace.

Inference-time budget control depends on writing that behavioral protocol into the SFT distribution ahead of time; hard truncation at serving time alone does not work reliably. A similar stage split appears in [DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948) (January 2025, updated January 2026): pure RL in R1-Zero produced emergent reflection and long reasoning, yet suffered poor readability and language mixing. R1 added curated cold-start SFT first, combined accuracy, format, and language-consistency rewards in RL, and fed rejection-sampled trajectories back into SFT to lift capability and usability together.

Even inside SFT **instruction data selection**, growing the candidate pool breaks methods that worked at small scale. [DEITA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15685) (ICLR 2024; led by HKUST with co-authors from ShanghaiTech, BUPT, Meituan, and Alibaba, often misattributed to a single company) used LLM scoring for **Complexity × Quality × Diversity**, letting 6K selected samples match or beat 10× larger open alignment datasets on 7B/13B models.

In 2025, Ai2 and UW scaled instruction selection from typical 100k to 200k pools up to **5.8M candidates** (selecting up to **2.5M samples**) in [*Large-Scale Data Selection for Instruction Tuning*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01807). **Several established small-pool selectors (Perplexity, IFD, and multiple external embedding models) degraded at million-sample scale and lost to random sampling.** Larger pools give extreme outliers far more ways to exploit an external scorer's quirks.

Their proposed method, **RDS+**, replaces external judges with **the target base model's own last-layer hidden states**. Because causal attention gathers more sequence context in later tokens, RDS+ applies position-weighted mean pooling over the full instruction and response sequence:

```math
e = \sum_{i=1}^{L} w_i h_i, \qquad w_i = \frac{i}{\sum_{j=1}^{L} j}
```

where $$h_i$$ is the last-layer hidden state of token $$i$$ and $$L$$ is sequence length. RDS+ computes cosine similarity against validation queries and samples candidates via deduplicated multi-task round-robin selection. Scaling the pool from 200k to 5.8M steadily improved RDS+, beating the human-curated Tülu 2 and Tülu 3 SFT mixes. Even counting feature-extraction FLOPs, RDS+ becomes more compute-efficient than random sampling past roughly 326k selected samples, and hidden states from smaller or cross-family models (1.5B to 70B) still transfer well.

## Boundary 3: Measuring Data Value Without Burning a Full Pre-Training Run

No team can afford to pre-train a flagship model from scratch for every mixture tweak. Without cheap, reproducible early signals, teams slide back to skimming 10 rows in a spreadsheet.

Three 2025 papers map out three distinct low-cost valuation routes: **corpus-level A/B ranking**, **document-level semantic tagging**, and **end-to-end meta-learned valuation**.

### 1. DataDecide: Corpus-Level Ranking at 0.01% Compute via Continuous Probabilities

In [DataDecide](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11393) (ICML 2025), researchers at Ai2, UW, and UPenn built a benchmark of 1,050 pretrained models across 25 corpora, 14 model sizes (4M to 1B, up to 100B tokens), and 3 random seeds to test whether small experiments predict which corpus wins at larger scale.

Two findings directly affect how pre-training ablations should be run. First, **single-scale small-model ranking matches complex scaling-law extrapolations**: ranking candidate corpora with a **150M** model predicts pairwise winners at **1B (100B tokens)** with roughly **80% accuracy**, and none of the 8 tested scaling-law baselines beat simple single-scale ranking on the compute-accuracy frontier. Second, **replace discrete accuracy with continuous probability proxies**: tiny models score near zero on generative coding tasks like MBPP and HumanEval, making pass@1 no better than a coin flip. Switching to character-length-normalized continuous probabilities, namely `Correct Prob` (probability assigned to the correct continuation) or `Total Prob` (total probability mass across candidate answers), pushes MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, MBPP, and HumanEval above **80% corpus-ranking accuracy at just 0.01% of target compute**. Even before a small model can solve a task, its probability mass shows whether a corpus supplies the right domain exposure.

DataDecide also documents its blind spot: **on standard math benchmarks, small-model predictions stayed near chance regardless of continuous proxy**. In addition, it ranks whole corpora rather than scoring individual documents.

### 2. DataMan: Distilling a Static Rubric Into a 1.5B Semantic Classifier

When the task is document-level filtering and domain mixing across hundreds of billions of tokens, [DataMan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19363) (ICLR 2025, Zhejiang University and Alibaba's Qwen Team) offers a practical industrial route.

Using "reverse thinking," the authors asked GPT-4-turbo to analyze why documents fell into the extreme top and bottom perplexity tails, deriving **13 individual quality criteria** (Accuracy, Coherence, Language Consistency, Semantic Density, Knowledge Novelty, Topic Focus, Creativity, Professionalism, Style Consistency, Grammatical Diversity, Structural Standardization, Originality, Sensitivity), plus **1 composite Overall Score** and **15 application domains**. GPT-4-turbo then labeled ~356K documents (>95% agreement with human experts) to fine-tune a **Qwen2-1.5B** scorer, which tagged 447B tokens of SlimPajama offline. Pre-training a 1.3B model on 30B top-scored (score 5) tokens beat QuRating and outperformed a uniform baseline trained on 50% more data.

This route is cheap, interpretable, and easy to tune across a domain-by-quality matrix. Its limitation is that it **freezes a teacher model's static judgment**, inheriting teacher biases without tracking what gradient signal the target model lacks mid-run.

### 3. DataRater: Training a Sample Scorer From Validation Loss via Meta-Gradients

To avoid hard-coding document quality rules with humans or GPT-4, Google DeepMind's [DataRater](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17895) (NeurIPS 2025) offers an end-to-end bi-level optimization approach.

DataRater is often misdescribed as using raw training loss as a label to select high-gradient samples, which would readily pick corrupted noise. Instead, it uses **bi-level optimization**: a 50M-parameter non-causal Transformer $$\phi_\eta$$ outputs normalized scalar weights for samples inside a micro-batch; an inner loop of eight 400M language models takes $$T=2$$ weighted update steps; an outer loop evaluates the updated models on a **disjoint held-out validation set** and backpropagates exact second-order **meta-gradients** through the unrolled inner updates (using `MixFlow-MG` mixed-mode differentiation) to update $$\eta$$.

DataRater rewards samples **whose gradient steps reduce held-out validation loss**. After meta-training, the 50M rater performs batch-level top-K filtering. Optimal discard fractions tuned on a 50M probe (10% on C4, 50% on C4/noclean, 75% on The Pile) held steady across 50M, 150M, 400M, and 1B target models, reaching up to a **46.6% net compute (FLOPs) gain** on noisier corpora.

Its limits are clear too: second-order meta-training adds far more system complexity and overhead than DataMan, and public validation ends at 1B target models. Whether a policy learned on 400M inner models transfers across two more orders of magnitude to 100B+ MoE runs remains untested in public literature.

In production, mature teams tend to **layer these three tools**: distilled rubric taggers like DataMan for trillion-token indexing and domain cataloging, DataDecide continuous-probability proxies as corpus-level A/B gates, and meta-learned valuation only on noisy, high-value pools where the extra overhead pays off.

## Boundary 4: Once Natural Corpora Run Dry, Environments and Verifiers Gate Synthetic Data

For long-horizon agents (editing repositories, calling tools, navigating browsers, reading errors, rolling back state) and multimodal search, clean, complete public trajectories with reliable feedback barely exist on the web. Filtering hits the ceiling of what already exists, shifting data engineering toward **synthetic generation**.

One dead-end shortcut should be ruled out first: extracting hidden reasoning traces from proprietary APIs. In August 2026, [*Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09867) documented a concrete system flaw across Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google: providers returned encrypted reasoning blocks to clients for multi-turn pass-through, but those blocks were interchangeable across sessions and models within the same provider. An attacker could feed a frontier model's encrypted block into a weaker, less restricted model from the same vendor to force verbatim plaintext decoding. The authors reproduced the flaw across all three providers and decoded 315,320 publicly scraped blocks, uncovering 367 PII items and 182 credentials. Such bugs get patched quickly and cross legal and security lines; durable data capability cannot depend on scraping closed traces.

Three mid-2026 system papers show what scalable, verifiable synthetic engines look like. All three start from real material and hand quality control to executable environments or isolated verifiers.

### 1. Kimi K3: Hierarchical Knowledge Graphs for Coverage, Isolated Verifiers Against Hacking

In [*Kimi K3: Open Frontier Intelligence*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24653) (July to August 2026), synthesis spans both ends of training.

On the pre-training side (§3.1), the pipeline scales style- and perspective-diverse **rephrasing** of knowledge and math corpora with chunk-wise fidelity checks against source text, alongside **programmatic multimodal data** binding code directly to rendered SVGs, 3D assets, and webpages.

On the post-training agent side (§4.2), to keep synthetic tasks from collapsing into repetitive templates, the team built a self-evolving **hierarchical Knowledge Graph (DAG; the report calls it a Knowledge Graph rather than a Task Graph)**. Starting from coarse seed concepts, agents recursively search the web for sub-concepts, query existing nodes to reuse equivalents before inserting duplicates, and direct edges strictly from coarse to atomic concepts. Task synthesis samples node combinations across granularities, retrieves real papers, blogs, and repositories using ancestor context, and generates tasks across composable white-box harnesses mimicking different coding agent scaffolds.

Quality gating centers on autonomous execution tasks (§4.2.6 AET): tasks omit reference trajectories and wrap execution in **public diagnostic verifiers, hidden held-out verifiers, and submission-budget penalties** to block brute-force probing and reward hacking.

### 2. Qwen-AgentWorld: Train a Language World Model on Real Traces, Then Scale Controlled Simulation

Live sandboxes, browsers, and external APIs are slow, expensive, and fragile, and production edge cases (intermittent API faults, truncated pagination, partial batch failures) rarely trigger on demand. In June 2026, [*Qwen-AgentWorld: Language World Models for General Agents*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24597) took a different route: train the environment itself as a model.

The Qwen team collected **10M+ real environment interaction trajectories** across 7 domains (MCP, Search, Terminal, SWE, Android, Web, OS) and trained 35B-A3B and 397B-A17B Language World Models (via CPT → SFT → RL) to generate next observations $$p(o_{t+1} \mid o_{\le t}, a_{\le t})$$ for large-scale **Simulation RL (Sim RL)**.

On the out-of-distribution OpenClaw platform, distilling real traces into reusable seed scenarios and varying both states and task goals produced **4,000 simulated environments**, lifting zero-shot scores by **+4.3 on Claw-Eval** and **+7.1 on QwenClawBench**. On MCP tool use, injecting controllable perturbations (API errors, pagination, partial returns) lifted average **MCPMark** scores from 21.5 (SFT) to **33.8 (+12.3)**, well above uncontrolled simulation (24.6). On search, the team constructed **1,000 self-consistent fictional relational-database worlds** (300 to 500 structured facts each, SQL-extracted ground truth, reverse-generated queries, and strict no-leakage partial snippets), boosting **WideSearch** by **+16.3** and **outperforming live real-environment RL (50.3% vs. 45.6%)**.

The paper also pins down the main bottleneck of simulated synthesis: **Initial State Conditioning**. Simulation fidelity depends heavily on feeding the world model detailed seed states (file systems, account configs, database schemas); skimping with rough prompts causes synthetic trajectories to drift quickly.

### 3. WeAgent-MMSearch: Backward Multi-Hop Expansion From Pixel-Dependent Endpoints

Multimodal search synthesis has a common failure mode: questions or URLs leak the answer in text, letting models guess correctly without inspecting the image. In August 2026, Weixin AI (Tencent) and Sun Yat-sen University published [*WeAgent-MMSearch*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28062) with a four-stage backward pipeline:

1. **Lock a visual endpoint ($$n_d$$)**: gather target images and visual facts from January to July 2026 web events and Wikipedia, accepting a seed only if the image is reachable inside `WeAgent-Harness` and the answer strictly requires **visible pixels**, unavailable in titles, URLs, or captions.
2. **Expand backward ($$n_0 \to \dots \to n_{d-1} \to n_d$$)**: instead of guessing multi-hop prompts forward, work backward $$d \in \{2, 3, 4\}$$ hops. At each step, give the expert agent only entity $$n_i$$ and search the web to write a bridge question whose answer resolves uniquely to $$n_i$$, producing $$n_{i-1}$$.
3. **Audit bridge uniqueness and leakage**: reject chains with missing images, ambiguous bridges, or metadata leakage.
4. **Filter SFT and RL separately**: SFT keeps only verified-correct, complete expert trajectories run inside the same persistent-image harness (3.3K in-house + 56.2K normalized open = 59.5K). Before RL, the SFT model samples **8 rollouts** per prompt and **drops both 8/8 all-correct prompts (no headroom) and 0/8 all-wrong prompts (no within-group reward contrast)**, retaining 4.9K in-house prompts.

All three systems share three design choices: ground coverage and initial states in real material to avoid mode collapse, close text shortcuts and leakage structurally to prevent fake difficulty, and delegate quality gating to isolated verifiers or rollout pass-rate contrast rather than model self-scoring.

## Five Questions to Answer Before Changing a Data Pipeline

Before modifying any data pipeline, answering five evidence-backed questions is far more dependable than eyeballing rows or copying a single trending paper:

<Steps>
  <StepItem title="Is the bottleneck compute or unique tokens?">
    When compute is tight, quality filtering, domain mixing, and per-dump deduplication (FineWeb, DataMan) remain the highest-leverage tools. Near multi-epoch limits on a fixed pool, watch for hidden distribution shifts from over-filtering and global deduplication (Bitter Lesson, ATLAS's 1-epoch bend).
  </StepItem>
  <StepItem title="Should this data build knowledge, behavioral protocols, or policy search boundaries?">
    Do not apply one yardstick across pre-training, annealing, SFT, and RL. Following Nemotron 3 Nano's pattern (writing thinking toggles into 10% and truncated budgets into 3% of SFT, and mixing `512k`/`4k` into long-context continuation), define the target behavioral protocol first and derive the data format from it.
  </StepItem>
  <StepItem title="Will the selector get gamed when the pool grows 10×?">
    External scorers that work well on 100k pools often amplify judge bias at million-sample scale (Ivison et al.). Benchmark against a random baseline at target pool size, and consider base-model hidden states (RDS+) or explicit diversity constraints.
  </StepItem>
  <StepItem title="Are decisions based on skimming 10 rows or running a cheap proxy?">
    Before spending cluster runs, set up corpus-level small-model proxies (using DataDecide's continuous probabilities rather than early discrete accuracy) alongside versioned data diffs, so spreadsheets do not turn into confirmation-bias tools.
  </StepItem>
  <StepItem title="Who verifies synthetic samples: LLM self-scoring or an executable environment?">
    For agents and long-horizon reasoning, the hard part is closing a verifiable feedback loop (such as Kimi K3's knowledge graph and isolated verifiers, Qwen-AgentWorld's detailed seed states, or WeAgent-MMSearch's backward visual chains), rather than generating another 100K plausible-looking traces.
  </StepItem>
</Steps>

<Callout type="note" title="Three open questions still lacking large-scale evidence">
In the public literature, three areas still lack large-scale empirical answers:<br />
1. **100B+ transfer of meta-learned valuation**: DataRater establishes stability from 400M inner models to 1B target models, but whether learned discard ratios and sample rankings hold across two more orders of magnitude on frontier MoE models has no public verification yet.<br />
2. **Small-scale proxies for math and deep reasoning**: DataDecide's continuous probabilities predict general knowledge and coding corpora at 0.01% compute, yet standard math benchmarks stayed near chance, leaving cheap pre-training proxies for reasoning-heavy data unsolved.<br />
3. **Raw web tails vs. verifiable synthetic compute**: *A Bitter Lesson for Data Filtering* assumes a fixed static web pool, while frontier labs put growing compute into rephrasing, graph synthesis, and world-model simulation. There is no unified public scaling law yet for splitting compute between raw web tails and verifiable synthesis.
</Callout>
