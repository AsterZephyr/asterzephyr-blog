Almost every LLM team agrees that data sets the ceiling, yet the moment engineers write cleaning scripts or set mixing ratios, their practices point in opposite directions. Faced with the same web crawl, one team discards over 95% of raw tokens before touching a cluster, while another cites 2026 experiments arguing that aggressive filtering hurts once compute scales. At sign-off, one camp eyeballs a few dozen rows in a spreadsheet and ships, while the other refuses to move without small proxy runs.

In industry shorthand, these clashing instincts are lumped together as **Data Taste**. Recently, Datumwell founder Allen published an X essay, [*What Is Good Data Taste?*](https://x.com/allen_datumwell/status/2123813175644782633?s=19), threading together over a dozen milestone papers from 2019 to 2026. Auditing each primary paper, technical report, and experimental premise reveals why teams keep talking past one another: **the field treats "good data" as a static property of text, whereas the exact same corpus flips from asset to liability once you change the compute regime, training stage, or verification loop.**

Before modifying any data pipeline, what actually works is not mysticism, but locking where you sit along four concrete boundaries: your compute-to-unique-token regime, your current training stage, whether you have cheap proxy signals to replace manual eyeballing, and whether verifiable environments can gate synthetic data once natural corpora run dry.

Wherever popular community summaries diverge from the primary source (such as Nemotron 3 Nano’s stage split, DataRater’s meta-gradient objective, or FineWeb’s deduplication failure mode), the text corrects them directly.

## Why Practitioners Still Feel Like "Data Taste" Is Folklore

Before looking at algorithms and scaling laws, two empirical studies of real engineering teams explain why data judgment still feels like tribal lore.

An ASE 2024 empirical study, [*What Makes a High-Quality Training Dataset for Large Language Models: A Practitioners’ Perspective*](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3691620.3695061), interviewed 13 senior LLM practitioners and surveyed 219 engineers across 23 countries. Out of 18 candidate attributes, practitioners ranked **Reliability, Relevance, Accuracy, and Compliance** at the top, placing completeness, balance, zero duplication, consistency, and freshness at the bottom.

Their biggest frustrations boiled down to three gaps: no standardized processing pipeline, no clear stopping rule for when data is "clean enough," and no trustworthy quantitative sign-off metrics once cleaning finishes.

In the same year, Google Research’s CHI 2024 extended abstract, [*Understanding the Dataset Practitioners Behind Large Language Model Development*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16611), interviewed 10 internal engineers spanning tooling, modeling, and evaluation. Even inside a frontier lab, data inspection split into two extremes: opening a spreadsheet to **eyeball** the first 10 rows and scroll randomly through the middle, or writing bespoke, throwaway Python notebooks—with almost no shared tooling in between.

The paper pinpoints the hidden hazard of ad-hoc eyeballing: **it systematically amplifies confirmation bias**. When a model behaves poorly, engineers browse the corpus with a hunch already in mind, easily pull out a few ugly rows to confirm that hunch, and still have no way of knowing whether those rows represent real noise, negligible dust, or rare high-value tail information.

Escaping this intuition-driven patch loop starts with dropping the blanket question "is this dataset clean?" and inspecting the four boundaries that actually govern data value.

## Boundary 1: Compute and Data Regimes Decide Whether Filtering Helps or Hurts

Every year or two, the community re-litigates how aggressively web crawls should be filtered. Put the 2019–2026 experiments back inside their original constraints, and papers that look contradictory actually sit in two different regimes: **compute-bound** versus **data-bound**.

### From Chinchilla to FineWeb: Why Cleaning Can Backfire Even Under Tight Compute

Modern pre-training begins with DeepMind’s Chinchilla paper, [*Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556) (2022). Across more than 400 runs spanning 70M to 16B parameters, the authors showed that for a fixed compute budget $$C$$, parameter count $$N$$ and training tokens $$D$$ should scale roughly together as $$C^{0.5}$$. A 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens (Chinchilla) outperformed 280B Gopher trained on 300B tokens at comparable compute. That set an intuitive baseline: when FLOPs are scarce, feeding denser, cleaner tokens pays off immediately.

Early web pipelines followed that logic straight down the line. Meta’s [CCNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00359) (2019) chained three steps on Common Crawl: paragraph deduplication, fastText language ID, and a 5-gram KenLM trained on Wikipedia to score perplexity (lower scores mean text closer to clean reference prose), splitting documents into head, middle, and tail tiers. Raw web dumps back then were flooded with mojibake, machine translation, and boilerplate, while training budgets were nowhere near exhausting unique tokens—so rule-based and language-model filters were almost risk-free wins.

Once corpora pushed past trillions of tokens, the first surprise hit. When Hugging Face released [FineWeb](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17557) (2024)—a 15T-token corpus built from 96 Common Crawl snapshots—systematic ablations revealed an unexpected drop: **global MinHash fuzzy deduplication across all 96 dumps significantly degraded downstream benchmarks.**

Tracing the `2013-48` snapshot exposed why. Iterative global deduplication worked backward from newest to oldest snapshots, stripping roughly 90% of early dumps and compressing the entire corpus down to 4T tokens. When the authors trained ablation models comparing the kept 10% (~31B tokens) against an independently deduplicated version of the discarded 90% (171B tokens), **the kept 10% scored worse than the discarded 90%.**

Manual inspection settled the mystery: evergreen high-value pages (reference articles, documentation, strong blogs) recur across dozens of snapshots, so global dedup wiped them from older dumps, while one-off ad pages, SEO keyword spam, and broken crawls survived and got upsampled. Switching to **per-dump independent MinHash deduplication** (yielding 20T tokens, then 15T after heuristic filters) recovered the lost ground and overtook RefinedWeb.

FineWeb did not refute filtering or deduplication—the follow-up FineWeb-Edu classifier delivered large gains. Its real lesson is sharper: **blunt cross-dump pruning silently distorts the distribution of whatever remains.**

### The Bitter Lesson: When Fixed Pools Meet High Compute, Filtering Reverses

In May 2026, a Stanford team (Mohri, Duchi, and Hashimoto) shifted focus to the opposite corner in [*A Bitter Lesson for Data Filtering*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19407) (NeurIPS 2026): the **high-compute, data-scarce** regime.

Mainstream filters cut deep—DCLM-Baseline keeps only ~2.1% of raw Common Crawl, and RefinedWeb keeps ~13%. To study abundant compute meeting a fixed pool of unique tokens under tractable budgets, the authors subsampled the 240T-token DCLM-Pool into fixed pools of 670M to 10B tokens and trained 15M to 7B dense Transformers on unfiltered data versus five filtered subsets (stop-words, dedup, English filter, RefinedWeb, DCLM-Baseline), measuring negative log-likelihood (NLL, lower is better) on C4, FineWeb-Edu, and Cosmopedia.

As scale grew, the curves crossed. On 15M models or low-compute budgets, heavy filtering won clearly. On the fixed 670M pool, once models reached ~330M parameters and trained longer, **the completely unfiltered raw pool overtook all five filtered subsets** to become Pareto-optimal.

Even more striking was a synthetic-noise injection test: deliberately mixing word-shuffled low-quality text into a clean pool caused a 330M model trained to ~11B tokens to beat the uncontaminated clean pool. The capacity explanation holds up cleanly: with enough parameters and steps, a model can wall off structural noise inside internal subspaces while still extracting residual co-occurrence statistics from corrupted prose.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do Not Misread This Paper as a Reason to Delete Cleaning Pipelines Tomorrow">
[arXiv:2605.19407](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19407) explicitly documents three assumptions; ignoring them leads straight to bad engineering decisions:<br />
1. **A very distant crossover**: Extrapolating to the full 240T Common Crawl pool puts the unfiltered-over-RefinedWeb crossover near $$10^{30}$$ FLOPs, far above almost any team’s budget today. Inside the compute-bound regime, filtering remains the #1 lever.<br />
2. **Static fixed-pool assumption**: The experiments forbid synthetic expansion. In practice, frontier labs increasingly put extra compute into faithful rephrasing or structured rewriting of noisy web text rather than training on raw dumps untouched.<br />
3. **Dense pre-training NLL only**: The study excludes annealing, curricula, and post-training alignment, and does not cover adversarial poisoning where text reads cleanly while injecting deliberate falsehoods.
</Callout>

### ATLAS: Asymmetric Cross-Lingual Transfer and the 1-Epoch Red Line

Even inside pre-training, keeping more data never means mixing blindly. [ATLAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22037) (MIT, UW, Stanford, and Google; submitted Oct 2025, ICLR 2026; [Google Research blog walkthrough](https://www.research.google/blog/atlas-practical-scaling-laws-for-multilingual-models/) Jan 2026) ran 774 experiments across 10M to 8B parameters and 400+ languages, establishing three quantitative laws:

- **Cross-lingual transfer is directional**: Measuring a $$38 \times 38 = 1,444$$ language transfer matrix showed that Spanish and Portuguese strongly boost Catalan, yet $$A \to B$$ transfer does not imply symmetric $$B \to A$$ benefit.
- **Positive transfer softens the "curse of multilinguality"**: Doubling supported languages from $$K$$ to $$2K$$ at constant per-language performance requires only **1.18×** parameters, **1.66×** total data, and **1.96×** compute—reducing data needed per language to **83%**.
- **Low-resource languages hit a 1-epoch cliff**: For scarce languages such as Hindi or Swahili, repeating beyond **1 epoch** causes marginal returns to collapse and validation loss to bend upward.

When compute is tight and pools are vast, filtering is the cheapest way to strip redundancy; when pools are fixed and compute is plentiful, aggressive hard cuts manufacture a data wall early. On either side, cross-lingual direction and epoch caps must be measured rather than guessed.

## Boundary 2: Pre-Training, Annealing, SFT, and RL Optimize Different Data Signals

Another common mistake is bringing pre-training habits into SFT, or collapsing Mid-training (annealing and continued pre-training) and SFT into one bucket. As unpacked earlier in [our Mid-Training deep dive](/blog/llm-mid-training-被tech-report遮掩的关键训练阶段/), major capability jumps often happen during late pre-training annealing and context extension rather than post-training. Real training recipes show why: each stage optimizes a fundamentally different distribution.

### Nemotron 3 Nano and DeepSeek-R1: Four Distinct Stage Contracts

NVIDIA’s [Nemotron 3 Nano (30B-A3B)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.20848) (Dec 2025) and its open [Nemotron recipe repository](https://github.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/Nemotron) draw clean stage lines. One viral misreading is worth fixing upfront: **the 23.5T and 1.5T figures are Phase 1 and Phase 2 of 25T total pre-training, not 1.5T of SFT.** The four stages divide cleanly:

1. **Pre-training Phase 1 (23.5T tokens)**: The first 94% of pre-training, spanning 15 broad categories (web, code, math, multilingual, synthetic) to maximize coverage and diversity.
2. **Pre-training Phase 2 / Annealing (1.5T tokens)**: Shifts sharply toward curated high-precision corpora, upweighting reasoning and factual accuracy.
3. **Long-Context Continued Pre-training (121B tokens)**: Uses NoPE (removing explicit positional encodings to ease length extrapolation) to push context to 1M tokens. Because training solely on `512k` sequences caused short-context benchmarks to slip, NVIDIA deliberately **mixed `512k` and `4k` sequences**: 20% long-document QA, 1% synthetic retrieval up to 256k, and 79% downsampled Phase 2 data. Those synthetic tasks scatter key evidence across multiple documents with distractors while preserving automatically verifiable answers.
4. **SFT (~18M samples) & Reasoning Control**: Here the model stops merely modeling generic $$p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$$ and learns multi-turn tool use, state transitions, and controllable reasoning protocols.

Section 3.1.5 of Nemotron 3 Nano reveals a remarkably pragmatic trick: strip the thinking chain from a random **10%** of SFT examples so the model learns to answer directly when reasoning mode is off; then truncate the thinking chain of another **3%** at varying token budgets and stitch the original final answer right after. At serving time, once a user’s thinking cap is hit, the server injects `</think>` and the model smoothly wraps up from partial thoughts.

Budget-controlled reasoning works because that behavioral contract was written into the SFT distribution upfront; brute-force truncation at inference time alone fails. The same stage complementarity shows up in [DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948) (Jan 2025 initial release, Jan 2026 revision): pure-RL R1-Zero spontaneously developed reflection and long reasoning, but suffered from language mixing and poor readability. R1 stabilized formatting with cold-start SFT first, combined accuracy, format, and language-consistency rewards in RL, and fed rejection-sampled traces back into SFT to lift both capability and usability together.

### DEITA to RDS+: Why External SFT Scorers Lose to Random at Million Scale

Even within SFT **instruction data selection**, scaling the candidate pool by an order of magnitude breaks methods that shine on small pools. [DEITA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15685) (ICLR 2024; led by HKUST with ShanghaiTech, BUPT, Meituan, and Alibaba researchers—often misattributed to a single company) scored **Complexity × Quality × Diversity** using LLMs and matched or beat 10× larger open alignment sets on 7B/13B models with just 6K curated examples.

In 2025, Ai2 and UW scaled the candidate pool from the usual 100k–200k up to **5.8 million examples** (selecting up to 2.5M) in [*Large-Scale Data Selection for Instruction Tuning*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01807), delivering a sobering result: **classic small-pool winners (Perplexity, Instruction-Following Difficulty / IFD, and several external embedding selectors) degraded at million scale and even lost to random sampling.** Larger pools contain more extreme outliers that easily exploit blind spots in external judges.

Their solution, **RDS+**, replaces external judges with **the final-layer hidden states of the target base model itself**. Running a forward pass over each instruction-response pair, it accounts for causal masking—where later tokens accumulate fuller sequence context—via linear position-weighted mean pooling:

```math
e = \sum_{i=1}^{L} w_i h_i, \qquad w_i = \frac{i}{\sum_{j=1}^{L} j}
```

Here $$h_i$$ is the final-layer hidden state of token $$i$$ and $$L$$ is sequence length. A 4-token toy sequence makes the weighting obvious in seconds: the denominator is $$1+2+3+4=10$$, so the four token weights are **0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4**—giving the final token, which attends over the whole prompt-response pair, 4× the weight of the first token. RDS+ then ranks candidates by cosine similarity against validation queries and samples round-robin across tasks for diversity.

As the pool grew from 200k to 5.8M, RDS+ gains kept expanding, beating manually tuned Tülu 2 and Tülu 3 SFT mixes. Even counting the forward-pass compute required to extract hidden states, RDS+ beats random sampling in net compute efficiency once selection passes ~326k examples, and representations extracted from 1.5B–70B cross-family models still transfer well.

## Boundary 3: Measuring Data Value Cheaply Before Burning a Full Pre-Training Run

No team can afford to re-train a 70B or trillion-parameter run for every data tweak. Without cheap, reproducible pre-flight signals, organizations inevitably slide back into the "eyeball 10 rows in a spreadsheet" habit documented by Google Research.

Three 2025 papers map out three complementary low-cost valuation tracks: **corpus-level A/B ranking**, **document-level semantic tagging**, and **end-to-end meta-learned valuation**.

### 1. DataDecide: Corpus-Level Ranking With Continuous Probabilities at 0.01% Compute

In [DataDecide](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11393) (ICML 2025), Ai2 and collaborators built a massive controlled testbed: 1,050 models spanning 4M to 1B parameters (14 sizes, up to 100B tokens, 3 seeds) across 25 pre-training corpora with distinct sources, deduplication, and filters, testing whether small experiments predict large-scale winners.

Two findings directly reshape pre-training ablation workflows:

- **Direct single-scale ranking ties complex scaling-law extrapolations**: Ranking corpus pairs directly with 150M models already predicts 1B (100B-token) winners with ~**80%** average accuracy; none of the 8 scaling-law extrapolation baselines beat simple direct ranking on the compute-accuracy frontier.
- **Stop watching discrete Accuracy early; switch to continuous probability proxies**: Small models score near-zero pass@1 (single-sample solve rate) on coding suites like MBPP and HumanEval, turning discrete accuracy into a coin flip. Switching to **character-normalized continuous probability** on the correct completion (`Correct Prob`) or total probability across options (`Total Prob`) pushes ranking accuracy above **80%** across MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, MBPP, and HumanEval at **just 0.01% of target compute**.

The intuition is simple: long before a small model can assemble a full passing program, probability mass shifting slightly toward the right continuation already reveals whether a corpus carries the right domain signal.

DataDecide is candid about its blind spot: **on standard math benchmarks, small-model predictions stay near random regardless of which continuous metric you pick.** It also ranks whole corpora A vs. B rather than scoring individual documents.

### 2. DataMan: Distilling Static LLM Judgment Into a 1.5B Semantic Scorer

When the task is document-level filtering and domain cataloging across trillions of tokens, [DataMan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19363) (Zhejiang University & Qwen, ICLR 2025) offers a practical industrial pipeline.

The team first used "reverse thinking" with GPT-4-turbo to diagnose why extreme high- and low-perplexity documents scored as they did, distilling **13 independent quality dimensions** (accuracy, coherence, language consistency, semantic density, knowledge novelty, topic focus, creativity, professionalism, style consistency, grammatical diversity, structural standardization, originality, sensitivity), plus **1 Overall Score** and **15 application domains**.

Annotating ~356k documents with GPT-4-turbo (>95% agreement with human experts), they fine-tuned a lightweight **Qwen2-1.5B** scorer to tag all 447B tokens of SlimPajama offline. Training a 1.3B model on just 30B top-rated (score 5) tokens beat QuRating and outperformed uniform sampling trained on 50% more tokens.

This route is cheap, transparent, and easy to rebalance across a domain × quality matrix. Its trade-off is equally clear: **it freezes the teacher model’s static taste**, inheriting teacher style biases and remaining blind to what gradients the target model actually needs mid-training.

### 3. DataRater: Bi-Level Meta-Gradients Driven Directly by Held-Out Validation Loss

To avoid hand-coding or teacher-prompting quality rules altogether, Google DeepMind’s [DataRater](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17895) (NeurIPS 2025) frames data valuation as end-to-end bi-level optimization.

Online summaries often mischaracterize DataRater as "picking high-loss training examples," which would immediately select gibberish and corrupted scrapes. In reality, it runs **bi-level optimization**:

A 50M-parameter non-causal Transformer scorer $$\phi_\eta$$ assigns normalized weights to samples in a training mini-batch. In the inner loop, a population of eight 400M language models takes $$T=2$$ weighted gradient steps. In the outer loop, those updated models are evaluated on a **disjoint held-out validation set**, and `MixFlow-MG` mixed-mode differentiation backpropagates **second-order meta-gradients** from validation loss directly into the scorer weights $$\eta$$.

A two-sample contrast makes the mechanism click in seconds: **Sample A is garbled HTML** with huge training loss; taking two inner steps on it pushes validation loss *up*, so the outer meta-gradient drives its weight down. **Sample B is a clean code derivation**; taking two steps on it pushes validation loss *down*, so the scorer learns to upweight it. DataRater never rewards raw difficulty—it rewards **samples that lower target validation loss after training**.

Once meta-trained, the 50M scorer simply performs Top-K batch pruning. Optimal discard rates discovered at 50M (10% on C4, 50% on C4/noclean, 75% on The Pile) held steady across 150M, 400M, and 1B target models, delivering up to **46.6% net FLOP savings** on noisier corpora like The Pile and C4/noclean.

Its limits are clear as well: second-order meta-training is far heavier to engineer than DataMan, and public validation stops at 1B target models. Whether valuation policies learned via 400M inner models transfer cleanly two orders of magnitude up to 100B+ MoE architectures remains an open empirical question.

In practice, mature teams **stack all three layers**: lightweight multi-dimensional taggers like DataMan for bulk cataloging, DataDecide’s continuous-probability proxies as corpus-level A/B gates, and meta-learned valuation selectively on noisy, high-value core pools.

## Boundary 4: Once Natural Data Runs Dry, Environments and Verifiers Must Gate Synthesis

For long-horizon agents (editing repositories, calling tools, browsing live pages, debugging failures, rolling back state) and multimodal search, the open web simply does not contain vast stores of clean, end-to-end trajectories with ground-truth feedback. Selection hits the ceiling of existing supply, pushing data engineering into **verifiable synthesis**.

First, rule out a dead-end shortcut: harvesting hidden reasoning traces from proprietary APIs. In August 2026, [*Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09867) uncovered a real cryptographic scoping flaw: major vendors encrypted hidden thinking blocks for client round-tripping across multi-turn calls, yet those ciphertext blocks remained interchangeable across sessions and models within the same provider. Injecting a frontier model’s ciphertext into a weaker, looser sibling model coerced it into printing the plaintext trace. The authors reproduced the flaw across Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google, even decoding 367 PII records and 182 credentials from 315,320 reasoning blocks scraped from public repositories. Such loopholes are patched quickly and cross legal and security red lines; durable data moats cannot be built on leaked black-box traces.

Three summer 2026 system papers show what scalable, grounded synthetic engines actually look like: every one anchors on real materials and hands quality gating to executable environments or isolated verifiers.

### 1. Kimi K3: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graphs for Breadth, Isolated Verifiers Against Cheating

In [*Kimi K3: Open Frontier Intelligence*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24653) (released late July 2026, revised August), synthesis spans both ends of training:

- **Pre-training (§3.1)**: Scales multi-style, multi-perspective **rephrasing** for knowledge and math with chunk-level fidelity checks against source documents, alongside **programmatic multimodal pairs** binding code directly to rendered SVGs, 3D assets, and web pages.
- **Post-training Agents (§4.2)**: To prevent synthetic tasks from collapsing into repetitive templates, the team builds a self-evolving **hierarchical knowledge graph** (a DAG explicitly named Knowledge Graph rather than Task Graph). Starting from coarse seed concepts, agents recursively search the web for sub-concepts, deduplicating against existing nodes before insertion, with directed edges strictly flowing coarse-to-fine down to atomic skills. Task synthesis samples cross-granularity node combinations, retrieves real papers, blogs, and repos along ancestor paths, and emits tasks across composable white-box harnesses (emulating diverse coding-agent scaffolds).

Quality gating centers on §4.2.6 Autonomous Execution Tasks (AET): tasks omit reference trajectories and instead encapsulate **public diagnostic verifiers, hidden held-out verifiers, and submission-budget penalties**, blocking brute-force probing and reward hacking.

### 2. Qwen-AgentWorld: Train the Environment Into a World Model, Then Scale Sim RL

Live sandboxes, browsers, and external APIs are slow, costly, and flaky—and production edge cases like intermittent 500 errors, truncated pagination, and partial batch failures rarely trigger on demand. In June 2026, Qwen’s [*Qwen-AgentWorld: Language World Models for General Agents*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24597) flipped the problem: **train the environment itself as a model.**

The team collected **over 10 million real interaction trajectories** across 7 domains (MCP, Search, Terminal, SWE, Android, Web, OS), trained 35B-A3B and 397B-A17B language world models through CPT → SFT → RL to predict environment transitions $$p(o_{t+1} \mid o_{\le t}, a_{\le t})$$, and used them to power large-scale **Simulation RL (Sim RL)**.

Three evaluations make the payoff unmistakable:

- **Out-of-distribution transfer (OpenClaw)**: Distilling reusable seed scenes from a few real traces and expanding along state-variation and task-variant axes into **4,000 simulated environments** lifted Claw-Eval by **+4.3** and QwenClawBench by **+7.1** zero-shot.
- **Controlled fault injection (MCP tool use)**: Deliberately injecting API errors, pagination cutoffs, and incomplete returns lifted MCPMark from an SFT baseline of 21.5 to **33.8 (+12.3)**, well ahead of unperturbed simulation (24.6).
- **Grounded synthetic search (WideSearch)**: Building **1,000 self-consistent fictional relational database worlds** (300–500 structured rows each, deriving ground truth via SQL, reverse-generating queries, and enforcing leak-free chunked returns) drove a **+16.3** gain on real WideSearch—even **beating live online RL** (50.3% vs. 45.6%).

The paper also flags the true bottleneck of simulated synthesis: **Initial State Conditioning**. The finer the seed state passed to the world model (filesystems, account configs, underlying database schemas), the more faithful the simulation; cutting corners with vague text prompts causes synthetic trajectories to drift within a few turns.

### 3. WeAgent-MMSearch: Reverse-Hop Expansion From Pixel-Dependent Endpoints

Synthetic multimodal search data has a persistent leak: question text or webpage URLs quietly give away the answer, letting models score full marks via text shortcuts without inspecting a single pixel. In August 2026, WeChat AI and Sun Yat-sen University closed that gap in [*WeAgent-MMSearch*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28062) with a four-step reverse pipeline:

1. **Lock a pixel-dependent endpoint ($$n_d$$)**: Harvest target images and visual facts from live Jan–Jul 2026 web events and Wikipedia, admitting an item only if the image is reachable inside the isomorphic `WeAgent-Harness` and the answer strictly requires **visible pixels** absent from titles, URLs, and captions.
2. **Reverse multi-hop expansion ($$n_0 \to \dots \to n_{d-1} \to n_d$$)**: Instead of writing questions forward, expand backward $$d \in \{2, 3, 4\}$$ hops from $$n_d$$. At each step, an expert agent sees only entity $$n_i$$ and searches the web to craft a prerequisite clue pointing uniquely to it, yielding $$n_{i-1}$$.
3. **Bridge uniqueness and leak audit**: Drop any chain with ambiguous intermediate hops, missing images, or metadata leakage.
4. **Tiered SFT and RL filtering**: SFT retains only **complete and correct** expert traces collected inside the exact deployment harness with persistent image storage and multi-turn recall (3.3K custom + 56.2K normalized open-source = 59.5K). Before RL, the SFT model samples **8 rollouts** (policy trajectory samples) per candidate prompt, **discarding 8/8 all-pass tasks (no headroom) and 0/8 all-fail tasks (no group contrast reward)** to keep 4.9K high-leverage prompts.

All three systems share one discipline: ground synthesis in real artifacts or fine-grained state to prevent collapse; block shortcuts structurally to eliminate fake difficulty; and hand acceptance gating to isolated verifiers or rollout contrast instead of model self-grading.

## Five Pre-Flight Questions Before Touching Any Pipeline

Turn the four boundaries into a one-page engineering checklist built around concrete **actions** and **red flags**:

<Steps>
  <StepItem title="Is the real bottleneck compute or your unique-token ceiling?">
    **Action**: Compare total planned training tokens against your deduplicated unique pool. Under tight compute, lean on per-dump deduplication and quality filtering; near a fixed-pool multi-epoch limit, relax harsh filters and invest in faithful rephrasing or measured cross-lingual transfer.<br />
    **Red Flag**: Deduplication wipes >80% of an early crawl dump, or held-out NLL bends upward after low-resource languages cross 1 epoch.
  </StepItem>
  <StepItem title="Is this batch teaching knowledge, runtime protocols, or policy boundaries?">
    **Action**: Maintain four separate acceptance bars for pre-training (broad coverage), annealing/long-context (high precision plus long-short mixing), SFT (encoding think toggles, budget cutoffs, and tool schemas directly in data), and RL (cold-start format stability before policy search).<br />
    **Red Flag**: Long-context benchmarks climb while 4k general scores drop, or serving-time truncation breaks answer convergence.
  </StepItem>
  <StepItem title="Does your selector still beat random once the pool grows 10×?">
    **Action**: Whenever an instruction pool passes a few hundred thousand items, always benchmark against same-budget random sampling; if external scorers slip, switch to target base-model hidden states (e.g., RDS+) with explicit diversity control.<br />
    **Red Flag**: Top-scored samples converge on one verbose template while OOD benchmarks trail random sampling.
  </StepItem>
  <StepItem title="Are you signing off by eyeballing 10 rows or running cheap proxy gates?">
    **Action**: Before burning full cluster runs, rank candidate corpora pairwise with ~150M proxy models using continuous probability metrics (`Correct Prob` / `Total Prob`) on code and knowledge tasks, keeping versioned data diffs.<br />
    **Red Flag**: Pipeline thresholds are debated around five cherry-picked spreadsheet rows with no reproducible proxy ranking log.
  </StepItem>
  <StepItem title="Who grades your synthetic data: a self-judging LLM or an executable verifier?">
    **Action**: Anchor synthetic tasks on real state, knowledge graphs, or visible pixels; separate public diagnostics from hidden held-out verifiers; filter RL prompts by rollout pass-rate contrast to remove all-pass and all-fail tasks.<br />
    **Red Flag**: Training reward surges steadily while hidden held-out verifiers or real online evals stay flat.
  </StepItem>
</Steps>

<Callout type="note" title="Three Open Questions Without Large-Scale Empirical Consensus">
Across public literature today, three critical zones remain unresolved:<br />
1. **100B+ transfer of meta-learned valuation**: DataRater validates from 400M inner models to 1B targets, but whether optimal pruning ratios and sample rankings stay robust two orders of magnitude higher on 100B+ MoE and hybrid architectures has no public proof yet.<br />
2. **Small-scale predictability for math and complex reasoning**: DataDecide’s continuous probabilities reliably rank knowledge and code corpora early, yet math benchmarks stay near random at tiny scales—leaving a gap for low-cost reasoning-data proxies.<br />
3. **Raw tail web data vs. verifiable synthesis under extreme compute**: The Bitter Lesson studies static fixed web pools, while frontier labs pour rising compute into rephrasing, graph synthesis, and world-model simulation; no unified public scaling law governs the optimal compute split between the two yet.
</Callout>
