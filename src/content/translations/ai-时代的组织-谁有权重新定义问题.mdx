AI can improve an individual's ability to complete tasks. But faster individuals do not necessarily mean smoother collaboration or a better direction. Organizations still need to decide what is worth doing, who should judge, how to work together, and who bears the consequences.

In [Life Principles for Long-Term Thinkers](/blog/长期主义者人生原则/), the first principle of organizational structure is high cohesion and loose coupling. Following that line of thought, here are several organizational principles for the AI era. The specific tradeoffs still depend on the business.

## 1. Organizational Design

1. **Organize work around a shared goal**: Establish the ultimate problem to solve before assigning responsibilities. Every department meeting its own targets does not mean the user's problem has been solved. For example, a delivery may have been signed for, but if installation is unfinished, the user still cannot use the product.

2. **High cohesion, loose coupling**: Where work requires frequent joint judgment, assign it to the same team whenever possible. Coordinate independently deliverable work through explicit agreements. A small experiment should not require repeated scheduling across multiple departments.

3. **Retain specialized expertise while allowing cross-disciplinary problem-solving**: Specialist teams should own long-term capabilities, standards, and talent development. When a problem spans several fields, someone should be able to bring the relevant people together to move it forward. This does not require everyone to become a generalist, nor should departmental boundaries define the boundaries of a problem.

4. **Match accountability with authority**: Whoever is responsible for an outcome should have the corresponding resources, time, and decision rights. With responsibility but no ability to mobilize resources, an owner can do little beyond chasing progress. Delegated authority also needs clear boundaries; it must not allow costs to be shifted arbitrarily onto other teams.

## 2. Knowledge and Collaboration

1. **Share the basis for your judgment**: Along with conclusions, communicate the key assumptions, constraints, and available evidence. Saying only “it cannot be done” leaves others unable to tell whether the prerequisites are temporarily missing or the direction itself is flawed.

2. **Diagnose together before assigning responsibility**: Cross-disciplinary problems often require investigation before their causes become clear. Do not insist that someone first prove a problem belongs to your team before agreeing to help investigate. Investigation needs resources, and once the cause is known, someone must take ownership of the solution.

3. **Distinguish disagreements about information, interpretation, and interests**: Missing knowledge calls for more information; different interpretations call for discussion of assumptions; conflicting interests call for changed arrangements or a decision about tradeoffs. Not every problem is “insufficient communication” to be solved by adding meetings.

## 3. Incentives and Organizational Learning

1. **Make collaboration worthwhile for individuals too**: Helping other teams should count in workload planning and contribution assessments. An organization cannot encourage cross-department collaboration while ranking people solely by their own department's output, leaving cooperation permanently dependent on a few helpful individuals.

2. **Recognize contributions that eliminate unproductive work**: Ruling out a wrong direction, stopping a project that is no longer worth pursuing, or removing an unnecessary process may be more valuable than adding work. Do not reward only project counts, team size, and visible busyness.

3. **Evaluate exploration and reliable delivery separately**: Mature businesses should emphasize efficiency, quality, and predictability; exploration should focus on whether important assumptions have been tested. A well-supported negative result can also improve subsequent decisions. But exploration still needs budgets, deadlines, and stopping conditions.

4. **Make experience testable and reusable**: Record what was done, why it was done, and under which conditions it worked. Preserve the evidence from failed attempts too. The faster experience spreads, the more important it becomes to distinguish what has been verified, what was merely a judgment at the time, and what is already obsolete.

## 4. Leaders' Judgment

1. **Judgment needs a basis and must remain open to revision**: Experience can help people identify a direction earlier, but judgments that affect resource allocation should come with reasons. Be willing to change decisions when new evidence emerges. Do not replace discussion with appeals to “taste,” leaving the team to guess a leader's preferences.

2. **Distinguish questions that can be verified from tradeoffs that must be accepted**: When facts are unclear, investigate further. When goals genuinely conflict, decide which costs to accept and who will bear them. Do not repeatedly use “let us analyze it some more” to postpone a decision. Where possible, begin important decisions with small, reversible experiments.

3. **Develop the ability to judge independently**: Help team members understand how to examine evidence, assess costs, and revise direction. A healthy organization should not need one particular person present for every decision. Providing direction is one thing; progressively handing over the information and authority needed to make judgments matters more.

## 5. The Division of Work Between People and AI

1. **Assign work by task and continuously test capability boundaries**: AI can take on more information gathering, preliminary analysis, and proposal generation. Check reliability for each specific task: a complete-looking answer is not necessarily correct, and today's division of work may shift tomorrow.

2. **Capability is not authorization**: AI being able to perform an action does not mean it may independently make commitments on an organization's behalf. Specify, at the level of concrete actions, what it may execute autonomously, what requires confirmation, and how to stop and recover when something goes wrong. When a problem occurs, an accountable person must be identifiable.

3. **Assess overall improvement, not just increased output**: More documents, code, and proposals show only that output has increased. Also ask whether problems are resolved sooner and whether waiting and rework have decreased. If checking and coordination costs rise instead, greater output is not an improvement. Using the time saved to test more important questions is more useful than simply squeezing in additional tasks.
