> Report: *On the Design of Qwen3.8-Next Architecture: Evaluation, Efficiency, and Training Stability*, Qwen Team, cover date 2026-08-26, arXiv:2608.30320v1 (2026-08-31).<br />
> [Official PDF](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next/blob/main/tech_report.pdf) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30320) · [Model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next)<br />
> The report covers Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, an open-weight architecture preview. The production Qwen3.8-Flash is built on it with additional product features, and that configuration is not evidence for this article. Page numbers refer to the official PDF.

Every layer of Qwen3.8-Flash-Next takes its input from four copies of intermediate state kept in parallel, and writes its result back into those four copies. According to the report, the inference cost of this structure is dominated by the memory traffic of reading those copies in and writing them back. The team noticed that in trained models each layer's writes were usually concentrated on two of the four, so they tried letting each layer use only the two with the highest gate values, both from scratch and introduced mid-training. Pretraining loss and benchmarks barely changed, but after post-training the model's quality dropped clearly, and keeping a different number of copies in different layers did not fix it. This bandwidth-saving trick was dropped. The authors made a point of recording it: had they looked only at pretraining metrics, they would have made the wrong decision here. (§2.2, PDF p.14.)

The 28-page report has several more disagreements of this kind. One change lowered loss only a little but raised benchmarks a lot; another kept lowering loss while benchmarks stopped improving first; others changed how large a learning rate training could withstand. The authors applied the same checks to every candidate change: loss and downstream benchmarks are read together; cost is counted separately for training, prefill and decode; and they check whether the change moves the optimal hyperparameters or makes training unstable. (Introduction, PDF pp.1 to 2.)

This article first follows those disagreements to see how the Flash-Next architecture was settled, then looks at why, once the architecture changes, the optimizer, learning rate, batch size and stability tests all have to be redone. If I could pick only two reports for learning how large models are really trained, I would choose this one and the [MAI-Thinking-1 technical report](https://microsoft.ai/pdf/mai-thinking-1.pdf). All measurements come from the report's authors. The diagrams, the approximate values read from figures and the derivations are this article's own, and nothing here reproduces the training or inference.

## What 125B, 6B, 51B and one-ninth each count

Flash-Next is a sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) model: each layer has many expert networks, and each token is routed to only a few of them. The report gives three parameter figures: 125B parameters in total, 6B activated per token, and another 51B parameters in n-gram embedding tables held in host memory outside the accelerator. (Abstract; Table 11, PDF p.22.) The official model card also lists a separate 4B MTP (multi-token prediction) module, which the report's text does not break out.

These numbers answer different questions. The 6B is the amount of weight that takes part in matrix multiplication during one token's forward pass, which roughly sets the compute per token. The 125B is the backbone weight that has to be stored, since unselected experts still have to live somewhere. The 51B of tables is looked up by address and each token fetches only a few rows, so the extra compute is negligible, but those parameters still take storage, and the fetched rows still have to be moved onto the accelerator. Adding the three numbers does not give a GPU memory requirement either, because the report says nothing about where the weights are placed or at what precision they are stored.

Against the Base model of the previous flagship, Qwen3.7-Plus (397B total parameters, 17B activated), Flash-Next-Base leads on 8 of 14 pretraining benchmarks and trails on the other 6 by at most 2.6 points. The report says this takes about a third of the activated parameters and a third of the training tokens, or roughly one-ninth of the training FLOPs. (Abstract; §4, PDF p.23.)

The one-ninth can be read through the common estimate `training FLOPs ≈ 6 × activated parameters × training tokens`: each factor is about a third, so the product is about a ninth (6B over 17B is about 0.35). That reading is this article's; the report does not publish how many training tokens either generation used. It is a ratio of arithmetic work, not a bill and not wall-clock time: the same FLOPs cost different time and money depending on hardware utilization, communication and optimizer overhead. The comparison also covers only Base models on pretraining benchmarks; post-trained models and serving costs are not in these numbers.

Cost also has to be split by phase. Training runs the forward pass, the backward pass and the optimizer update over large batches of full sequences. At inference, prefill reads the whole input at once, and the report says this phase is dominated by attention over the entire context; decode produces one or a few tokens per step and is limited by memory traffic, since every step has to read the weights, caches and states. (Introduction, PDF p.2.) The same change often helps only one of these phases, and the sections below keep the accounts separate.

The overall structure is in Figure 1. Input tokens first look up an ordinary vocabulary embedding, while the n-gram layer uses the few short n-grams ending at the current token to look up vectors in its tables, and the result joins at Layer 2. The backbone comes in groups of four layers, three Gated DeltaNet (GDN) layers and one QSA layer; per the model card the model has 48 layers, so 12 groups and 12 QSA layers. Each layer has two sublayers, a token mixer (GDN or QSA) followed by an MoE. Every sublayer first reads its input from four residual branches through a GR Read (these are the four copies of intermediate state from the opening), and after computing writes back to the four branches through a GR Write; the details come later. The last GR Read is merged with the normalization before the output, and its result goes to the prediction head and the MTP modules.

## One full-attention layer in four, with RoPE kept

The token mixer decides which earlier information a position can reach. Full attention lets every position look up any earlier token directly by content. Over a sequence of n tokens (in training or prefill), the pairwise scoring work grows with n squared; during generation, each new token scans the whole history, so its work grows linearly with history length, and the KV cache grows linearly as well. Sliding-window attention (SWA) lets each position see only a recent window, so the work per query and the KV that the layer must keep are both bounded, while the total work over a whole sequence still grows linearly with its length, and information outside the window can only travel indirectly through more layers. GDN takes a different route: each head compresses the prefix into a fixed-size state and updates it once per token read, so the per-token read and write cost does not grow with the history. (§2.1.1, PDF pp.3 to 4.)

Each GDN head keeps a state matrix `S` of shape `d_k × d_v`, whose size does not depend on how many tokens have been read. When token t arrives, the input first produces a query `q_t`, a key `k_t` (both L2-normalized) and a value `v_t`, and the state is updated by the report's Eq. 1 to 4:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\tilde S_{t-1}&=\alpha_t S_{t-1},\quad \alpha_t\in(0,1)\\
e_t&=v_t-\tilde S_{t-1}^{\top}k_t\\
S_t&=\tilde S_{t-1}+\beta_t k_t e_t^\top,\quad\beta_t\in(0,1)\\
y_t&=S_t^\top q_t
\end{aligned}
```

The four rows apply global decay, read the old value with the key to compute the error, write back only the error, and read the current output with the query.

Both `α_t` and `β_t` are computed from the current input: `α_t` controls how long old memory survives as a whole, and `β_t` controls how much this step rewrites. What separates GDN from ordinary linear attention is the second line: before writing, it uses `k_t` to check what the state already holds, and writes only the prediction error.

A small example, for illustration only: `d_k = 2`, `d_v = 1`, two orthogonal unit keys `k1 = (1, 0)` and `k2 = (0, 1)`. Take `α = β = 1` first. This is the boundary case of no decay and full correction; real gate outputs fall inside (0, 1) and never reach these values. Write k1→5, then k2→2, then k1→3 again:

```text
write k1→5: error 5 − 0 = 5, reading with k1 gives 5
write k2→2: error 2 − 0 = 2, k1 still reads 5, k2 reads 2
write k1→3: error 3 − 5 = −2, k1 reads 3, k2 still reads 2
linear attention that just accumulates k vᵀ without error correction: k1 reads 5 + 3 = 8
```

When the same key comes back, GDN rewrites the old association instead of piling up without bound. Away from the boundary values the gates act as decay and partial correction; both cases below start from the state after the third write. With `α = 0.5` and `β` still 1, every old association is halved before the write, so writing k2→2 again leaves k1 reading 1.5 and k2 still reading 2. With `α = 1` and only `β` changed to 0.5, the step corrects only half the error, so writing k1 from 3 toward 1 makes it read 2.

In a real model the keys are never exactly orthogonal and the state has limited capacity. The report's position is that no finite-state recurrent memory can exactly reproduce direct token-level retrieval, which is why a full-attention layer is placed every few layers. Before entering the recurrence, q, k and v also pass through a short causal convolution that adds some local information; on the output side, the report replaces the original GDN's SiLU gate with a bounded sigmoid gate and says it was consistently better in their experiments, without giving numbers. (PDF p.4.)

For the hybrid design itself, the report gives one comparison at a fixed scale (Table 1, PDF p.5). The model is a 28-layer 25B-A3B MoE based on the Qwen3.5 architecture, trained first on 400B tokens at 4K length and then on 80B tokens at 32K. The pure full-attention version averages 49.87 over 9 benchmarks; the SWA hybrid, in which three of every four layers are SWA with a window of 128, averages 51.15; the GDN hybrid, which replaces those three layers with GDN, averages 53.81. The GDN hybrid beats full attention on 8 of the 9, the exception being EvalPlus, and beats the SWA hybrid on 7; the SWA hybrid is slightly higher on MMLU and stronger on EvalPlus.

The authors note themselves that these results support a hybrid design but cannot say which component produced each gain, and all 9 benchmarks are short-context. That one full-attention layer in four strikes a good balance between efficiency and quality, and that periodic full attention matters especially for long context, are stated as conclusions without a corresponding ablation over the ratio.

The positional encoding in the full-attention layers was settled only after post-training. A NoPE version without RoPE was nearly indistinguishable from the RoPE version during pretraining, but after post-training it was clearly more prone to endless generation that would not stop. RoPE was therefore kept in the full-attention layers. The report gives neither the scale of this experiment nor the actual rates. (§2.1.1, PDF p.4.)

On the training side, the GDN kernel was rewritten with FlashQLA, which is built on TileLang. The report says that across multiple settings on NVIDIA GPUs its forward pass is 2 to 3 times faster than the FLA Triton kernel and its backward pass about 2 times faster; this is an operator-level comparison. (PDF p.5.)

## QSA scores compressed blocks but still reads the original tokens

The one full-attention layer in every four is still a quadratic cost at long context. DeepSeek's DSA first uses a lightweight indexer to score history tokens and runs attention only over the selected ones, but the indexer itself has to score every query-key pair, so its cost is still `O(n²)` and becomes more visible as sequences grow. QSA lets the indexer look only at compressed keys. It is introduced during continued pretraining (CPT) and replaces every full-attention layer in the backbone and in the MTP modules. (§2.1.2, PDF pp.5 to 7.)

Follow one query at position `i`; the report numbers positions from 0. The indexer is a very light multi-query attention in which 4 query heads share 1 key head. Keys are split into non-overlapping blocks of 4 adjacent tokens (compression ratio `r = 4`). Each block is averaged and RMS-normalized, and only then receives RoPE (applied to 64 of the 128 dimensions in each head), with every block positioned at its start `4b`, while the query keeps its own position `i`. Compressing before adding position avoids averaging vectors with different rotary phases.

Only blocks that have fully appeared are scored: block `b` takes part only if its last token `4b + 3` is not later than `i`, and its score is the sum over the 4 heads of the query-key inner products after ReLU. With a token budget of `K = 2048`, each query keeps the `2048 / 4 = 512` highest-scoring blocks and expands them back to the original token indices; the 0 to 3 tokens that have already appeared in the block not yet filled are always added. Core attention finally reads the K/V of these original tokens; the compressed blocks are used only for scoring.

```math
\begin{aligned}
\bar k_b=\operatorname{RoPE}\bigl(&\operatorname{RMSNorm}(\\
&\operatorname{mean}(k_{4b},\ldots,k_{4b+3})),4b\bigr)\\
I_{ib}&=\sum_h\operatorname{ReLU}(q_{i,h}\cdot\bar k_b)\\
B_i&=\operatorname{Top512}(I_{i,\cdot})\\
S_i&=\operatorname{expand}(B_i)\\
&\quad\cup\{4\lfloor(i+1)/4\rfloor,\ldots,i\}
\end{aligned}
```

RoPE uses position $$4b$$. Scores are computed only for complete blocks satisfying $$4b+3\le i$$. $$B_i$$ contains the 512 highest-scoring blocks, with $$K=2048$$ and $$r=4$$. Their original tokens are expanded and combined with the current incomplete block.

Take `i = 9`. Positions 0 to 3 and 4 to 7 form two complete blocks and can be scored; the block holding 8 and 9 still lacks 10 and 11, so it is not scored, and the already visible 8 and 9 are read directly as the tail; 10 and 11 have not been generated and no path can reach them. At `i = 11`, the block from 8 to 11 is complete, the tail is empty, and that block competes in the top-k like any other. The complete-block condition and the tail rule are written in Eq. 15 and Eq. 19, so even though the whole sequence is available during training, scoring never touches future positions.

The budget implies one more property: with 512 blocks plus at most 3 tail tokens, as long as the visible prefix is at most 2,051 tokens, every query reads exactly all visible tokens, the same set that causal full attention reads, and low-scoring blocks start being skipped only from token 2,052 onward. This is this article's derivation from Eq. 16 and Eq. 19. How many positions in the short-context benchmarks fall inside this range depends on the actual length of each item, which the report does not give. Even where the read sets are identical, the QSA model and the full-attention baseline went through different training, so their weights are not the same.

QSA is trained in two stages. Stage 1 is dense distillation, which trains only the indexer, with full attention itself as the teacher: the softmax distributions of all teacher heads are summed and L1-normalized to give query `i`'s probability over every token, which is then max-pooled within each block and renormalized into a block-level target, and a KL divergence makes the indexer's block scores fit it. This stage runs 1,000 steps at learning rate `1e-3` with 8 sequences of 256K per step, about 2B tokens in total. Stage 2 is sparse training: the backbone attends over the tokens chosen by the indexer and is trained together with it, the KL is computed only over the selected blocks, and the teacher probabilities are renormalized within those blocks; it runs 8,000 steps at learning rate `2.5e-5` with 96 sequences of 256K per step, about 200B tokens. (Eq. 17 to 20, PDF pp.6 to 7.)

The same block is pooled differently in two places. The index key takes the average, a summary of the four tokens' content. The teacher target takes the maximum, so a block gets a high target if any one of its tokens receives strong full attention, without being averaged down by the other three; preserving salient token-level signals is exactly the report's reason. During training the LM loss curves with and without QSA nearly coincide, and the report puts the difference on the order of `10⁻⁴`, with QSA slightly higher in the inset. (Figure 4, PDF p.7.)

The block size and the number of indexer heads were chosen by long-context retrieval. The ablations were run at the 35B-A3B scale and scored with RULER at lengths up to 1M, all after Stage 2 training. (PDF pp.8 to 9.)

RULER is about 86.8 at `r = 4` against about 86.3 for the full-attention baseline; it falls to about 83.6 at `r = 8` and about 79.4 at `r = 16`; `r = 2` ties `r = 4` at twice the estimated cost. The comparison method, IndexShare, lets neighbouring full-attention layers share one set of top-k indices; at an estimated cost of 0.5, which means two attention layers separated by three GDN layers share one set of indices, it reaches about 85.5, still below the baseline. The authors' explanation is that neighbouring attention layers in a hybrid model are not very similar, which limits cross-layer index sharing and favours compression within the layer.

In the right panel, going sparse directly after Stage 1 leaves RULER at only about 77.5 to 78.1, and more heads do not rescue it; after joint training, 1 or 2 heads give about 85.5, 4 heads about 86.8 and 16 heads about 87.2. The final choice was 4 heads.

With the configuration fixed, the report compares full attention and QSA directly on Flash-Next. On 8 short-context benchmarks the average goes from 75.9 to 76.8, QSA ties or beats full attention on 7, and MMMLU drops from 81.8 to 81.1. (Table 2, PDF p.8.) Up to 256K the two are about even, with QSA slightly lower on MRCR; beyond that QSA leads, with RULER in the 512K to 1M range going from 90.08 to 93.00 and MRCR at 1M from 20.71 to 26.44. In absolute terms, 8-needle MRCR at 512K and 1M is far from reliable with either attention. QSA's CPT sequence length is 256K, so 512K and 1M are beyond the training length; the model card gives a native context of 262,144 tokens, with 1M as an extended configuration. The report does not analyze how positions are handled beyond the training length or why QSA degrades less there.

Attention in the MTP modules is also replaced with QSA, and the same top-k indices are reused across the prediction steps of speculative decoding, an approach taken from GLM-5. Under four-step speculative decoding the mean accepted length goes from 4.06 to 4.07, so reusing indices did not make the drafts worse. (Table 4, PDF p.8.)

To judge how much QSA saves, first look at what Figure 6 measures. It is kernel-level latency of the attention module, including both the indexer and the sparse core attention, compared with FlashInfer's paged GQA full attention. Prefill measures the last 16K chunk under chunked prefill at batch size 1; decode uses batch size 4 and `next_n = 4`, which includes three MTP prediction steps. The report does not say what hardware was used. (Figure 6, PDF p.9.)

At a total context of 1M, the attention module is 7.6 times faster in prefill and 4.9 times faster in decode; at 64K the two are about the same, and the gap widens with context length. The 7.6 times in prefill is the latency ratio for processing the last 16K chunk when the total context is 1M. Earlier chunks of the same long input have shorter prefixes and smaller ratios, so the speedup over a whole prefill is lower than this number.

Putting (a) next to (c) shows where the remaining cost sits. These are again approximate values read from the vector graphics. At a total context of 1M, processing the last 16K chunk takes about 193 ms with GQA and about 25 ms with QSA, of which the ratio-4 indexer alone is about 15 ms; in decode, GQA takes about 1.18 ms, QSA about 0.24 ms and the indexer about 0.16 ms. At 64K, the prefill indexer is only 1.6 ms of QSA's roughly 10.3 ms. Each doubling of context multiplies the indexer time by 1.6 to 2.1, approaching a doubling at longer lengths, while the part of QSA outside the indexer grows only from about 8.7 ms to about 10.5 ms, because the number of tokens each query reads is fixed at just over two thousand.

So at 1M, most of QSA's cost is in the indexer. It still scores every complete block, so the work per query grows with `n/4`, and a whole prefill is `O(n²/r)`: compression shrinks the constant of the quadratic term to a quarter but does not make it linear. The 3.8 times and 4.4 times speedups of ratio 4 over ratio 1 in panels (a) and (b) are consistent with that ratio.

The 2,048 budget limits how much each query reads at this step, not how much history is kept. Any complete block may be selected by some later query, so the K/V of every original token has to remain accessible, and the selection budget by itself does not cut the KV history down to 2,048 tokens. The report does not say where these caches live or how many bytes they take.

These numbers cover only the attention module. Only 12 of the 48 layers are QSA; the GDN layers, MoE, GR reads and writes, n-gram lookups and communication are outside the comparison, and the report gives no end-to-end prefill or decode throughput. Two assumptions give a feel for the gap: suppose half of a service's prefill time is spent in these attention layers (`f = 0.5`), and suppose that whole part speeds up 7.6 times; the overall speedup is then only `1 / (0.5 + 0.5 / 7.6) ≈ 1.77` times. Both conditions are this article's assumptions, since Figure 6 measures only one chunk at a fixed position.

## After widening the residual stream, spend the expressiveness on the read

Every sublayer of a Transformer reads its input from the same residual stream and adds its output back to that stream. Pre-norm (normalizing before each sublayer) keeps large models stable in training, but the price is that each sublayer reads the sum of all earlier sublayers' outputs, so a feature written early has to compete with everything written after it. (§2.2, PDF p.10.)

The first step is widening alone. The residual state becomes 4 parallel branches; each block forms its input as a weighted sum of the 4 branches with 4 learnable scalars, and writes its output into one branch chosen in turn by layer index, which is a simplified version of AltUp (Eq. 21 to 22). Each block gains only 4 parameters and no matrix multiplication, so compute barely changes; what grows is the memory traffic of moving 4 branches instead of 1. On a 25B-A3B MoE trained on 400B tokens, this alone lowered training loss by about 0.01. The remaining question is how much read and write machinery is worth adding on top of the widened stream.

Hyper-Connections (HC) split reading and writing into three operators: the read operator `H_mix` decides how much of each branch goes into the sublayer input, the write operator `H_combine` decides in what proportions the sublayer output is written into each branch, and the mixing operator `H_res` is a 4×4 matrix that directly exchanges the branches' contents on the identity path (Eq. 23 to 25). Each operator comes in static and dynamic forms: static operators are learned parameters that are the same for every token, while dynamic operators are computed from the current token's residual state and differ from token to token. mHC uses a sigmoid activation and constrains `H_res` to a doubly stochastic matrix. Table 5 records the two ends of a path that starts from static operators and adds machinery step by step (25B-A3B MoE, 560B tokens, same evaluation as Table 1):

| Residual design | Loss | 9-benchmark average |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Pre-norm (single residual stream) | 1.617 | 50.91 |
| mHC (static) | 1.596 | 52.49 |
| mHC (dynamic) | 1.594 | 54.47 |
| GR | 1.590 | 54.66 |

Data source: report Table 5, PDF p.11; only Loss and the average are listed, see the original table for individual scores.

From pre-norm to static operators, loss fell by 0.021 and the average rose by 1.58; from static to dynamic, loss fell by only another 0.002 while the average rose by 1.98. The relative size of the two steps reverses between loss and benchmarks, and looking at loss alone would understate the second step. The authors say that cases like this are why they kept reading loss and benchmarks together throughout the design process.

For the remaining findings the report gives only text. Read granularity matters more than write granularity: refining the read operator from one scalar per branch to one weight per branch and channel helps, while the same refinement of the write operator does almost nothing, so the write stays one scalar per branch. Predicting the operators from all branches beats using only the last branch or pooling the branches first, and normalizing each branch separately with RMSNorm gives a further gain. Once the read and write are flexible enough, adding the 4×4 `H_res` brings no significant improvement. (PDF pp.10 to 11.)

The final Gated Residual (GR) spends almost all of its expressiveness on the read. It has the same form as GatedNorm, which the team proposed in separate work: a lightweight elementwise self-gate after RMSNorm (Eq. 29). On the read, each of the 4 branches is RMS-normalized separately; the normalized branches are concatenated and passed through a low-rank bottleneck of rank `d/8` (`d` is the hidden size) and a sigmoid, giving one gate value for every channel of every branch; the gated branches are averaged to form the sublayer input. On the write, each branch gets just one scalar `s_i = 2σ(·)` between 0 and 2, and the sublayer output, multiplied by it, is added back to that branch. The attention sublayer and the MLP sublayer each have their own GR module.

```math
\begin{aligned}
\hat R_i&=\operatorname{RMSNorm}(R_i),\quad i=1,\ldots,4\\
G&=\sigma\Bigl(W_u\operatorname{SiLU}\bigl(\\
&\qquad W_d[\hat R_1;\ldots;\hat R_4]/4\bigr)\Bigr)\\
x&=\frac14\sum_i G_i\odot\hat R_i\\
y&=F(x)\\
s&=2\sigma\bigl(W_w[\hat R_1;\ldots;\hat R_4]/4\bigr)\\
R_i'&=R_i+s_i y
\end{aligned}
```

Each branch is normalized separately. $$G$$ produces $$4\times d$$ gate values and $$x$$ is the sublayer input. $$F$$ is GDN, QSA or MoE; $$s$$ produces four scalars, and the output is written back to each unnormalized branch.

<TechGraph src="/images/posts/qwen38-flash-next/gr-read-write.svg" title="RICH READ, SCALAR WRITE" alt="One GR read and write. Each of the four residual branches is RMS-normalized separately; 4 by d elementwise gate values are computed from all branches, and the gated normalized branches are averaged into the sublayer input; the output of the GDN, QSA or MoE sublayer is multiplied by one scalar per branch and added back to the four unnormalized branches. The 4 by 4 mixing matrix H_res on the identity path is removed, and branches still exchange information through the read, the sublayer and the write; residual branches are stored in FP8, and the read and the write are each fused into one kernel." caption="Author-created diagram of one GR read and one GR write, based on §2.2, Eq. 30 to 34 of the report. The read is gated elementwise per branch and channel, while the write has only one scalar per branch; there is no explicit branch-mixing matrix, so information is exchanged through the read, the sublayer and the write." />

Because the read already normalizes and gates, GR replaces the pre-norm in front of the sublayer, and widening adds no normalization layer. Without `H_res` there is no explicit branch mixing on the identity path: any exchange of information between branches goes through a read, a sublayer and a write. Each branch is therefore just the earlier sublayers' outputs accumulated with their write scalars, which makes the information flow easy to follow, and the analysis later relies on this.

GR differs from mHC (dynamic) mainly in its elementwise read and in dropping `H_res`, and the two are comparable in quality at this scale (averages of 54.66 and 54.47). Dropping `H_res` pays off in decode: each block skips one read of the entire residual state, and the report says the inference cost of a widened residual stream is dominated by this kind of memory traffic. The authors also argue that GatedNorm itself improves training stability and that removing `H_res`, which needs extra constraints, removes a possible source of instability; the stress tests later provide evidence for the first point. (PDF p.12.)

The report also compares a different approach, Attention Residual (AttnRes), in which each sublayer uses softmax attention over the outputs of earlier layers to decide what to read. On a 28-layer model, Full AttnRes, which attends over the output of every earlier sublayer, reaches a loss of 1.762, the same as GR; Block AttnRes, which sums sublayers in groups, is 0.008 to 0.011 worse; adding GatedNorm lowers every AttnRes variant by another 0.004 to 0.005, taking Full AttnRes with GatedNorm to 1.758, 0.004 below GR. (Table 6, PDF p.12.) The report does not explain why it was not chosen. Mechanically, every sublayer of Full AttnRes reads the outputs of all earlier sublayers, so decode moves far more data than reading 4 branches, which may be one reason; that is only this article's guess, with no measurement behind it. What the authors draw from these results is a different point: the more complex the input a sublayer reads, the more the gate helps, and that gate is exactly what GR carries in its read.

Now back to the shortcut abandoned in the opening. To move fewer bytes, the team tried two things. The first was letting each block use only the two branches with the highest gate values: pretraining metrics were barely affected, quality dropped clearly after post-training, and it was not adopted; the report alternately calls this a sparse read and a sparse write, but means the same variant. The second was storing the residual state in FP8. The report's reason is that the gates in GR, gated attention and GDN all bound the magnitude of what is written into the residual stream, so residual values stay in a narrow range that suits a low-precision format. Compared with BF16, FP8 halves the bytes moved for the residual state, and the report says quality is almost unaffected; this one was adopted, although the report does not say at which stage of evaluation that judgment was made. The read (Eq. 30 to 32) and the write (Eq. 33 to 34) are also each fused into a single kernel, with the group RMSNorm folded into the read, so each block traverses the widened stream once in each direction. (PDF p.14.)

To see where the added width goes, the report decomposes what each sublayer reads. Without a branch-mixing matrix, the input a sublayer reads can be split exactly into a sum of contribution vectors from every earlier sublayer (Eq. 35 to 36). The authors measure path strength with the share `π_uv` of Eq. 37: the norm of the contribution vector from sublayer u to the input of sublayer v, divided by the sum of the norms of all contributions. A share measures the size of a contribution vector, not an amount of information. On a 20-layer MoE, the authors compare a GR model with a reference model that has the same recipe, data, optimizer and training steps but no GR, probe both on the same tokens, and look at how much each path's share increases.

<Figure src="/images/posts/qwen38-flash-next/figure-7-gr-paths.png" alt="Report Figure 7. Four rows correspond to residual branches b0 to b3; arcs run from the sublayer that wrote into a branch to a later sublayer that reads it, with line width showing the extra input share compared with a single residual stream. Long arcs on b0 run from layer 0 to the attention layers at layers 11, 15 and 19, while the other branches carry mostly short connections; grey bands mark the softmax-attention layers." caption="Source: report Figure 7, PDF p.13. Faithfully cropped without modification. Only the 21 of 780 paths whose share increases by at least 0.05 are drawn." />

The result is that one branch specializes in long-range paths while the other three stay local. Long-range here means skipping many layers, a distance along the network's depth, not a distance between token positions. On b0 every path starts at layer 0 and lands on attention layers after layer 10. Each of 5 GR checkpoints has exactly one such branch, with a typical skip of 10.9 layers against 3.4 to 3.9 for the other branches. The share of the path from the layer-0 GDN to the layer-15 attention rises from 0.020 in the reference model to 0.138. The sublayers that read these branches most are the softmax-attention layers, from which the authors conclude that global attention is a hub for integrating the explicit long-range history that GDN compresses away. The share-weighted average skip over all paths is almost unchanged (3.97 against 3.91); what changes is how the shares are distributed over skip distances, concentrated on a few paths. (PDF pp.12 to 14.)

This is an observational decomposition of trained models that shows where GR's read weight concentrates. The report runs no intervention, such as switching off the long-range branch and watching loss and benchmarks, so the figure cannot say how much of the earlier gains comes from these paths.

## Lookup-table capacity: loss keeps falling, benchmarks stop first

An n-gram embedding adds capacity outside the backbone: the few short n-grams ending at each token serve as keys into embedding tables, and the retrieved vectors augment that token's representation. The lookup addresses depend only on the token sequence, so which rows to fetch is known before the token is computed, which lets the tables sit in host memory and be fetched asynchronously in advance; the extra compute per token is negligible. (§2.3, PDF pp.14 to 15.) The report does not disclose which n-gram orders are used, how addresses map to table slots, the vector dimension or the storage precision. All experiments in this section train with 300 tokens per activated parameter, and the model size is not stated.

Placement was compared with the total n-gram parameter count fixed (Table 7, PDF p.14). The baseline without n-grams has a loss of 1.585 and a 9-benchmark average of 45.44; at Layer 2 the loss is 1.541 and the average 47.94. Layers 1 and 2 perform strongly, intermediate and deep placements are not far behind, and no single depth dominates. Splitting the same parameters across two layers brings no consistent benefit either: Layers 2 and 25 together give a slightly lower loss (1.540) without better downstream results. In the end a single layer is used, at Layer 2, for an engineering reason: the table fetch can overlap with the computation of Layer 1.

The report tests table size as two separate questions. The first fixes the total parameter count: n-gram tables are added while MoE experts are removed, keeping the total unchanged (Table 8, PDF p.15). Loss is not monotonic in table size and is lowest when the n-gram vocabulary is 10 times the base vocabulary and the tables hold 25% of all parameters, consistent with the allocation sweet spots reported in earlier work; yet the out-of-domain uncheatable PPL barely moves, and downstream benchmarks are not clearly better than the MoE-only baseline. From this the authors conclude that n-gram tables and MoE experts play different roles in scaling capacity, and the later experiments keep the MoE budget fixed and add tables on top.

The second question is adding extra parameters (Table 9): the n-gram vocabulary is scaled from 20 to 200 times the base vocabulary (250K).

Loss falls monotonically all the way to 1.526 at 200 times. Downstream benchmarks rise broadly when the tables are first added, after which most saturate or fluctuate: MMLU barely moves beyond 50 times, and GSM8K and MATH both peak at 20 times and then fall back. The Chinese benchmarks C-Eval and CMMLU are the exception and keep rising with vocabulary size. The report also tried several ways to improve parameter efficiency, including token normalization to compress the vocabulary, allocating different numbers of slots to different n-gram orders and partitioning slots by frequency, and none gave consistent gains in their recipe. (PDF p.15.)

Flash-Next ends up with 51B parameters of tables; the report does not say which vocabulary multiple that corresponds to. Moving the tables to host memory frees that capacity on the accelerator, but the cost does not disappear. At 2 bytes per parameter (BF16 is this article's assumption; the report does not state the storage precision), 51B parameters come to about 102 GB that host memory has to hold; the rows fetched for each token travel over the host-to-accelerator link, and if they have not arrived when Layer 1 finishes, Layer 2 has to wait. Transfer volume, bandwidth use and waiting time are all unmeasured.

## Muon only on matrices that act as linear maps

Once the architecture is set, the optimizer has to be adjusted too. Flash-Next's main optimizer is Muon. AdamW scales each parameter element adaptively on its own; Muon treats a weight matrix's momentum as a whole, approximately orthogonalizes it with Newton-Schulz (NS) iterations, and uses the result as the update direction. The report's settings: orthogonalize Nesterov momentum with coefficient 0.95, run 8 NS steps with the per-step coefficients from the Polar Express method, which minimize the worst-case error for a given number of steps, and multiply the result by `0.2 · sqrt(max(A, B))` so that the update RMS of an `A × B` matrix does not depend on its shape. Eight steps orthogonalize more accurately than fewer, and the authors say this reduced both the size and the frequency of gradient-norm spikes in the stress test, paying some extra compute for that stability. (§3.1, PDF p.16; Introduction, PDF p.2.)

The choice of which parameters use Muon is a rule this recipe settled by experiment. The attention q/k/v and output projections, the GDN input and output projections, the fc1/fc2 of routed and shared experts, and the key/value projections of the n-gram layer use Muon; all of these are two-dimensional weights that act as linear maps. The exceptions, with the reasons the report gives:

| Parameters | Optimizer | Reason given in the report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MoE router | AdamW | Muon in early training amplifies fluctuations and destabilizes routing; switching to Muon in mid-to-late training causes no problems but brings no gains either. The authors suggest that each output dimension of the router is one expert's score, the dimensions are largely independent, and there is no shared linear structure for orthogonalization to exploit |
| GR's two low-rank projections | AdamW | Very elongated shapes, and AdamW works better |
| GDN decay and β projections | AdamW | Each head outputs one scalar, so these are effectively vectors and orthogonalization is meaningless |
| Attention output gate, GDN z projection | AdamW | AdamW was on par or slightly better in ablations |
| n-gram embedding tables | Adam with weight decay disabled | No separate explanation; the introduction loosely says AdamW, and this article follows §3.1 |

Data source: report §3.1, PDF p.16. The input embeddings and the output head also stay on AdamW; the introduction summarizes these groups as cases where orthogonalization is impractical or unhelpful.

In Megatron-LM, the attention qkv projection, the SwiGLU fc1 and the GDN input projection are each stored as one concatenated matrix, even though semantically each is several independent linear operators stacked along the output dimension. Orthogonalizing the concatenated matrix directly is wrong in two ways: the iteration mixes singular directions across unrelated sub-blocks, and the scaling coefficient is computed from the concatenated shape instead of the shape of the real operator. So the fused gradient is split first, NS runs on each piece, and the results are gathered back into the original layout. The qkv and GDN input projections are split per head, which improves both loss and benchmarks; fc1 is split into its gate and up halves, which leaves loss about the same and improves benchmarks slightly. After splitting, sub-matrices such as the GDN decay projection can also be excluded from Muon individually. (PDF p.16.)

Muon also brings engineering problems that AdamW does not have. Running K NS steps on an `A × B` matrix costs about `4K · max(A, B) · min(A, B)²` FLOPs, which depends on the shape and not just the number of elements. An example (the shapes are this article's assumption): a 4096×4096 matrix and a 1024×16384 matrix both have about 16.8 million elements, but with K = 8 the first needs about `2.2 × 10¹²` FLOPs and the second about `5.5 × 10¹¹`, a factor of 4. NS also needs the complete matrix. Under tensor parallelism (TP) no single rank holds the full weight; under data parallelism (DP), Megatron splits evenly by element count, so the rank that gets a large square matrix slows everyone down. The team built Canzona for this: it assigns whole matrices to DP ranks by estimated NS FLOPs, without cutting inside a tensor, then reassembles each full matrix across the TP ranks, so that each owner performs an update mathematically equivalent to single-device Muon. In addition, after the fused matrices are split, one layer contributes on the order of a hundred sub-matrices, and an optimizer step turns into a long chain of very small kernels whose time is set by launch overhead, so the whole optimizer step is captured in a CUDA graph. (PDF p.16.)

## Prove it is more stable under stress first

At trillions of parameters and tens of trillions of tokens, instabilities appear that small experiments never show. For example, a long run spends many more steps near the peak learning rate, giving instabilities more chances to surface as loss spikes or even divergence, which can only be handled by restarting from a checkpoint. (§3.3, PDF p.19.) Flash-Next changes the residual, the token mixer and the optimizer at once, and all three alter how updates and activations are scaled, so whether training stays stable over a long run had to be checked first.

The stress test borrows an observation: raising the learning rate reproduces large-scale training instabilities even in small models (Wortsman et al.). Concretely, the learning rate is held constant at 2 or 4 times its optimal value, with no decay, to simulate a production run that sits at the peak learning rate for a long time. The test model is a 28-layer 25B-A3B MoE, and all runs share the same batch size and a gradient-norm clipping threshold of 0.5. The pass criterion is that the new recipe must be at least as stable as the Qwen3.5 structure with AdamW, which has already been scaled successfully. Three quantities are recorded: loss spikes, meaning steps whose loss exceeds a 201-step rolling median by more than 0.1; the p99.9 of the pre-clip gradient norm and the number of times it crosses the clipping threshold; and the maximum activation of each block. (PDF p.20.)

<Figure src="/images/posts/qwen38-flash-next/figure-10-stress-loss.png" alt="Report Figure 10. The left panel holds the learning rate at 2 times its optimal value and the right panel at 4 times, comparing training loss for AdamW with the Qwen3.5 structure, Muon with the Qwen3.5 structure, and Muon with Gated Residual; at 4 times the AdamW curve shows repeated spikes and one large jump." caption="Source: report Figure 10, PDF p.19. Faithfully cropped without modification. Bold lines are moving averages; the faint lines behind them are per-step losses." />

At 2 times the optimal learning rate, the AdamW baseline starts to spike, 4.3 times per 10k steps, while both Muon configurations spike 0.2 times. At 4 times, AdamW spikes 183 times per 10k steps and crosses the clipping threshold on 213 of 19,932 steps, about 1.1%. Neither Muon run crosses the clipping threshold; as for loss spikes, the GR run records zero, and for the Muon-only run the report gives no count at 4 times. (PDF p.20.)

In the gradient and activation curves (Figure 11), at 2 times the learning rate the Muon runs have a higher median gradient norm and larger maximum activations than AdamW, yet far fewer spikes. At least in these curves, large activations do not mean more spikes. Adding GR lowers both the frequency and size of gradient-norm spikes and the size of activation outliers. (PDF pp.20 to 21.)

In this comparison, going from AdamW to Muon changes the optimizer, while going from Muon to Muon + GR adds the widening and the gate together. To isolate the gate, the report runs a single-variable pair: the same 28-layer model at 3 times its optimal learning rate, with the AdamW optimizer, the structure and the data order unchanged, toggling only GatedNorm (Figure 12). Turning the gate on cuts spikes from 32.0 to 3.2 per 10k steps and threshold crossings from 256 to 20. For the ungated baseline at 1, 2 and 3 times the learning rate, activation outliers grow almost in proportion to the learning rate while the spike rate grows much faster; the gated run at the highest learning rate has smaller outliers than the ungated run at the lowest. (PDF pp.20 to 21.) This isolation was done under AdamW; how much the gate contributes on its own under Muon has no matching single-variable experiment.

The authors' explanation is that training at a high learning rate needs some rescaling mechanism: without an explicit gate the network achieves it by growing activation outliers, which leaves it fragile, while a multiplicative gate supplies the rescaling directly and keeps training stable. This explanation draws on the team's earlier work on gated attention and outliers; it is an interpretation of these curves, and the experiments here do not prove it directly.

The stress test deliberately departs from the production configuration. To confirm that the benefit survives the real configuration, the report compares the first 276B tokens of three runs: the Qwen3.5 structure with Muon, the same structure plus GR, and the full Flash-Next recipe (with a further refined GR and the n-gram layer). All three share the data order, learning-rate schedule and optimizer, and the learning rate is the one used for the final run. (PDF pp.21 to 22.)

<Figure src="/images/posts/qwen38-flash-next/figure-13-production-early.png" alt="Report Figure 13, curves for the first 276B tokens of three runs: (a) training loss, with an inset magnifying the last 126B tokens; (b) pre-clip gradient norm, with an inset showing its standard deviation within a 1000-step window; (c) maximum residual activation. The light grey region is the learning-rate warmup." caption="Source: report Figure 13, PDF p.21. Faithfully cropped without modification. The shaded band is the learning-rate warmup." />

At 276B tokens, adding GR lowers loss by 0.026 and the full recipe lowers it by another 0.032, 0.058 below the Muon baseline in total. On gradient norm, the Muon-only run has roughly twice the median of the two gated runs and at least 4.2 times their p99.9 (0.097 and 0.298, against 0.053 and 0.071, and 0.043 and 0.066), and it is the only run that crosses the clipping threshold; the two gated runs have a 4.3 to 4.7 times lower standard deviation of the gradient norm within a 1000-step window. The authors say this reproduces the stress-test finding at 8 times the model scale and at the production learning rate, without saying how the 8 times is measured. The full recipe has an even lower gradient norm than Muon + GR; the authors think this is mainly because the residual read before the output and the final normalization were fused into a single gated read. For maximum residual activation, adding GR clearly lowers the maximum at every depth; judging from the figure, the full recipe's maximum lies between the other two runs.

The report also says that thanks to these stability improvements, the full-scale Flash-Next training run had not a single loss spike or anomalous gradient-norm fluctuation, and did not rely on explicit clipping methods such as qk-clip or SwiGLU-clip. (PDF pp.3, 22.) Those two are measures that specifically hold down attention logits or activations, which is not the same as ordinary global gradient-norm clipping; the latter is still configured, and in Figure 13 the Muon-only run crosses it. The production curves shown stop at the first 276B tokens, so the no-spike statement for the whole run is the authors' account.

## Once training is more stable, the learning rate and batch size need refitting

With both the architecture and the optimizer changed, the learning rate η and batch size B fitted with the previous generation's scaling law may no longer be optimal. When the team trained the new structure with the Qwen3.5 hyperparameter recipe, they found it trained noticeably more stably than before, which is the result of the previous section, and saw room for more aggressive settings, so they refit the hyperparameter scaling law. The new fit predicts substantially larger batch sizes and learning rates, with the learning rate decaying more slowly as models grow. The report says the larger batch improves parallel throughput at scale and the larger learning rate improves convergence. (§3.2, PDF pp.3, 16 to 17.)

A scaling law fitted at small scale is useful only if it extrapolates reliably. The report validates the two predictions separately, each in the setting most likely to expose an error: batch size on a small model trained for a long time, where the harm of an oversized batch shows up only after many tokens; learning rate on a large model trained briefly, where the main risk is instability.

The batch size test uses a 20-layer 10.8B-A0.89B MoE trained on 4T tokens. It compares the old recipe's B = 12.6M, the newly predicted 25.2M and a 1.5 times larger 37.7M, each paired with the learning rate the scaling law prescribes for it, with the same total tokens. Averaged over the final 20B tokens the losses are 1.5774, 1.5702 and 1.5707: the new prediction beats the old recipe by `7.2 × 10⁻³`, and another 1.5 times costs only `4.3 × 10⁻⁴`, which the authors consider not significant. Loss rises steeply below the predicted value and stays nearly flat above it. (Figure 8a, PDF p.17.)

Large runs often grow the batch size during early training, known as batch-size warmup. The rationale is that the critical batch size is small early on, making a large batch inefficient, and that small batches with a scaled learning rate are easier to stabilize in the first steps. The team's earlier hyperparameter sweep showed that with Muon, going below the predicted batch size costs more than going above it, and that Muon keeps its data efficiency at large batch sizes where AdamW starts to degrade; for a sparse MoE, a large batch also gives every expert enough diverse tokens at every step. So they retested batch-size warmup: start at 6.3M and add 6.3M at a time, reaching 25.2M at 524B tokens. One variant keeps the peak learning rate of the constant-batch optimum; the other lowers the peak learning rate to suit the small early batches. (PDF pp.17 to 18.)

Neither variant helped. Both fell within run-to-run variance but were slightly worse than the constant batch, by `2.5 × 10⁻⁴` and `3.5 × 10⁻⁴`. For the same token budget, warmup takes 18.8% more optimizer steps, which the report says means more wall-clock time, without giving a time figure. For Muon, every step runs NS over all matrices, a cost that does not depend on batch size and therefore grows with the step count; this is this article's inference from §3.1. None of these runs showed instability: no step exceeded its local median loss by more than 0.1, and the p99.9 of the pre-clip gradient norm ranged from 0.088 to 0.190, well below the threshold of 0.5.

The authors read the curves as follows: during warmup the small batch brings higher gradient noise and a higher loss; once the batch reaches its target, the warmup variant briefly leads thanks to the extra steps taken early; as the learning rate decays and the model approaches convergence, that step advantage is neutralized and the constant batch wins in the end. So production training does not use batch-size warmup. Only the batch-size warmup is dropped; learning-rate warmup remains, and both Figure 9b and Figure 13 shade the warmup interval.

The learning rate test uses a 48-layer 156B-A7B MoE trained on 419B tokens. The settings are the predicted optimum (B = 8.4M, η = `1.76 × 10⁻³`), the learning rate divided and multiplied by √2, a 25% larger batch with its matched learning rate, and the previous generation's recipe (B = 4.2M, η = `6.8 × 10⁻⁴`). The old recipe ends `7.8 × 10⁻³` above the predicted optimum in loss; the four settings near the optimum end within `7 × 10⁻⁴` of each other, close to the noise level, and the 25% larger batch is nominally the best, by `3 × 10⁻⁴`. (Figure 9, PDF p.18.) Downstream benchmarks:

| Setting | B | η | 7-benchmark average |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| New fit, predicted optimum | 8.4M | 1.76 × 10⁻³ | 60.55 |
| New fit, η ÷ √2 | 8.4M | 1.24 × 10⁻³ | 59.14 |
| New fit, η × √2 | 8.4M | 2.49 × 10⁻³ | 60.10 |
| New fit, B × 1.25 | 10.5M | 2.01 × 10⁻³ | 60.06 |
| Qwen3.5 recipe | 4.2M | 6.8 × 10⁻⁴ | 56.41 |

The predicted optimum has the highest average, the old recipe trails by about 4.1 points, and moving toward a larger learning rate or batch costs only a few tenths. The authors note themselves that each run was evaluated once and the margins among the top settings are narrow, so these rankings should be treated as observations, possibly all within evaluation noise. For stability, none of the five runs triggered gradient clipping after warmup; at the predicted optimum the maximum pre-clip gradient norm reached only 28% of the threshold, against 51% for the old recipe, whose smaller batch makes its gradients noisier; the run at √2 times the learning rate was just as stable. (PDF pp.18 to 19.)

The report's conclusion links these steps into a causal chain: GR provides rescaling that improves training stability, and the stability margin pushes the optimal learning rate and batch size upward, improving both throughput and convergence. (§5, PDF p.23.) What these experiments support is that the optimum of the new architecture combined with Muon really is larger, and that training at the predicted values is better in the validation settings. The refit covers the whole new recipe and does not measure how far GR or Muon alone moved the optimum; in discussing batch size, the report also credits part of the effect to Muon's data efficiency at large batches and to MoE experts needing enough token signal. The link in which the stability margin pushes up the optimal hyperparameters is the authors' synthesis of several experiments and has not been tested on its own.

## What the final comparison shows, and what is missing

Table 11 sets Flash-Next-Base beside two Base models: it scores higher than the dense Qwen3.8-27B-Base on all 14 benchmarks. Against Qwen3.7-Plus-Base it trails on six: MultiPL-E (79.09 against 81.68, 2.59 behind), MATH (72.78 against 74.38, 1.60 behind), MMLU-Redux (0.79 behind), INCLUDE (0.50 behind), GPQA (0.10 behind) and MMLU (0.07 behind); its largest leads are MGSM (3.91 ahead) and SuperGPQA (2.94 ahead). These are all Base-model scores in few-shot or zero-shot settings, with the shot count for each benchmark listed in §4 of the report. (PDF pp.22 to 23.)

These numbers cover only the pretraining stage. Section 1 of the report promises evaluation of both the Base model and the post-trained model, but the body contains only Base-model results. The report says the architectural improvements, together with far fewer activated parameters, significantly lower inference cost, yet apart from the kernel-level measurements in Figure 6 it gives no end-to-end throughput, latency or cost. The ablations in each section were also run at different scales and token budgets: GR at 25B-A3B, QSA at 35B-A3B, batch-size validation at 10.8B-A0.89B and learning-rate validation at 156B-A7B. These gains cannot be added together, and the final scores cannot be divided among the components.

The tightest bottleneck the authors name in their conclusion is evaluation throughput: a cheaper mid-scale probe that reliably predicted the ordering after post-training would make the design space far easier to search. (§5, PDF p.23.) The two-copy shortcut from the opening and NoPE in the full-attention layers both looked fine on pretraining loss and benchmarks and were exposed only after post-training. Validation at the pretraining stage alone lets problems like these slip through, and there is not yet a cheap way to predict them in advance.
