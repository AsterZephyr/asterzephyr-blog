<Callout type="note" title="Reading Notes">
This article is compiled from Bryan Magee's posthumous work *Ultimate Questions*, with some content interpreted through Dedao Brain. Magee was a British philosopher, BBC presenter, and former Member of Parliament who devoted his life to bringing philosophy out of academia and to the public. This book was written when he was in his eighties—his final systematic reflection on life's ultimate questions.
</Callout>

## Unknowable Truths

Magee's opening is powerful. He says:

> From before time began, or from when time began—one thing is certain: in a certain year, a leader named Margaret Thatcher would appear in a place on Earth now called Britain. No matter what point in history, no matter where, this statement is true.

But before Thatcher was born, no one had ever uttered this statement. Therefore, before the twentieth century, it was a **truth that could not possibly have been known**.

This impossibility has nothing to do with language. The ancient Romans knew of Britain, knew of Boudicca, and possessed sufficient linguistic tools to describe such a thing. The truth itself is simple and easy to express, yet it simply could not have been known—not because of insufficient intelligence, but because of the wrong position in time.

This example reveals a disturbing fact: **reality contains vast quantities of truths that are true right now, that have always been true, yet that we cannot possibly know.**

Magee immediately clarifies a common misunderstanding—some people say that if statements about the future are already true now, doesn't that imply the future is deterministic? He says no. These people are confusing linguistic reference with causal connection. If at some point in the future you make a free decision to do something, then "you will do it" is already true now. This requires no determinism to support it.

## The Metaphor of Congenital Blindness

In my view, the most brilliant metaphor in the entire book is this one:

> What is the visual world like for someone congenitally blind?

For a sighted person, the visual world is simply "there." But a congenitally blind person cannot understand this "there-ness." He possesses nearly all the language for describing the experiential world, yet he cannot describe the visual world—because he cannot understand it. We can tell him that something called "color" exists, but no words can help him conceptualize what real color is. He knows colors exist but cannot imagine them.

Magee uses this metaphor to illustrate humanity's predicament when facing the transcendent: **for those aspects of reality that we cannot experience, there simply cannot be corresponding concepts, and therefore there cannot be corresponding descriptive statements.**

Schopenhauer once said that if a being with cognitive capacities superior to humanity's were to tell us certain things about reality that lie outside the range of what humans can know through experience, we would not understand what they were talking about. Magee believes Schopenhauer was right "in certain respects"—we might be able to know that some momentous fact exists (know that it is so), but what the lived experience of it would be like remains forever beyond comprehension (know why it is so).

## The Gaps in the Puzzle

Magee used an irresistible analogy to describe the human cognitive predicament:

> The human condition is like holding some incomplete puzzle pieces, trying to assemble a complete picture. No matter what we ultimately piece together, it will not be the true picture.

But if we abandon the pursuit of completeness and **tolerate the gaps between puzzle pieces**, then every piece we hold can be in its correct position, in proper relation to the other pieces—this is possible.

The problem is that we have an "irresistible urge" to force the pieces together, producing an image that looks more glorious, more meaningful, more satisfying. But such an image inevitably contains things that go beyond the truth.

This passage reminds me of many analogies in modern contexts—from investment decisions to technical architecture, from career planning to worldview construction, we can never resist piecing incomplete information into a seemingly complete story. And "tolerating the gaps" may be the intellectual attitude that requires the most courage.

Magee summarized two reasons that preclude us from understanding the totality of reality: **our position in time**, and **the limitations of our instruments of understanding**. The former will be continuously corrected as subsequent generations appear, but the latter is the same for all members of humanity.

> How large a portion of the totality of reality is beyond our comprehension? This is a question we will never know the answer to. But we should assume it is nearly all of it.

## The Nature of Human Existence

In the second part, Magee turns to more immediate questions—what we are, and what our existence means.

He starts from the most basic fact: **we have no say in our own existence and no choice in the matter.** We simply wake up to find ourselves in this world. After birth, the survival instinct is written into our biological programming. We exist, we want to keep living, we want to live better. And so we are compelled to make arrangements for our lives—despite not knowing who we are and knowing extremely little about the universe.

Then he makes an important distinction: compared to our relationship with the universe, social forms are secondary. A person can choose to live in an entirely different society, and millions have done so. But one cannot choose to live in a different universe.

> The essence of life does not lie in the differences between social forms—this is something the greatest minds have always understood. This may also be why Marxism is somewhat shallow—it pays too much attention to social differences, and such differences are like a thin layer of makeup, capable of producing only relatively superficial effects on art or life.

### We Don't Know Our Own Bodies

Magee was in his eighties, and he said something remarkably candid:

> I have lived in my own body for over 80 years, and I have almost never understood myself as being the organs that constitute me. I possess my body and am inside it, rather like a driver sitting in the car he owns.

Sometimes, crossing the street, he would unexpectedly catch his reflection in a shop's large glass window—a stooped, white-haired, bespectacled man in his eighties walking toward him. Only a moment later would he realize it was himself. "That is nothing like what I think I look like."

He says that in fact very few people know what they look like. People are always surprised the first time they see themselves on video, and hearing their own voice on a recording for the first time is like hearing a stranger.

On the surface, this passage is about bodily perception, but underneath it points to a deeper problem: **between our self-knowledge and our actual existence, there is a gap that can never be fully bridged.**

### The Communication of Inner Selves

Magee argues that our understanding of other people far exceeds what language and observable behavior can explain.

He uses the example of orchestral conductors: many music lovers can hear the difference between recordings of the same piece by Toscanini and Beecham, yet no one can explain how those differences are produced. Conductors transmit vast amounts of information to dozens of musicians that we cannot account for. The musicians themselves admit they play differently for different conductors, but cannot explain exactly why.

And then there are eyes—according to the laws of physics, we see other people's eyes only because light reflects off the surface of the eyeball. Nothing is emitted outward from inside the eyes. Yet in the process of communication, eyes can convey things more subtly than language.

Even silence on the telephone is a form of information:

> If I am speaking on the phone and what I say causes a sudden unexpected silence on the other end, I can immediately sense that person's inner reaction. Silence is silence, no sound at all, yet information has still been transmitted.

From these observations, Magee draws a bold inference: **morality may be rooted in our sense of having direct contact with the inner selves of others—as if we share the same inner world.** This is why the golden rule "do not do unto others what you would not have done unto you" appears independently in almost all classical ethical systems and major religions.

## We Don't Know Why It Is Wrong

The passage that hit me hardest in the entire book:

> We all hold the firm conviction that torturing children for fun is wrong. But as different individuals, we give different reasons for why it is wrong. Some believe it violates God's commandments; some believe it is because of sympathy and compassion; still others believe it violates the requirements of humanity's collective survival and flourishing.
>
> Yet the disturbing truth is that we do not know why it is wrong. Here is something we all know, something we are utterly certain of, yet we do not know why.

Magee points out that certainty is no guarantee of truth. Perhaps our fundamental mistake lies in wanting to find an ultimate reason—**perhaps things are not right or wrong because of some reason.**

Socrates said that an inner voice would occasionally tell him not to do something, but never told him what to do. Magee believes that most of us have such a voice within us, drowned out by the noise of daily life.

## Sixty People's Worth of Civilization

The third part shifts to a cosmic perspective. This is also the passage I quoted in my [Ad Conference speech](/posts/我们在故事的开端-ad大会演讲稿):

> Line up long-lived people one after another, each one knowing the next—the entire span of civilization has occurred within the consecutive lifetimes of just 60 people. That is roughly the number of friends I would invite to a cocktail party.
>
> - 20 people take us back to the time of Jesus
> - 21 people take us back to Caesar
> - 10 people take us back to the Norman Conquest of 1066
> - 6 people take us back to the Renaissance

If we use a single human lifetime as the unit of measurement for history, the history of human civilization is unbelievably short, and the speed of historical change is unbelievably swift. Every great empire, from rise to fall, typically spans no more than 6 consecutive lifetimes.

From this, Magee draws a corollary: **we ourselves may still be near the very beginning of the whole story.** Tomorrow follows tomorrow, one millennium after another, and twenty thousand years, two hundred thousand years, two million years will eventually arrive. In that distant future, how will they regard us humans at the beginning of the story?

### The Prison of Time and Space

In the dimension of time, we are trapped in an extremely narrow window. Magee quotes Einstein:

> From a purely scientific point of view, there is no objective "now" in physics. The so-called "now" depends on the observer's position relative to the object being observed.

If "now" exists only relative to the observer, then the past and future also exist only relative to the observer. Events have an order within time—the sequence is objective, but the passage of time is not.

In the dimension of space, the range of human life's activity is limited to the surface of the Earth. Besides Earth, the only celestial body humans have ever set foot on is the Moon (380,000 kilometers away), while the visible universe extends to approximately 16 billion light-years.

> The journey across consecutive lifetimes confines us to an insignificant little corner of the vast cosmic river, like a drop in the ocean.

### A Brief Combinatorial Arrangement

Magee cites Heisenberg in pointing out that all matter is composed of the same fundamental particles. After we die, the atoms that constituted us will disperse, but the atoms themselves will not cease to exist. They were once part of certain solids, liquids, or gases; they briefly aggregated to form you and me; after dispersing, they will go on to form other things.

> Each of us is nothing more than a brief combinatorial arrangement.

Shakespeare grasped this point centuries ago. In *Hamlet*, the prince says: "Imperial Caesar, dead and turn'd to clay, might stop a hole to keep the wind away." And: "A king may go a progress through the guts of a beggar."

## Ignorance Is a Reason Not to Believe, Not a Reason to Believe

Throughout the book, Magee repeatedly returns to a core stance: **active agnosticism.**

He is not the lazy sort who says "I don't know, so never mind." His agnosticism is an active principle—an openness toward the unknown, accompanied by honest intellectual inquiry, and the mind's acceptance of whatever inquiry yields.

He simultaneously rejects two forms of evasion:

1. **The arrogance of materialism**—the belief that everything can in principle be understood by humans, that nothing exists beyond possible understanding. Magee considers this idea "not worth entertaining."

2. **The evasion of religion**—using our ignorance to justify groundless assertions. "The absence of a rational explanation for something in no way means we have no choice but to accept some other, different explanation already on offer."

> Denying the spiritual nature of the world, denying the mystery of the world, or embracing supernatural explanations—neither approach can help us escape our predicament.

He quotes Wittgenstein as a final summation:

> "If there is any value that does have value, it must lie outside the whole sphere of what happens and is the case. It must lie outside the world."

## My Reflections

While reading this book, what I kept thinking about was Magee's age when he wrote these words—over eighty. This is not a young person doing intellectual gymnastics at a desk. This is a man about to face death, using the philosophical training accumulated over an entire lifetime to honestly examine the most fundamental questions.

He offers no answers. He even says that on the most important question—"do we cease to exist after death?"—we may never know the answer. But his honesty is itself a kind of answer—**to go on living in a state of not-knowing, while never abandoning inquiry, is itself a form of human dignity.**

Schopenhauer's philosophy is "replete with acute insights," Magee says, and he wants to believe them; they bring him emotional and intellectual satisfaction. "But a fact I can never overcome is that I have absolutely no way of knowing whether he is actually right. This eternal unknowability tortures me."

This torment is real. But there is no more inappropriate response than "taking a leap to end the torment"—however tempting it may be.

The posture Magee ultimately chose is this: tolerate the gaps in the puzzle, refuse to comfort yourself with false completeness, and continue placing every piece you hold in its correct position.

This is probably the best thing a person can do when facing ultimate questions.
