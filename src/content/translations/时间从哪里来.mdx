Life is full of goals. Learning, work, and everyday living give us countless tasks to complete, and every task takes time. Yet time is always limited. The more complex a task, the more it depends on the outside world; more dependencies mean more risk and a greater chance of failure. Given these facts, the first principle of task management is: **complete what is necessary quickly.**

**The Central Tensions**

We get caught up in the details of a task. Perfectionism at the local level makes us forget how much that particular objective contributes to the larger goal. We do many unnecessary things and waste time. Once a project's completion date slips, the more complex the project is, the more likely unexpected events become. Tail risk rises sharply and can ultimately cause the project to fail. Every task-management method is, at its core, trying to resolve these two tensions: **the part versus the whole, and risk versus time.**

**An Approach**

Borrow the principle of time-sharing from operating systems. Since these dimensions conflict, handle them in different task contexts. As an OS does, separate the kernel from the applications. The kernel consists of system tasks that maintain the greatest overall benefit from task management; the applications are the concrete work you actually carry out.

- System task—planning: five years is a useful horizon. Set your goals and key actions for the next five years, then break down the paths needed to carry out those actions.
- System task—OKRs: set personal OKRs on a monthly cycle, adjusting the interval if needed. The objectives come from the paths laid out in your five-year plan, and the key results are the key actions for achieving each objective. Review each month and set the next month's OKRs.
- System task—weekly review: spend a little time each week adjusting how you execute the tasks in your monthly OKRs. Stay flexible and draw up the following week's to-do list.
- System task—daily plan: each morning, create that day's to-do list based on how the weekly plan is progressing.

This is a fairly ordinary way to organize tasks into layers. For it to work, however, several principles are essential:

1. Keep improving your judgment: **planning means choosing the right things to do**. That requires judgment, and the fundamental way to improve judgment is **learning**.
2. Think slowly, act quickly: allow enough time to design an effective approach and consider different possibilities. The more complex a project is, the more time you need to learn and develop a considered plan. Once action begins, however, move quickly to reduce the chance of unexpected events.
3. Do only what is necessary: **What counts as necessary? Anything that can be postponed, handed to someone else, or abandoned is not necessary; what remains is.** Apply this principle at every level of execution. This is the principle of lean work.
4. Step back from the local task: anyone can fall into the trap of local perfectionism. The daily plan's most important contribution is to give each task a default plan—a time window for completing it. This is key to keeping the overall weekly plan on track. When a task exceeds its allotted time and encroaches on the time reserved for other tasks, you must decide: **keep going or stop?** Stopping is usually difficult and calls for thought, but at this point a quick judgment is what matters most. Use an approximate calculation: how much longer will this take? When is the deadline? Can it be deferred until sometime before that deadline? If it cannot be deferred, is that really true, or could some requirements be cut? After thinking this through quickly, if the task truly cannot wait, you must abandon the default plan. Otherwise, make a quick capture note recording the current state and the next action. Get it onto the page and out of your head, return your attention to the current default plan, and take the first step.
5. Clear the mental clutter: we all get distracted; stray thoughts periodically interrupt our concentration. The best response is to write the thought down quickly, reassuring your brain that it has been captured and that you will revisit it at the next weekly review. Right now, something more important needs your attention. Most ideas are passing impulses. When you review them on Sunday, you will find that many have no value, but one or two bright spots are worth revisiting. Over time, these accumulate into the compounding returns of knowledge.
6. Use a behavior model: sustaining repetitive activities is difficult. Keeping up the system tasks above can be extremely hard; sometimes we cannot even get ourselves out of bed on time. Psychology already offers a classic worth studying on this subject: *The Fogg Behavior Model*. With this approach, we can accept short-term failures without guilt while keeping our attention on long-term success.
